# A solver cannot check reality for you

A solver does not verify your constraints. It solves the ones you hand it. Anything you leave out, it treats as unlimited.

**The example from the previous article already had a wall in it.** Three channels, 1,000,000 of budget. Equalizing the marginals gives search 300,000, social 400,000, content 300,000, total return 2.52. But the social channel is locked by contract at no more than 300,000. The solver never saw that wall, so it returned 400,000 — 100,000 too much, and total return fell to 2.45.

That is one constraint. There are at least four more.

## Five limits to write down before solving

**Committed volume.** A channel you have signed a year-long commitment with, or a block of ad inventory bought out for a period. These numbers live in contracts, not in reports, and the solver has to have them.

**Minimum spend you owe.** Brand campaigns that have to keep showing, new products that have to cover every channel, platform algorithms that expect a certain daily burn. Minimums never show up in "what did we spend last month." They live in operating commitments.

**What you can actually sell.** Inventory covers a certain number of weeks. Production capacity covers a certain number of orders. **This is the one most often forgotten, because it belongs to the warehouse rather than to marketing** — yet it sets how large the budget can be at all.

**People.** How many times a day the buying team adjusts bids, how many accounts one person can watch, how long a new batch of creative takes to ship. Leave these out and the optimum grows a shape nobody can execute.

**Regulation and platform rules.** Some ad categories are restricted. Consent rules change what you can measure. Some channels refuse whole verticals.

**A real constraint looks like this.** The chart above is marketing advertising spend as a share of revenue for NIKE and Deckers (SEC XBRL, fetched 2026-11).

NIKE's revenue fell from 51.2 billion to 46.4 billion over three years, down 9.4%, while marketing advertising spend rose from 4.06 billion to 4.75 billion, up 17%. **Spend intensity was pushed from 7.93% to 10.25% without anyone deciding to push it** — revenue fell, the budget did not, so the ratio climbed.

Deckers is the other case: revenue rose 50.7% over three years and the marketing ratio moved from 7.48% to 9.06%. **That is a deliberate increase, not a passive one.**

Same line, two different causes. In one case a constraint is binding; in the other, a decision is in charge. Confuse the two and you read "passive rise" as "weak growth," then add budget to the one place it does not belong.

## What a missing constraint produces

**First: the solver hands you a solution you cannot execute.** It does not know you have two people on the buying team, so it parks 40% of the budget on a channel that needs daily monitoring.

**Second, and more expensive: you fix it by hand, and your fix is not the optimal one.** Social is 100,000 over, so you move 100,000 into content. **You corrected the one constraint that was visibly violated** and left the rest in place, which gives you an executable but non-optimal solution.

**Third, and the most dangerous: the solver hands you a reasonable-looking solution that violates a constraint you did not know existed.** You execute it, and three months later find out the channel never had the capacity to absorb it.

**How constraints reshape the solution.** The chart above picks up the three marginal curves from the previous article and draws the constraints as vertical lines: social capped at 300,000 by contract, content capped at 350,000 by inventory.

**With no walls**, the three curves cross at a common marginal of about 2.0. **With walls**, the crossing gets cut off and the optimum slides toward the corner.

**A wall does not shrink the plan. It moves it.** Social is capped at 300,000, search stays at 300,000, and the extra 100,000 goes to content, which moves from 300,000 to 400,000 and whose marginal falls from 2.0 to 1.3. **That is where the 0.07 of total return goes.**

**Each layer of constraint costs a slice of the return on the same curve.** 2.52, then 2.45, then 2.32.

**Yes, the return goes down. That is the point.** The 2.52 was never real — it rested on assumptions you cannot satisfy. **A real 2.32 is worth more than a fake 2.52**, because you can actually run it.

## The optimal split that nobody can execute

Close out the example from the previous article. Three channels, 1,000,000, all constraints in place at once:

- Social capped at 300,000 by contract
- Content limited to 350,000 by inventory
- Search limited to 350,000 by the platform's daily budget cap

**Without constraints:** search 300,000, social 400,000, content 300,000, total return 2.52.

**With all three walls up:** social 300,000 (4.3), content 350,000 (8.7), search 350,000 (10.2), total return **2.32**.

**The constrained solution looks nothing like the equalized one.** The equalized split is roughly a third each (30/40/30). The constrained split is 35/30/35 — **search and content each take 350,000 and social is pushed down to 300,000.** The steepest curve, search, takes the most money, because it peaks earliest and has to claim its volume first.

**This is why constraints get written down first.** A constraint is not something you check after solving. **It changes the shape of the answer.** Discovering that social cannot exceed 300,000 after the solve, and patching it by hand, throws away the whole round of optimization. Know it before you solve, and the optimum is not that shape to begin with.

## List the constraints, then solve

The order is fixed: **list constraints, solve, verify feasibility.**

**Listing constraints is where teams cut corners.** The usual failure is that what you wrote down is not reality but the shape you wish the answer had. Every constraint has to answer three questions: **who set it, where is it written down, and when does it stop applying?**

- Committed volume — in the contract, void at renewal
- Inventory — in the warehouse system, changes daily
- People — on the staffing plan, changes when someone leaves
- Regulation — in legal advice or platform policy, changes whenever either is rewritten

**A constraint you cannot point to on paper is a constraint you imagined.** Invented constraints produce a solution that looks rigorous and that nobody can execute.

**One more thing: constraints move.** Inventory is tight this month and loose next. A contract expires this quarter and gets renegotiated. The team changes and the accounts-per-person ratio shifts. **So the constraint table needs a date, and the solve needs repeating.** Solving today's budget against constraints from three months ago solves a problem that does not exist.

**Do not skip the last step.** Take the solver's answer back to the list of real constraints and check it line by line: if it matches, execute it; if not, solve again. **This pass is mechanical, and it is the only thing in the chain that stops a pretty number from being wrong.**
