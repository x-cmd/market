# A budget split is only optimal when every channel's last dollar earns the same

A 1,000,000 budget across three channels. The tempting move is to give all of it to the channel with the highest return. That wastes money.

**Equalize the marginal return instead: let the last dollar in each channel earn the same.** Real markets run on this every day. Few compute it this way.

Meta's reported numbers for 2021 through 2025: growth in ad impressions, and in average price per ad. **In 2022 and 2023 Meta pushed impressions up 18% and 28%, and the average price per ad fell 16% and 9%.** In 2024 and 2025 the market recovered: impressions grew 11% and 12%, price rose 10% and 9%.

The same dollar bought wildly different exposure depending on when it was spent. **This is what uneven marginal returns look like.**

## The next dollar goes wherever it earns the most

**Put the money where one more dollar earns the most.** The keyword is **marginal**: what one more dollar returns, not what an average dollar returns. The average folds in money already spent, so it describes the past, not the next dollar.

Each channel has its own curve. Search gets more expensive the deeper you push it; social gets more efficient the more you spend. In the worked example below, the first level of search returns 4.8 and the fifth level of social 2.3. **These curves are the input to the allocation.**

## Stop when every channel's last dollar earns the same

**The equal marginal returns principle: with a fixed total, total return peaks when every channel's marginal return is the same.** Say search has a marginal of 2.0 and social has 1.8. Move one dollar from social to search: search gains 2.0, social loses 1.8, and the net is +0.2. **The budget did not change, and total return went up.** So the original split was not optimal. Keep shifting until the marginals match. **That shared number is the shadow price**: how much total return rises when the budget grows by one unit, and **the ceiling on what you would pay for it.**

**Same 1,000,000 budget. All into search returns 1.17; equalizing on marginals returns 2.52. More than double.**

Now sort by average ROI instead: search 2.34, content 2.14, social 1.72. The money goes to search and content, and social gets nothing. **But social's fifth level still returns 2.3, above search's fourth level at 1.1.**

## Four conditions, and breaking any one of them breaks the rule

**Four assumptions hold this up. Break one and it fails.**

**One: the marginal cannot be estimated.** Marginal return is the slope of a curve at a point, not a line between two points. You get it by splitting spend into levels and measuring what each level returned. Most teams hold one number — total spend, total sales — and dividing gives average ROI. **Without levels there is no marginal, and equalizing is equalizing nothing.**

**Two: the total is not fixed.** If one more dollar still returns 2.0, the question is not how to split but how much to spend. The test shifts from "the channels are equal to each other" to "the marginal equals zero." **The principle governs the split, not the size of the budget.**

**Three: there is a hard limit.** A contract locks the budget, inventory only supports so much, the media team has no more slots, a channel carries brand requirements. Each puts a wall in front of the optimum, and **the optimum then lands on the wall rather than at the equalized point.** Cap social at 300,000 and the optimum becomes search 300,000, social 300,000, content 400,000, total return falling from 2.52 to 2.45. **Constraints come first; optimization after.**

**Four: the channels interfere.** More money on search ads can take clicks away from organic search; more on TV can take search volume rather than add to it. **With cross-effects the per-channel marginals are no longer independent, and the equalized allocation has to be discounted before you trust it.**

## The equalized point tells you how much your estimates can be wrong

**The equalized point is a set of numbers, not one: how much each channel gets, and each one's marginal.** Here: search 300,000, social 400,000, content 300,000, all at 2.0.

**First, it is unique, which makes it checkable.** Three monotonic curves cross in one place, so two optima cannot coexist. Re-estimate the marginals, get a different allocation, and the earlier estimates were wrong. **Second, it is sensitive to estimation error, but forgivingly so.** The curves tend to be flat near the crossing, so **a rough estimate shifts the split while costing little total return**. Where a curve has a cliff, the same error costs a lot. **Third, that flatness is good news.** You do not need precision down to the last level. This runs opposite to a significance test, where the worry is treating noise as signal. Here the worry is leaving money on the table, so cut into more levels and look around.

## Equalizing stops waste, but the level still has to clear 1

**Equalizing guarantees you do not waste. It does not guarantee you earn.** The equalized marginal in the worked example is 2.0, so every unit nets one back. **But at 1.2 every unit nets 0.2 — the allocation is perfect and the books are underwater.**

**Both allocations are equalized, and the one on the right earns five times more.** Equal marginal returns tells you where not to put money, not whether the price is worth paying. **So the test has two halves. First, the marginal returns across channels are equal** — the condition for not wasting. **Second, that shared value is above 1** — the condition for earning. A marginal of 1 is break-even. Below 1, **every extra unit of spend sends money out of the door.** With an open total, **the test shifts from "equal to each other" to "equal to zero."**

**One common misconception: equalizing does not mean spending the same in every channel.** Search gets 300,000 and social 400,000 here, a third apart. **The same marginal value costs very different amounts in different channels**, because slopes differ.

**Move money toward the highest marginal. Stop when every channel's last dollar earns the same. Then check that the number is above 1.** Without estimable marginals there is nothing to optimize. Without written-down constraints the result is unusable. Below 1, the plan loses money however clean it looks.
