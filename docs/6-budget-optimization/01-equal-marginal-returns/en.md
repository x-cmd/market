# The best split is the one where every channel's last dollar earns the same

A 1,000,000 budget across three channels. The tempting answer is to give all of it to the channel with the highest return. That answer wastes money.

**Equalize the marginal return instead: let the last dollar in each channel earn the same amount.** This happens in real markets every day. Nobody computes it that way.

Meta's reported numbers for 2021 through 2025: ad impression growth, and the growth of average price per ad. **In 2022 and 2023 Meta pushed impressions up 18% and 28%, and the average price per ad fell 16% and 9%.** In 2024 and 2025 the market recovered: impressions grew 11% and 12%, and average price rose 10% and 9%.

The same dollar bought wildly different volumes of exposure depending on when you spent it. **This is what uneven marginal returns look like in the real world.** Where the money should move next is hidden in the gap between those two lines.

## The next dollar goes wherever it buys the most

**Put the money where one more dollar returns the most.**

The keyword is **marginal**: what one more dollar buys back, not what an average dollar buys back. The average folds in the return on money already spent, so it describes the past and stays silent about the next dollar.

Each channel has its own curve. Search gets more expensive the deeper you push. Social gets more efficient the more you spend. Content sits in between. In the worked example below, the first level of search returns 4.8 and the fifth level of social returns 2.3.

**These three curves are the input to the allocation, not decoration.** Where they intersect is where the money stops moving.

## Stop when every channel's last dollar earns the same

**The equal marginal returns principle says: when the total is fixed, the allocation that maximizes total return is the one where every channel's marginal return is the same.**

You can see it without any formulas. Say search has a marginal of 2.0 and social has 1.8. Move one dollar from social to search: search gains 2.0, social loses 1.8, and the net is +0.2. **The budget did not change, and total return went up.** So the original split was not optimal.

Keep moving until the two marginals are equal, at which point one more dollar raises one side by exactly what it lowers on the other, and the net effect is zero.

**That shared number has a name: the shadow price.** It is how much total return rises when the budget grows by one unit, and **it is also the ceiling on what you would pay for that extra unit.**

Now sort by average ROI and see what breaks. Search at full spend averages 2.34, content 2.14, social 1.72. On that ranking the money goes almost entirely to search and content, and social gets nothing. **But social's marginal is still climbing: its fifth level returns 2.3, higher than search's fourth level at 1.1.** The order that average ROI produces is close to reversed in the marginal world.

## Four conditions, and any one of them breaks the rule

**The principle rests on four assumptions. Break one and the whole thing fails.**

**One: the marginal cannot be estimated.** A marginal return is the slope of a curve at one point, not the line between two points. You get it by splitting spend into levels and measuring what each level returned. Most teams hold a single number — total spend this month and total sales it produced — and dividing gives average ROI, not marginal. **Without levels you cannot get a marginal, and equalizing is equalizing nothing.**

**Two: the total is not fixed.** If one more dollar still returns 2.0, the question is not how much to spend in total. The test changes with it, from "all channels are equal" to "the marginal equals zero." **Equal marginal returns governs the split, not the size of the budget.**

**Three: there is a hard limit.** A contract locks the budget, inventory only supports so much, the media team has no more slots, a channel has brand requirements. Each puts a wall in front of the optimum. **With a wall in the way, the optimum sits on the wall, not at the equalized point.** Cap social at 300,000 in the worked example and the optimum becomes search 300,000, social 300,000, content 400,000, with total return falling from 2.52 to 2.45. **Constraints enter first. Optimization comes after.**

**Four: the channels interfere.** More money on search ads can take clicks away from organic search. More money on TV can take search volume rather than add to it. **With cross-effects, the per-channel marginals are no longer independent, and the allocation table needs a discount before you trust it.**

## What the equalized point tells you about your estimates

**Equalizing produces a set, not a single number: how much each channel gets, and what each one's marginal is.** Here: search 300,000, social 400,000, content 300,000, all at 2.0.

**It is unique (as long as the curves are monotonic).** Three monotonic curves cross in one place, so two optima cannot coexist. That makes equalizing checkable: re-estimate the marginals, and if the allocation comes out different, the earlier estimates were wrong.

**It is sensitive to estimation error.** The region near the crossing is often flat — shift a level and total return barely moves. So **a rough marginal estimate shifts the optimal split but costs little total return.** The reverse is worse: if the curves are steep near the crossing (one channel has a cliff), the same error moves total return far.

**A flat peak is good news.** You do not need precision down to the last level. This runs opposite to a significance test, where you fear mistaking noise for signal. Here you fear missing a slice that earns money, so cut more levels and look.

## Equalizing is a test, not an answer, and the absolute value still matters

**The step most often missed: equalizing guarantees you do not waste, not that you earn.**

In the worked example, the equalized marginal is 2.0, so every unit spent nets one unit back. **But if the equalized point sits at 1.2, every unit nets 0.2. The allocation is perfect and the books are still underwater.**

**Both allocations are equalized, and the one on the right earns five times more.** Equal marginal returns tells you where not to put money, not whether the price is worth paying.

**So the full test has two halves, and both are required. First, the marginal returns across channels are equal** — the condition for not wasting. **Second, that shared value is above 1** — the condition for earning. A marginal of 1 means you break even. A marginal below 1 means **every additional unit of spend sends money out of the door.**

**This is also why "the total is not fixed" is a real problem.** With a fixed total, 1,000,000 is 1,000,000 however you split it, and equalizing at least stops things from getting worse. With an open total, a marginal below 1 means spending should stop, and **the test shifts from "equal to each other" to "equal to zero."**

**One common misconception to clear up: equalizing does not mean spending the same amount in every channel.** In the worked example, search gets 300,000 and social 400,000, a difference of a third. **The same marginal value costs very different amounts in different channels**, because the slopes differ. Splitting the budget evenly is often the opposite of equalizing.

**Move money toward the highest marginal, stop when every channel's last dollar earns the same, then check that the number is above 1.** Equal marginal returns is a test, not an allocation table to copy. If the marginals cannot be estimated, do not optimize yet. If the constraints are not written down, the result is not usable. If the equalized value sits below 1, a beautiful allocation still loses money.
