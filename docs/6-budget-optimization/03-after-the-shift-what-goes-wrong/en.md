# A flat report after a budget shift means you read it too early

**Move search from 60,000 a day to 20,000, social from 20,000 to 60,000, then check the dashboard next morning. Nothing moved.** That is normal, not proof it failed.

The dashboard is not the problem. **The saturation point** rose the moment you moved the money, because competitors were bidding for the same inventory. The new effect lands over days, and a one-week window reads it early.

**Take one thing away: before judging a shift, check whether the saturation point moved and whether you gave it enough time.**

## Watch the ad ratio, not total revenue

Wayfair is a US furniture retailer. In 2016 it spent 409 million dollars on advertising and booked 3.38 billion in revenue; in 2025 it spent 1.43 billion and booked 12.46 billion. **Over ten years ad spend grew 3.5 times and revenue 3.7 times, but the two lines did not move together.**

From 2016 to 2020 ad spend rose from 409 million to 1.41 billion dollars while revenue rose from 3.38 billion to 14.15 billion, and **the ad ratio — ad spend as a share of revenue — fell from 12.1% to 10.0%.**

2021 through 2024 looks nothing like that. **Ad spend sat between 1.38 billion and 1.47 billion without moving while revenue fell from 13.71 billion to 11.85 billion.** The ratio climbed from 10.05% to 12.42%: **not one dollar less was spent, and revenue was down 13.5%.**

**Separate two things.** The revenue fall cannot be read as "advertising stopped working" — the US furniture industry was contracting from 2021 to 2024, and much of it is market. **But the ad ratio divides the market out.** Efficient spending pushes the ratio down; it rose, so each dollar bought back less. **Watch the ratio, not revenue.**

## The saturation point moved without telling you

**The saturation point is the spend past which one more dollar buys almost nothing.** The Hill curve is the usual shape: effect(x) = x^α / (x^α + k^α), where **k is the saturation point**. **The problem is that k is not a constant.** Competitors bid on the same auction inventory you do, so more budget pushes the clearing price up and you buy less. **You raised k yourself, and that raise shows up in the auction logs, not the report.** The other source is **internal cannibalisation**: brand and non-brand keyword search compete for the same people, so net gain falls short.

**Pushing the same shape to the ceiling costs several times more:** Hill curve algebra says **reaching 90% of the ceiling takes 9^(1/α) times the saturation point.** All three curves saturate at 1.0: α=1 takes 9 times, α=2 takes 3 times, α=3 takes 2.1 times. A channel ramped up gradually is steeper (large α); one pushed hard at once flatter (small α). **And α is the least reliable number in the model**, so when k drifts α drifts too. **That is why "the saturation point moved" cannot be found by re-estimating k** — the new k is no better than the old one.

## A short window punishes both sides of the shift

**Ad effects do not settle the same day they are bought.** Television is the extreme case: nobody orders the same night, and the effect spreads for weeks. **Marketing mix models handle this with adstock** — the "stock" of advertising: some of what you spend today carries into tomorrow, sometimes three weeks later. The common model is exponential decay, **keeping a fixed share λ of the effect each day**, and **λ differs enormously by channel: search 0.1 to 0.3, social around 0.7, television 0.7 to 0.9.**

**With search at λ=0.1, a tenth of yesterday's effect survives to today and is gone by day three; at λ=0.3, 3% is left on day three. Social and television at λ=0.7 hold 3% a week later; television at λ=0.9 holds 6% on day 22.** In time: search is spent in two days, social takes seven or eight, television over three weeks. Move 200,000 from search into television and, on the day-14 report, **the 200,000 of television has only delivered 86%** — part of what reads as "television brought no matching lift" is still on the road. **That subtraction has to be made**: give television three weeks, or add back the unreleased part.

**There is a trap: the effect of the channel you cut is still decaying.** Halve search spend and the day-3 report looks better at once: search effects finish same-day, **while the social or television you funded does not pile up until next week.** You see bad news on both sides and call it a mistake, when only the date failed. **The slowest-decaying channel sets the window.**

## The total did not change, so only the structure can

**A shift is zero-sum:** search down from 60,000 to 20,000 a day, social up from 20,000 to 60,000, daily spend still 80,000. **So the only route to a better total return is a better structure.** No improvement means the structure did not improve, or the gain was cancelled. The trap is **treating average ROI as the allocation's score**.

**Worked example: 500,000 of search spend split into five bands of 100,000, returning 480, 310, 200, 110 and 40 thousand in sales.** Average ROI is cumulative: earlier bands underwrite later ones, so **the last band still shows an average ROI of 2.28 and the channel looks healthy.** But **the marginal ROI of that band is 0.4** — those 100,000 bought 40,000 back, so one more dollar there returns barely more than it costs. **Watch the marginal line, not the average: once the two separate, the channel already carries a lot of money** — a raised saturation point.

## Two checks to run right after a shift

**First, has the saturation point been pushed up? Do not test k itself; test whether volume still rises when you add money.** Add a small increment on top of the new position (5% to 10% of the total) and check whether the volume bought rises as before. If it does, the shift was sound; if it barely moves, that channel is full.

**Second, did you give it enough time. Set the observation window by the decay tail of the channel you funded into, not by when you want a result.** Two days for search, a week for social, three weeks for television. Either wait for the tail to finish or add back the unreleased part.

**Demand moves on the calendar too.** Sales at US furniture and home furnishings stores have a strong seasonal shape: **107.1 in January 2023 and 92.4 in October, a 16% spread**. A shift across a seasonal turn compares two seasons.

**The mistake that follows a shift most often is testing a structural claim with a report that has not settled yet.** The two errors arrive together: the structure did not improve, and the report is too early. The shift looks like a mistake, you put the budget back, **and you ran the original allocation through the machine twice for nothing.**

**Order of work: list the constraints, solve the allocation, then run these checks.** See [→ How to split the money without wasting it](../01-equal-marginal-returns/en.md) and [→ List the constraints first](../02-state-constraints-first/en.md).
