# A flat report after a budget shift usually means you read it too early

**Move 40,000 a day out of search and into social, then check the dashboard the next morning. Nothing moved.** That is the normal outcome, not a sign the shift was wrong.

The reason is not the dashboard. The saturation point — where the next yuan of spend buys almost nothing new — got pushed up the moment you shifted the money, because competitors were bidding against you for the same inventory. The new effect also arrives over days, and a one-week window reads it before it lands. And a plainer point gets forgotten: **a shift does not change the total budget, only how it is spread.**

**Take one thing away: before you judge a shift, check whether the saturation point moved and whether you gave it enough time.**

## A flat report after a shift means the ratio moved, not the revenue

Wayfair is a US furniture retailer. In 2016 it spent 409 million dollars on advertising and booked 3.38 billion in revenue; in 2025 it spent 1.43 billion and booked 12.46 billion. **Over ten years ad spend grew 3.5 times and revenue grew 3.7 times, but the two lines did not travel together.** Between 2016 and 2020 ad spend rose from 409 million to 1.41 billion dollars while revenue rose from 3.38 billion to 14.15 billion, and **the ad ratio — ad spend as a share of revenue — fell from 12.1% to 10.0%.** More money in, more revenue out, and each yuan pulled back more revenue than the year before.

2021 through 2024 looks nothing like that. **Ad spend sat between 1.38 billion and 1.47 billion without moving, while revenue fell from 13.71 billion to 11.85 billion.** The ratio climbed from 10.05% back to 12.42%: **not one yuan less was spent, and revenue was down 13.5%.**

**Separate two things.** The revenue fall cannot be read as "advertising stopped working" — between 2021 and 2024 the US furniture industry was contracting, and much of that decline belongs to the market, not the media plan. **But the ad ratio divides the market out.** When money is spent efficiently the ratio falls; it rose, which says each yuan bought back less revenue than it had before. **So watch the ratio after a shift, not total revenue.**

## The saturation point moved without telling you

**The saturation point is the spend on a channel past which one more yuan buys almost nothing new.** The Hill curve is the usual shape: effect(x) = x^α / (x^α + k^α), and **k is the saturation point**. **The problem is that k is not a constant.** Competitors bid on the same auction inventory you do, so when you add budget the clearing price rises and you buy less than the old price implied. **You raised k yourself, and that raise shows up in the auction logs, not the reporting table.** The other source is **internal cannibalisation**: brand and non-brand keyword search compete for the same people, so the net gain is smaller than the two channel lines suggest.

**Pushing the same shape to the ceiling can cost several times more.** The Hill curve has an algebraic property: **reaching 90% of the ceiling takes 9^(1/α) times the saturation point.**

**All three curves saturate at 1.0.** With α=1 it takes 9 times the saturation point to reach 90% of the ceiling; with α=2, 3 times; with α=3, 2.1 times is enough. **How far the ceiling sits depends on how the channel's spend got there, not only on where it sits today.** A channel ramped up gradually is steeper (large α); one pushed hard at once is flatter (small α). **And α is the least reliable number in the model**, estimated alongside k, so when k drifts α drifts with it. **That is why "the saturation point moved" cannot be discovered by re-estimating k** — the new k is no more trustworthy than the old one.

## The window was too short, and the report punishes both sides

**Ad effects do not settle the same day they are bought.** Search ends with the click; brand awareness carries into later days; television is the extreme case, where nobody orders the same night they saw the ad and the effect spreads over weeks. **Marketing mix models handle this with adstock**, the "stock" of advertising: some of what you spend today carries into tomorrow, the day after, and sometimes three weeks later. The common model is exponential decay, **keeping a fixed share λ of the effect each day**, and **λ differs enormously by channel: the standard priors are search 0.1 to 0.3, social around 0.7, television 0.7 to 0.9.**

**With search at λ=0.1, a tenth of yesterday's effect survives to today and it is gone by the third day; at λ=0.3, 3% is left on day three. Social and television at λ=0.7 still hold 3% a week later; television at λ=0.9 still holds 6% on day 22.**

**Turned into time: search is spent within two days, social takes seven or eight, television at λ=0.9 takes more than three weeks.** A two-week window is enough for search and short for television. Move 200,000 from search into television and, on the day-14 report, **the 200,000 of television has only delivered 86%** — part of what you read as "television brought no matching lift" is still on the road. **That is a subtraction that has to be made**: either give television three weeks or add the unreleased part back.

**There is a common trap here: the effect of the channel you took money from is still decaying.** Halve search spend and the day-3 report looks better right away, because search effects finish the same day, **while the social or television you funded does not pile up until next week. So the channel you cut has already lost its effect and the one you funded has not produced its own yet, you see bad news on both sides,** and you conclude the shift was wrong when the only thing that failed was the date you looked. **The slowest-decaying channel you funded into sets the window.**

**Demand moves on the calendar too.** Sales at US furniture and home furnishings stores have a strong seasonal shape: **107.1 in January 2023 and 92.4 in October, a 16% spread.** A shift straddling a seasonal turn compares two different seasons.

## The total did not change, so only the structure can

**A shift is zero-sum:** search down from 60,000 to 20,000 a day, social up from 20,000 to 60,000, daily spend still 80,000. **So the only route to a better total return is a better structure.** No improvement means either the structure did not really improve, or the improvement was cancelled out. The trap is **treating average ROI as the score of the new allocation**.

**Worked example: 500,000 of search spend split into five bands of 100,000, returning 480, 310, 200, 110 and 40 thousand in sales.** Average ROI is cumulative: the money earned in the earlier bands underwrites the later ones, so **the last band still shows an average ROI of 2.28 and the channel looks healthy.** But **the marginal ROI of that band is 0.4** — those 100,000 bought 40,000 back, so **one more yuan spent there returns barely more than it costs.**

**What you watch when you move money is the marginal ROI line.** Read the average and you see a channel "still returning more than 2x" and you pile money into it. **The moment the two lines separate, the channel already carries a large amount of money** — the fingerprint of a raised saturation point.

## The two checks that come right after a shift

**First, has the saturation point been pushed up? Do not test k itself; test whether volume still rises when you add money.** Add a small increment on top of the new position (5% to 10% of the total) and see whether the volume bought rises at the old rate. If it rises about as before, the shift was sound; if it barely moves, that channel is full. A clearing price above what you paid before says the same thing. **Second, did you give it enough time. Set the observation window by the decay tail of the channel you funded into, not by the day you most want to see a result.** Two days for search, a week for social, three weeks for television. Either wait for the tail to finish, or add the unreleased part back.

**The mistake that follows a shift most often is testing a structural claim with a report that has not settled yet.** The two errors arrive together: the structure did not really improve, and the report is too early. The shift then looks like a mistake, you put the budget back, **and you have run the original allocation through the machine twice for nothing.**

**Order of work: list the constraints, solve for the allocation, then run these two checks on what you did.** See [→ How to split the money without wasting it](../01-equal-marginal-returns/en.md) and [→ List the constraints first](../02-state-constraints-first/en.md).
