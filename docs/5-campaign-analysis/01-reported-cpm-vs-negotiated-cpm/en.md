# Two CPM numbers, one budget, and no one is cheating

**The report says 45. You negotiated 60. Same channel, same month, same budget.**

**The 15 yuan between them is not markup. The two numbers divide by different things.**

The report counts the impressions the channel bills for — an ad that was served and began rendering. The number you negotiated counts the impressions it promises you can see. A smaller denominator makes the price per thousand look higher.

**Before you negotiate, ask two questions: what is the denominator behind this CPM, and does the discount come off the unit price or come out of a rebate?**

## The 15 yuan gap is a denominator gap, not a price gap

A mid-sized performance channel, September 2025, 1,000,000 yuan of spend. The platform's delivery report shows CPM 45. Sales negotiates 60 on the same contract.

The report bills on *delivered impressions* — the ad was handed to the delivery system and began rendering. The negotiation bills on *billable viewable impressions* — at least half its pixels inside a focused browser tab, held for 1 second.

| Caliber | Denominator (impressions) | CPM | Rule |
|---|---|---|---|
| Delivered impressions | 22,222,222 | 45 yuan | Counts as soon as it renders |
| Billable viewable impressions | 16,666,667 | 60 yuan | Half the pixels plus 1 second |

**16,666,667 ÷ 22,222,222 = 75%.** Only three quarters of this channel's impressions met its own stated visibility standard, and **the ratio between the two CPMs is that viewability rate. Nobody owes you the missing 25%.** It is not a discount, not a rebate, not withheld delivery. The same impressions land on opposite sides of two counting rules.

## One metric, three ways to count an impression

**CPM itself is unambiguous: what you paid per thousand impressions.** Magnite's fiscal 2025 10-K defines it exactly that way. **What is ambiguous is the phrase "one impression".** One campaign, three numbers answering to that name.

**Delivered impressions.** The ad was served and began rendering. Nobody checks whether a human saw it. This is the default billing denominator.

**Viewable impressions.** MRC's *Viewable Ad Impression Measurement Guidelines* (v2.0, August 18, 2015) set a hard bar: a display ad needs **at least 50% of its pixels** inside the viewable space of the browser, on a focused tab, **held for at least one continuous second**. Video needs **two continuous seconds**. Verification companies and media-side quotes run on this caliber.

**Actual visible duration.** The IAB/MRC *Retail Media Measurement Guidelines* call this *duration weighting*: real watch time across billable viewable impressions, divided by the creative's own length. It measures seconds, not counts, and **the guidelines say so plainly: duration weighting is not a measure of advertising effectiveness and cannot stand alone as a CPM.**

**Same month, same batch of impressions, different denominator, different CPM.**

**This is the official worked example from the IAB/MRC *Attention Measurement Guidelines*, November 2025**: 100,000 delivered impressions, 92,100 in the viewport, 81,900 clearing the billable viewability bar. That is an 81.9% viewability rate. **The same 10,000 yuan of spend across those three denominators gives CPMs of 100, 108.6 and 122.1 yuan.** The spread is only 22%, because the example runs an 81.9% viewability rate.

**No real channel performs like that.** Much of the inventory sits below the fold, inside an iframe, or on a tab the user has since switched away from, so real viewability runs well under 81.9%. DataBeat's US programmatic data shows the same point from another angle: **in June 2025 display CPM fell 29.5% year over year while video fell 19.4%. A year later display was up 12.9% and video up 14.3%. Then in September 2026 display rose 8.2% while video fell 20.1%.**

**"CPM was up 8.2%" is not a finished sentence.** Which CPM, which format, which month? Tell your boss "CPM rose 8.2%" and they may hear a video CPM that fell 20.1% that same month.

## One channel, two CPMs, and where the third number comes from

**Back to the 1,000,000 yuan channel.** The contract is negotiated at CPM 60. The channel delivered 16,666,667 billable viewable impressions that month. What is that worth at the report's 45 yuan? 45 × 16,666.7 = **750,000**.

**You paid 1,000,000 and got 750,000 back at the report's rate. The 250,000 in between is the 25% of impressions that never cleared the visibility bar.**

**The chart puts all three calibers side by side: 45 in the report, 60 in the negotiation, 130 under duration weighting.** Here is where 130 comes from. Take a 15-second creative. The MRC example puts average visible time at 5.2 seconds, and only about a third of that time has a person actually watching. Buying a thousand "fully watched" impressions takes 2,885 deliveries, so 2,885 ÷ 1,000 × 45 ≈ 130 yuan. **Quote 130 as a CPM and it cannot be compared against another channel.**

**Two other things flatter the report.** MRC's *Desktop Display Impression Measurement Guidelines* require display counts to happen client-side, after the ad starts rendering. Counting server-side when the page is served — the industry calls it *count on decision* — is rejected as the moment furthest from a human seeing the ad, and plenty of reports use it anyway. MRC's *Invalid Traffic Detection and Filtration Standards Addendum* also splits invalid traffic into **general invalid traffic (GIVT)** — bots, prefetch, invisible placements such as 0x0 and 1x1 — and **sophisticated invalid traffic (SIVT)**. **Skipping that filter inflates the denominator. Part of that 15 yuan may be bots you paid for.**

## Four questions to ask before you sign, and what to do with the answers

**A 25% gap is not the channel taking a 25% cut, and not you being overcharged by 25%. It is two different denominators.**

**The caliber.** Three questions: is the denominator delivered impressions or billable viewable impressions? Is viewability measured at MRC's 50%-of-pixels-plus-one-second standard? For video, two seconds or a 30% completion rate? **This one answer sets the baseline for every number that follows.** Get it in writing, with the threshold and the filter status.

**Where the discount sits.** When you land on "60 yuan", is that a unit price cut from 60 down to 45, or the same unit price with a rebate settled quarterly on top? **The first takes effect at once. The second sits on your payment terms and never shows up in the report, so the paper number stays 60 forever.**

**Whether the rebate sits inside the price or on top of it.** A rebate inside the price is money returned to your account. A rebate on top comes out of the channel's own budget. **A rebate inside the price pulls your effective unit price below the reported CPM. A rebate on top does not.** Compare a report CPM against a competitor's unit price without settling this and the comparison can reverse.

**The validity period.** Many quotes lock the CPM to a quarter and re-auction at the end of it. **A "negotiated" CPM with no expiry stops existing at renewal.**

**Then put your own numbers on one caliber.** Convert every channel's CPM to billable viewable impressions using MRC's standard before you compare. Only then do the numbers compare.

**When the two sides do not reconcile, use the process, not an email.** Section 4.4.1 of the MRC addendum sets out discrepancy handling: both sides run a formal reconciliation, each puts forward a response deadline, and the guidelines suggest 30 days. Start with a tag review to see whether the counting step is broken. If the disagreement survives, escalate to MRC for a third-party read on whether the difference is non-compliance or differing conventions. **This step gets skipped most often and is the most valuable one: recovering 5% of impressions in one reconciliation beats a 2% discount negotiated over a quarter.**

**Last line: the report CPM is not fake, and the negotiated CPM is not the truth.** Neither is wrong. They count different things. **What matters is that the money you pay, the impressions you get, and the CPM you compute all use one denominator.**
