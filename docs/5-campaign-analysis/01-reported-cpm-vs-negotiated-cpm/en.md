# Two CPM numbers, one budget, and no one is cheating

**The report says 45. You negotiated 60. Same channel, same month, same budget.**

**The 15 between them is not markup. The two numbers divide by different things.**

The channel's report counts the impressions it bills for — an ad that was served and began rendering. Your negotiated rate counts the impressions it promises you can see. A smaller denominator makes the price per thousand look higher.

**Before you negotiate, ask two questions: what is the denominator behind this CPM, and does the discount come off the unit price or come out of a rebate?**

## Where the 15 yuan gap comes from

**The gap is in the denominator, not the unit price.**

A mid-sized performance channel, September 2025, 1,000,000 yuan of spend. The platform's delivery report shows CPM 45. Sales negotiates 60 on the same contract. The report bills on *delivered impressions* — the ad was handed to the delivery system and started rendering. The negotiation bills on *billable viewable impressions* — at least half its pixels inside a focused browser tab, held for 1 second.

| Caliber | Denominator (impressions) | CPM | Rule |
|---|---|---|---|
| Delivered impressions | 22,222,222 | 45 yuan | Counts as soon as it renders |
| Billable viewable impressions | 16,666,667 | 60 yuan | Half the pixels plus 1 second |

**16,666,667 ÷ 22,222,222 = 75%.** Only three quarters of this channel's impressions met its own stated visibility standard, and **the ratio between the report CPM and the negotiated CPM is the viewability rate. Nobody owes you that 25%** — it is not a discount, not a rebate, not withheld delivery, only the same impressions landing on different sides of two counting rules.

## One metric, three ways to count an impression

**CPM itself is clear: how much you paid for a thousand impressions.** Magnite's 10-K filed in 2025 puts it that way. **What is murky is the four words "one impression".** The same campaign has three numbers claiming that name.

**First, delivered impressions.** The ad was served and began rendering, and nobody asks whether a human saw it — the default billing denominator.

**Second, viewable impressions.** MRC's *Viewable Ad Impression Measurement Guidelines* (version 2.0, August 18, 2015) set a hard bar: a display ad needs **at least 50% of its pixels** inside the viewable space of the browser, on a focused tab, **held for at least one continuous second**; video needs **two continuous seconds**. This bar is a *billable viewable impression* — the caliber verification companies and media-side quotes run on.

**Third, actual visible duration.** The IAB/MRC *Retail Media Measurement Guidelines* call this *duration weighting*: real watch time across all billable viewable impressions, divided by the creative's own length. It measures seconds, not counts, and **the guidelines say it plainly: duration weighting is not a measure of advertising effectiveness and cannot be used as a standalone CPM.**

**Same month, same batch of impressions, different denominator, different CPM.**

**This is the official worked example from the IAB/MRC *Attention Measurement Guidelines*, November 2025**: of 100,000 delivered impressions, 92,100 entered the viewport and 81,900 met the billable viewability bar, a viewability rate of 81.9%. **The same 100,000 yuan across those three denominators gives CPMs of 100, 108.6 and 122.1 yuan**; scaled to 1,000,000 yuan, 1,000, 1,086 and 1,221. **The spread is only 22%, because this example runs an 81.9% viewability rate.**

**No real channel performs like that.** Plenty of inventory sits below the fold or on a tab the user has switched away from, so real viewability runs well under 81.9%. DataBeat's US programmatic data shows the same point from another angle: **in one month display CPM fell 29.5% year over year while video fell 19.4%, and a year later display was up 12.9% while video was down 20.1%. The two formats move in opposite directions.**

## One channel, two CPMs, and where the third comes from

**Back to the 1,000,000 yuan channel. The contract is negotiated at CPM 60, and the channel delivered 16,666,667 billable viewable impressions that month. What is that worth at the report's 45 yuan?** 45 × 16,666.7 = **750,000**.

**You paid 1,000,000 and got 750,000 of value back at the report's rate. The 250,000 in between is the 25% of impressions that never cleared the visibility bar.**

**This chart puts all three calibers together: 45 in the report, 60 in the negotiation, 92 under duration weighting.** The 92 comes from the MRC example: average visible time is 5.2 seconds against a 15-second creative, and only about a third of that time is watched, so the per-second cost is 45 ÷ 0.347 ≈ 92 yuan. **Used as a CPM, the 92 cannot be compared against any other channel.**

**Two more things flatter the report.** MRC's *Desktop Display Impression Measurement Guidelines* require display counts to happen on the client side, after the ad starts rendering; counting server-side when the page is served — the industry calls it *count on decision* — is rejected as the moment furthest from the user seeing the ad, and plenty of reports use it anyway. And MRC's *Invalid Traffic Detection and Filtration Standards Addendum* splits invalid traffic into **general invalid traffic (GIVT)** — bots, prefetch, invisible placements such as 0x0 and 1x1 — and **sophisticated invalid traffic (SIVT)**. **Skipping that filter inflates the denominator: part of that 15 yuan may be bots you paid for.**

## Four questions before you sign, and what to do with the answers

**A 25% gap is not the channel taking a 25% cut, nor you being overcharged by 25%. It is two different denominators.**

**First, the caliber.** Ask three questions. Is the denominator delivered impressions or billable viewable impressions? Is viewability measured at MRC's 50%-of-pixels-plus-one-second standard? For video, is it two seconds or a 30% completion rate? **This one answer sets the baseline for every number that follows.** Get it in writing, with the viewability threshold and the invalid-traffic filter status.

**Second, where the discount sits.** When you land on "60 yuan", is that a unit price cut from 60 down to 45, or the same unit price with a rebate settled quarterly on top? **The first takes effect immediately; the second sits on your payment terms and never appears in the report, so the paper number stays 60 forever.**

**Third, whether the rebate is inside the price or on top of it.** A rebate inside the price is money returned to your account; a rebate on top comes out of the channel's own budget. **A rebate inside the price pulls your effective unit price below the reported CPM; a rebate on top does not.** Compare report CPMs against a competitor's unit price without settling this and the comparison can reverse.

**Fourth, the validity period.** Many quotes lock the CPM to a quarter and re-auction at the end of it. **A "negotiated" CPM with no expiry stops existing at renewal.**

**Then put your own numbers on one caliber.** Convert every channel's CPM to billable viewable impressions before you compare, using MRC's standard. Only then do the numbers compare.

**When the two sides do not reconcile, use the process, not an email.** Section 4.4.1 of the MRC addendum sets out discrepancy handling: both sides run a formal reconciliation, each puts forward a response deadline, and the guidelines suggest 30 days. Start with a tag review to see whether the counting step is broken; if the disagreement survives, escalate it to MRC for a third-party read on whether the difference is non-compliance or differing conventions. **This step gets skipped most often and is the most valuable one: recovering 5% of impressions in one reconciliation beats a 2% discount negotiated over a quarter.**

**Last line: the report CPM is not fake, and the negotiated CPM is not the truth.** Neither is wrong. They count different things. **What matters is that the money you pay, the impressions you get, and the CPM you compute use one denominator.**
