# The same budget buys three times as many impressions in Germany as in the United States

**Spend the same money in the United States and in Germany and you get 3.46 times more impressions in Germany, and 13% fewer conversions.**

That gap is not a pricing mistake by the platform, and it is not you overpaying. Expensive regions are expensive because more brands bid for the same person, because fewer impressions there turn into conversions, and because only so many kinds of inventory exist there.

**A geographic cost map is not a CPM bar chart. It is CPM, conversion density, bidding density and inventory, read together.**

## Where the three-times gap comes from

**AgencyAnalytics calculated these country averages in October 2025 from more than 44,000 Google Ads delivery records.**

| Market | Cost per click | CPM | Cost per conversion |
|---|---|---|---|
| United States | $2.64 | $114.89 | $38.41 |
| United Kingdom | $1.30 | $43.00 | $33.98 |
| Germany | $1.10 | $33.25 | $34.71 |
| India | $0.15 | $2.48 | $5.91 |

**Germany's CPM is 29% of America's, and its cost per conversion is only 9.6% lower.** The CPM savings are mostly eaten back by weaker conversion density. Four things hold up that gap.

**Bidding density.** How many brands want the same person. US search ad revenue reached $102.9 billion in 2024, which is 39.8% of the $258.6 billion in total US digital ad revenue. More brands paying for the same person pushes the price up.

**Conversion density.** How many conversions one impression produces. Germany is expensive partly because fewer people buy there, and partly because the same impression converts less often.

**Inventory variety.** In the United States you can buy social, video and retail media inventory; in Germany the same assortment may not exist. Public estimates put programmatic guaranteed PMP/PG deals (a contract where a publisher sells a fixed amount of inventory in advance) at 1.5 to 3 times the CPM of open-auction inventory. A market where most of what you can buy is guaranteed has no one to negotiate against.

**Purchasing power.** Bidders price in what people in the region can afford. **Of the four, only inventory is yours to change in a contract.**

## Four numbers a geographic report has to carry

**A typical geographic report gives you CPM and clicks. To read it as a cost map you need three more. The first is conversion density, which reports rarely carry, so compute it: cost per conversion ÷ CPM = conversions per thousand impressions.**

| Market | Conversions per thousand impressions | Impressions needed for one conversion |
|---|---|---|
| United States | 2.99 | 334 |
| Canada | 2.67 | 374 |
| Australia | 2.32 | 431 |
| United Kingdom | 1.27 | 790 |
| Germany | 0.96 | 1,044 |
| India | 0.42 | 2,383 |

**Germany needs 3.1 times as many impressions per conversion as the United States.** On CPM alone it looks cheap; read with conversions, it is expensive.

**The second is bidding density.** No direct number appears in a report, but two indirect signals exist: **impression share**, which falls when other advertisers compete for the same placements, and **the number of competitors on the same keyword**.

**The third is inventory.** Which formats you can buy, and whether those placements sit in an open auction.

**And one thing hiding in the report's own categories: people versus location.** Platforms split "people who live here" from "people interested in here" into separate rows, and reports hand you the two mixed together by default. **For a geographic cost map, take only the "people here" rows.**


**This is the most important chart on the page.** United States against Germany: CPM differs by 3.46 times, cost per conversion differs by 1.13 times. **Cheap impressions in Germany do not buy cheap conversions.**

## Cheap is not the same as good value

**Turn that counterexample into money.** A $100,000 budget split half and half. The $50,000 in the United States: CPM 114.89 buys 435,199 impressions, and at $38.41 per conversion that is 1,302 conversions. The $50,000 in Germany: CPM 33.25 buys 1,503,759 impressions, and at $34.71 per conversion that is 1,441 conversions. **At the same price, the United States delivers under a third of the impressions and only 10% fewer conversions.**

**Move that $50,000 between the two markets and the averages say:**

| Split | Impressions | Conversions | Blended cost per conversion |
|---|---|---|---|
| $50k US / $50k Germany | 1,938,958 | 2,742 | $36.47 |
| $70k US / $30k Germany | 1,511,534 | 2,687 | $37.22 |
| $80k US / $20k Germany | 1,297,822 | 2,659 | $37.61 |

**Shift budget toward the expensive market and conversions fall**, because the cheap market's low conversion density spreads its unit price advantage thin.

**Cheap markets have a second job: buying volume fast.** The same $50,000 buys 1,503,759 impressions in Germany in a month and 435,199 in the United States, so **delivery runs 3.46 times faster.** Quarter-end pushes, a limited-time launch, a one-off awareness burst: cheap markets have a place there. Expensive markets are the other road: high bidding density, fast delivery, and people likelier to buy. Smart Bidding treats location as one bidding signal because it expects certain markets to convert better. **Cheap markets convert efficiently but reach cheap people; expensive markets convert poorly but reach better ones. Which one you want depends on whether you are short of money or short of time this month.**

## A plan for running two markets three times apart

**The situation: an outdoor gear brand splits budget between the United States and Germany in March 2026, and has never run the two markets separately.**

**Step one, fix the reporting scope.** Location reports default to mixing "people here" with "interested in here". **Switch the view to "people here" only before the plan starts.**

**Step two, run a baseline before splitting budget.** $5,000 per market, the same keywords and the same creative, for two weeks. Take out three numbers: CPM, cost per conversion and impression share, each by market.

**Step three, split budget on cost per conversion, not on CPM.** Germany at $34.71 and the United States at $38.41 look 9.6% apart. Read impression share alongside it: **if Germany's impression share is already high, you have bid the low-cost market down to its floor, and more money will not buy more impressions** — leaving the expensive market as the only place to scale.

**Step four, set a stop-loss line.** Geographic costs move on their own. AsterioBid's first-half 2025 report has the average US eCPM rising from $0.85 to $1.037, up 22%, while the European average moved only from $0.36 to $0.39. **America's expense is climbing; Germany's discount is stable.** Write the stop-loss against that: if a market's cost per conversion runs 30% above baseline for two weeks running, cut that budget.

**Then verify, because a report records what happened, not what the ads caused.** Leave one market unadvertised. That is a geo experiment, and Google's 2011 paper *Measuring Ad Effectiveness Using Geo Experiments* is the template: split markets into two groups at random, one advertising as normal and one not advertising at all. **The platform bill showed a cost per click of $2.40, while the experiment measured an incremental cost per click of $3.00.** Platform reporting understates cost by 20%, because one in five paid clicks was an organic click taken over. Granularity has limits: the US unit is the 210 Nielsen DMA regions, and **the finer the cut, the smaller the control group.**

**Then read your own report rather than the industry average.** A study published in October 2026 (Doctor Ads Profit Forensics, 11 accounts, 38 matched pairs, full-year 2025 data) gives a reference magnitude: **inside the same search campaign, the more expensive of two countries has a median cost per click 1.22 times the cheaper one**, and across a whole account the median ratio is about 2.0. **Differences between campaigns (keywords, bids, formats) are larger than the difference between countries**, so much of the 3.46 times in your own account is not geography.

**And the country mix changes on its own.** In the same study, one country costs $1.00 per click and another $1.20. In March, 80% of clicks come from the cheap country and the campaign average is $1.04; in April the split is even and the average rises to $1.10. **Neither country's price moved, and the campaign average went up 6%.** Statisticians call this Simpson's paradox, where a shifting mix of groups reverses the trend in the average. **A CPM that rose in your report may reflect a shift in country mix, not a market that got more expensive.** The fix: take last period's prices, multiply by this period's click shares, recompute. The difference is the mix effect, the remainder the price effect.

## Read geography with conversions attached, never on CPM alone

**No number on a geographic cost map stands on its own.**

**A low CPM does not mean good value.** Germany's CPM is 29% of America's and its cost per conversion only 9.6% lower, and conversion density eats the missing 65%.

**A high CPM does not mean overpriced.** Markets with high bidding density cost more, but you buy volume faster and reach people who convert better.

**A country average CPM in a report is a mixture** of bidding density, conversion density, inventory variety and purchasing power, with "people here" and "interested in here" mixed in on top.

**Four actions travel with this. Filter the report and take only the "people here" rows. Compute cost per conversion and CPM together, where impressions per conversion = cost per conversion ÷ CPM × 1000. When a campaign's average CPM moves, recompute it with last period's prices to strip out the country mix before calling it a price change. And to verify anything, leave a market unadvertised and measure the increment with the method in [→ Incrementality testing](../../8-analysis-toolbox/02-incrementality-testing/en.md).**

**Last line: not one number on a geographic cost map can decide anything by itself.** Cheap CPM is not good value, expensive CPM is not a reason to cut, a report average is not your account, an industry average is not next month's auction. **What the map does tell you is where you have room, where you do not, and what to measure next.**
