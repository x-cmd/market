# The United States costs 3.46 times Germany's CPM, and almost all of that gap is not a price difference

**The same budget buys 3.46 times more reach in Germany than in the United States.**

That gap is not a pricing mistake and not you overpaying. The United States is expensive because more brands bid for the same person, because fewer impressions there convert, and because only so many kinds of inventory exist.

**A geographic cost map is not a CPM bar chart. It is four numbers read together: CPM, conversion density, bidding density and inventory.**

## A three-times CPM gap hides a much smaller cost-per-conversion gap

**The raw numbers.** AgencyAnalytics calculated these country averages in October 2025 from more than 44,000 Google Ads delivery records.

| Market | Cost per click | CPM | Cost per conversion |
|---|---|---|---|
| United States | $2.64 | $114.89 | $38.41 |
| United Kingdom | $1.30 | $43.00 | $33.98 |
| Germany | $1.10 | $33.25 | $34.71 |
| India | $0.15 | $2.48 | $5.91 |

**Germany's CPM is 29% of America's, but its cost per conversion is only 9.6% lower.** Most of that CPM saving gets eaten back by weaker conversion density. Four things hold the gap up.

**Bidding density** is how many brands will pay for the same person. US search ad revenue reached $102.9 billion in 2024, 39.8% of the $258.6 billion total US digital ad revenue. **Conversion density** is how many conversions one impression produces: Germany is expensive because fewer people buy there and because the same impression converts less often.

**Inventory variety** is what formats you can buy. Public estimates put programmatic guaranteed deals (a contract where a publisher sells inventory in advance) at 1.5 to 3 times the CPM of open-auction inventory, and where most of what you can buy is guaranteed, you have nobody to negotiate against. **Purchasing power** sits inside the bid: advertisers price in what people in the region can afford. **Of these four, only inventory is yours to change in a contract.**

## What a geographic report has to carry beyond CPM

**A standard geographic report gives you CPM and clicks. A cost map needs three more. Conversion density is missing from reports, so compute it: cost per conversion ÷ CPM = conversions per thousand impressions.**

| Market | Conversions per thousand impressions | Impressions needed for one conversion |
|---|---|---|
| United States | 2.99 | 334 |
| United Kingdom | 1.27 | 790 |
| Germany | 0.96 | 1,044 |
| India | 0.42 | 2,383 |

**Germany needs 3.1 times as many impressions per conversion as the United States.** On CPM alone it looks cheap. Read with conversions, it is expensive.

**Bidding density** has no direct number, but two indirect signals exist: **impression share**, which drops when other advertisers compete for the same placements, and **how many competitors bid on the same keyword**. **Inventory** is which formats you can buy (search, social, video and retail media CPMs are not comparable) and whether those placements sit in an open auction.

**One more thing hides in the report's own categories: people versus location.** Platforms split "people who live here" from "people interested in here" into separate rows, and reports mix the two by default. **For a geographic cost map, take only the "people here" rows.**

## Cheap is not the same as good value

**Turn that into money.** Split a $100,000 budget half and half. The $50,000 in the United States buys 435,199 impressions, which at $38.41 per conversion is 1,302 conversions. The $50,000 in Germany buys 1,503,759 impressions, which at $34.71 per conversion is 1,441 conversions. **The US delivers under a third of the reach for 10% fewer conversions.**

**Shift that $50,000 between the markets:**

| Split | Impressions | Conversions | Blended cost per conversion |
|---|---|---|---|
| $50k US / $50k Germany | 1,938,958 | 2,742 | $36.47 |
| $70k US / $30k Germany | 1,511,534 | 2,687 | $37.22 |
| $80k US / $20k Germany | 1,297,822 | 2,659 | $37.61 |

**Move budget toward the expensive market and conversions fall.** The cheap market's low conversion density spreads its unit price advantage thin.

**Cheap markets have a second job: buying volume fast.** The same $50,000 buys 1,503,759 impressions in Germany in a month and 435,199 in the United States, so delivery runs 3.46 times faster. Quarter-end pushes, a limited-time launch, a one-off awareness burst: cheap markets have a place there.

**Expensive markets are the other road:** high bidding density, fast delivery, and people likelier to buy, which is why Smart Bidding treats location as one bidding signal. **Cheap markets reach cheap people, expensive markets reach better ones. Which one you want depends on whether this month you are short of money or short of time.**

## A plan for running two markets three times apart

**The situation: an outdoor gear brand splits budget between the United States and Germany in March 2026, and has never run the two markets separately.**

**Fix the reporting scope first.** Location reports mix "people here" with "interested in here" by default. Switch to "people here" only before the plan starts.

**Run a baseline before splitting budget.** $5,000 per market, the same keywords and creative, two weeks, to get three numbers per market: CPM, cost per conversion, impression share. **Split budget on cost per conversion, not on CPM.** Germany at $34.71 and the US at $38.41 look 9.6% apart. Read impression share alongside: **if Germany's impression share is already high, you have bid the low-cost market down to its floor and more money will not buy more impressions**, leaving the expensive market as the only place to scale.

**Set a stop-loss line.** Geographic costs move on their own. AsterioBid's first-half 2025 report has US average eCPM rising from $0.85 to $1.037 while the European average moved only from $0.36 to $0.39. Cut a market's budget once its cost per conversion runs 30% above baseline for two weeks running.

**Then verify, because a report records what happened, not what the ads caused.** Leave one market unadvertised. That is a geo experiment, and Google's 2011 paper *Measuring Ad Effectiveness Using Geo Experiments* is the template: split markets at random into one group that advertises as normal and one that does not advertise at all. **The platform bill showed a cost per click of $2.40, while the experiment measured an incremental cost per click of $3.00.** Platform reporting understates cost by 20%, because one in five paid clicks was an organic click taken over. Granularity has limits too. Google has said postcode-level targeting does not work, and the standard US unit is the 210 Nielsen DMA regions, where **the finer the cut, the smaller the control group and the wider the interval.**

**Read your own report rather than the industry average.** A study published in October 2026 (Doctor Ads Profit Forensics, 11 accounts, 38 matched pairs, full-year 2025 data) puts the gap in perspective: **inside the same search campaign, the more expensive of two countries has a median cost per click 1.22 times the cheaper one**, middle half of accounts between 1.12 and 1.42. Across a whole account the median ratio is about 2.0. **Differences between campaigns run larger than the difference between countries, so much of the 3.46 times in your own account is not geography.** The country mix changes on its own. In the same study, one country costs $1.00 per click and another $1.20. In March 80% of clicks come from the cheap country with a campaign average of $1.04; in April the split is even and the average rises to $1.10. **Neither country's price moved, and the campaign average went up 6%.** Statisticians call this Simpson's paradox, where a shifting mix of groups reverses the trend in the average. **A CPM that rose in your report may reflect a shift in country mix, not a market that got more expensive.** The fix is to take last period's prices, multiply by this period's click shares, and recompute.

## Read geography with conversions attached, never on CPM alone

**No number on a geographic cost map stands on its own.** A low CPM does not mean good value: Germany's CPM is 29% of America's and its cost per conversion is only 9.6% lower, with conversion density eating the missing 65%. A high CPM does not mean overpriced: markets with high bidding density cost more, but you buy volume faster and reach people who convert better. And a country average CPM in a report is a mixture of all four factors, with "people here" and "interested in here" mixed in on top.

**Four actions travel with this: filter the report down to the "people here" rows, compute cost per conversion and CPM together (impressions per conversion = cost per conversion ÷ CPM × 1000), recompute a moving campaign average with last period's prices to strip out the country mix, and verify with a held-back market using the method in [→ Incrementality testing](../../8-analysis-toolbox/02-incrementality-testing/en.md).**

**Cheap CPM is not good value, expensive CPM is not a reason to cut, a report average is not your account, and an industry average is not next month's auction.**
