**That is the whole argument: a promotion month cannot be judged on its own. The three months after the promotion decide whether it made money.**

## How much of the November spike was ever new

**Start with the spike.** In November 2024 the EU27 seasonally adjusted retail turnover index for "specialised stores selling cultural and entertainment goods" jumped from 112.6 in October to 116.6, up 3.6% month on month. The last Friday of November was Black Friday, and nearly every retailer loaded its discounts into those few days.

**Judge that month alone and the story flatters you: the category grew 3.6%, and the discounts take all the credit.** But December fell back to 114.5 and January 2025 to 113.8, 1.8% and 2.4% below November. **Three months of give-back ate most of the spike.**

**How much of that 3.6% was seasonal in the first place?** On the unadjusted figures, December 2024 turnover rose 39.1% month on month, the bare "year-end sale" number. Seasonal adjustment turns the same December into minus 1.8%. **One December, two readings, forty percentage points apart. The gap is the question "how busy is this month normally?"**

**So seasonality is the first layer to strip off. What survives has to be read from the months that follow.**

## A promotion pulls future orders into the present

**"Pulling demand forward" means something specific.** Some buyers were going to purchase this in December anyway, perhaps as a Christmas gift, perhaps waiting for the new year sale. They saw Black Friday and bought early. Their spending did not vanish; it moved three weeks earlier. The extra volume in November is exactly the volume missing from December.

**The data shows it.** In 2023, November came in at 116.4, 2.4% above the August-to-October average of 113.7. December was 114.3, January 2024 was 112.3, February was 113.6. **Two of those three months sit below November, and the third is nearly flat.**

**If the promotion had created genuinely new demand, December through February would have come back to the old level or higher, because the new customers would still be buying.** Those three months went the other way. **That is the signature of demand pulled forward.**

**January ran 3.5% below November, deeper than December's 1.8%,** because December still had Christmas gifting underneath it and January had no such floor.

## Align three windows, and read the three months after

**The three-month window is not arbitrary.** A promotion week squeezes volume out of the weeks behind it, so the useful number is the stretch after the promotion, long enough for the pulled-forward demand to show itself.

**The method is three aligned blocks:**

| Block | Period | What it answers |
|---|---|---|
| Baseline | Three months before the promotion (August–October) | Where this category sits when there is no promotion |
| Promotion | The promotion month (November) | How much volume the promotion pushed up |
| Observation | Three months after the promotion (December, January, February) | Whether the pulled-forward volume shows up here |

**Baseline and observation both take three months, because one month gets thrown around by one-off events** such as weather and holiday timing.

**Applied to the 2024 promotion: the baseline (August–October) averages 112.53. November is 116.6, 4.07 points above it, or 3.6%. The observation block (December 2024 through February 2025) averages 113.67, 1.01% above the baseline.**

**Of the 3.6% the promotion month showed, 1.0% survives into the observation window. The net increment this Black Friday left behind is roughly a third of what the promotion month appeared to deliver.**

## Splitting out the part that was real

**That one-third is the 2024 result, and the number that matters most here is that it does not repeat. The share of demand pulled forward changes year to year, so it has to be measured, not assumed.**

**The same method on two more categories shows the spread.** One is "specialised stores selling information and communication equipment". The other is "specialised stores selling food, beverages and tobacco", which barely discounts and has no Black-Friday-style date when retailers set prices together.

**For cultural and entertainment goods in 2023, November ran 2.4% above baseline and the observation window came back 0.3% below it. In 2024 it was 3.6% and 1.0%, so about a third of that year's net increment stayed.**

**Information and communication equipment is the other extreme.** In 2023 November ran 2.5% above baseline and the observation window fell to 1.3% below it, **averaging minus 0.3% across the four months**. In 2024 it was 1.2% and minus 0.9%, averaging to minus 0.4%. **Seen a year later, that promotion month added nothing.**

**Food, beverages and tobacco is the reference line:** November ran only 0.4% and 0.7% above baseline, no promotion spike, and the observation window held at 1.0% and 1.4% above it. **That is real growth: nothing extra in the promotion month, nothing missing afterwards.**

**Averaging all four months gives the cleanest answer: cultural and entertainment goods +0.4% in 2023 and +1.7% in 2024; information and communication equipment -0.3% and -0.4%; food, beverages and tobacco +0.8% and +1.2%.**

**The harder the promotion month jumps, the harder the observation window falls, and the smaller the net increment after averaging.**

**The split has three steps. Strip seasonality** with the adjusted figures, removing what is busy every year. This layer takes out the most volume and is the one most easily skipped. **Then read the give-back:** compare the three months after the promotion with the three months before it, and if the difference is near zero or negative, the volume was moved rather than added. **Then average for the net,** weighting the promotion month's excess over baseline together with the observation window's deviation from baseline. **That number is what the promotion actually left behind.**

**One check runs alongside it: payback.** If the third or fourth month after the promotion climbs back above baseline, those buyers came back for more, and they are new customers who span periods. **If nothing returns, the volume was pulled forward permanently, and the later months never had it to begin with.**

## A 40% week that lands back where it started

**Back to the opening question.** One promotion week lifts the report by 40%, and three weeks later it is back where it started. **That is not a failed promotion. That is a promotion working as designed.** Here is what a "successful-looking" Black Friday looks like once the numbers are spread out:

| | Seen in the promotion month | Next three months | Net increment after averaging four months |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cultural and entertainment goods 2023 | +2.4% | -0.3% | +0.4% |
| Cultural and entertainment goods 2024 | +3.6% | +1.0% | +1.7% |
| Information and communication equipment 2023 | +2.5% | -1.3% | -0.3% |
| Information and communication equipment 2024 | +1.2% | -0.9% | -0.4% |
| Food, beverages and tobacco 2023 | +0.4% | +1.0% | +0.8% |
| Food, beverages and tobacco 2024 | +0.7% | +1.4% | +1.2% |

**The rows where the promotion month column and the last column diverge most are the rows with a promotion spike.** The two food, beverages and tobacco rows are nearly identical in those two columns, because their growth was real to begin with.

**What does that gap mean for the business?** A promotion that only shifts orders from later into the earlier period has a net increment near zero, **which means it washes out on the books**: the extra gross margin from the days that sold more does not cover the margin given up in discounting plus the operating cost. **That does not make it worthless.** It may have brought in new customers, verified through repeat purchase. It may also have taken share from a competitor, which is a separate thing from net increment.

**So the point of this page is narrow and precise: the lift in the promotion month carries almost no information about whether the promotion worked.** It mixes new demand with future demand that was moved, and the ratio changes with the category, the year, and the competitive situation. **The only way to know the real effect is to wait for the promotion to end and read what follows.**

## Price the promotion on the post-promotion books

**Three numbers belong to a promotion: the gross margin given up in discounting, the incremental revenue it brought in, and how much of that increment was real. The third one has to be settled first.**

**First, change the denominator of the promotion ROI.** The usual method divides promotion week revenue by the promotion spend. **That denominator is loaded with pulled-forward volume, so the ROI reads high.** Replace it with the net increment of the three months after the promotion relative to the baseline.

**Second, the baseline cannot be last year's promotion month.** Last November was a promotion month too, so using it as the baseline treats the promotion itself as normal. **The baseline has to come from a stretch with no promotion** — August to October, or the same months a year earlier.

**Third, the three months after the promotion cannot be eaten by the next promotion.** If December brings another big sale, the observation window has no clean stretch in it. **Either move the window earlier, or use a category with no following promotion as the reference.**

**One cost rarely gets mentioned: demand pull-forward trains customers to wait.** Once buyers learn to shop during a sale, conversion outside promotion periods stays a notch lower for good. **That loss never shows up in the promotion month's report. It shows up in the baseline of every month that follows.**

**Finally, the test.** A promotion is worth what its net increment over the three months after it, relative to the baseline, is worth at that margin, minus the gross margin given up in discounting and the operating cost. **If that number comes out negative, the promotion did not fail. It spent next year's business early.**

**Data**: All figures come from the Eurostat monthly turnover and volume of sales in wholesale and retail trade, dataset `sts_trtu_m`. Scope is the EU27 (EU27_2020), the index base is 2021=100, and two versions are used: seasonally and calendar adjusted (SCA), and calendar adjusted but not seasonally adjusted (CA). The three categories map to NACE codes G476 (cultural and entertainment goods), G474 (information and communication equipment) and G472 (food, beverages and tobacco). The baseline block is the three-month average from August to October; the observation block is the three-month average of December, January and February. The net increment after averaging four months is computed as (promotion month above baseline + 3 × observation window deviation from baseline) ÷ 4. The adjusted month-on-month figures come from the PCH_PRE series in the same dataset; the unadjusted December month-on-month figure is derived by dividing the calendar-adjusted index month by month. Data runs through October 2025, the last month of the adjusted index series; November 2025 and later have not been published. February 2020 through 2021 was hit by the pandemic and produced extreme swings in every category, and no comparison here uses that period. No paid data source was used.

**Reproduce**: `x dbnomics data Eurostat/sts_trtu_m/M.NETTUR.G476.SCA.I21.EU27_2020` (swap G476 for G474 or G472 for the other two categories) returns the adjusted index, `x dbnomics data Eurostat/sts_trtu_m/M.NETTUR.G476.SCA.PCH_PRE.EU27_2020` the adjusted month-on-month, and `x dbnomics data Eurostat/sts_trtu_m/M.NETTUR.G476.CA.I21.EU27_2020` the calendar-adjusted index. The numbers in the charts are direct readings and differences of these three series; change the series inside the d= parameter of the link to swap in your own data.
