**This is the spine of the argument: the lift in the promotion month cannot be booked on its own. The three months after the promotion decide whether the promotion made money.**

## How much of the November spike was ever new

**Start with the spike on the chart.** In November 2024, the EU27 seasonally adjusted retail turnover index for "specialised stores selling cultural and entertainment goods" jumped from 112.6 in October to 116.6, a 3.6% month-on-month rise. The last Friday of November was Black Friday, and almost every retailer pushed its discounts into those few days.

**Take that month alone and the story sounds great: the category grew 3.6% in the promotion month, and the discounts get all the credit.** But December's index fell to 114.5 and January 2025 to 113.8, 1.8% and 2.4% below November. **Three months of give-back ate most of the November spike.**

**How much of that 3.6% was seasonal anyway?** Look at the unadjusted raw numbers. December 2024 retail turnover rose 39.1% month on month — the bare "year-end sale" figure. Run the same data through seasonal adjustment and December comes out at minus 1.8%. **One December, two numbers, forty percentage points apart. The whole gap is the question "how busy is this month normally?"**

**So the first layer to strip off the promotion week's 40% is seasonality.** How much survives has to be read from the months that follow.

## A promotion pulls future orders into the present

**"Pulling demand forward" has a specific meaning.** Some people were going to buy this in December anyway — maybe as a Christmas gift, maybe waiting for the new year sale. They saw Black Friday at the end of November and bought early.

**In aggregate, that spending did not disappear. It moved three weeks earlier.** The extra volume on the November report is exactly the volume missing from the December report.

**What does it look like in the data?** Take 2023. November's index was 116.4, 2.4% above the 113.7 average of August through October. Then December was 114.3, January 2024 was 112.3, and February was 113.6. **Two of those three months sit below November, and the third is nearly flat.**

**If the promotion had genuinely created new demand, December through February would come back to where they were, or higher, because the new customers would still be buying.** Instead those three months went the other way. **That is the signature of demand pulled forward.**

**January was 3.5% below November, a deeper drop than December's 1.8%,** because December still had Christmas gifting underneath it and January has no such floor.

## Align three windows, and read the three months after

**Three months is not an arbitrary window.** A promotion week squeezes the volume out of the weeks after it, so the number to read is the stretch after it, long enough for the pulled-forward demand to show itself.

**The method is three aligned blocks:**

| Block | Period | What it answers |
|---|---|---|
| Baseline | Three months before the promotion (August–October) | Where this category sits when there is no promotion |
| Promotion | The promotion month (November) | How much volume the promotion pushed up |
| Observation | Three months after the promotion (December, January, February) | Whether the pulled-forward volume shows up here |

**Both the baseline and the observation block take three months, because a single month gets knocked around by one-off events** like weather and holiday timing.

**Run the method on the 2024 promotion: the baseline (August–October) averages 112.53, November is 116.6 — 4.07 points above it, or 3.6% — and the observation block (December 2024 through February 2025) averages 113.67, 1.01% above the baseline.**

**Of the 3.6% the promotion month showed, only 1.0% is still there in the observation window. The net increment this Black Friday left behind is roughly a third of what the promotion month appeared to show.**

## Splitting out the part that was real

**That one-third is the 2024 result. It is not the same every time, and this is the most important line in the article: the share of demand pulled forward changes every year, so it has to be measured, not assumed.**

**The same method applied to two more categories shows the spread immediately.** One is "specialised stores selling food, beverages and tobacco", which barely discounts and has no Black-Friday-style date where retailers set prices together. The third is "specialised stores selling information and communication equipment".

**For cultural and entertainment goods in 2023, November ran 2.4% above the baseline and the observation window came back 0.3% below it. 2024 was 3.6% and 1.0%, so about a third of that year's net increment stayed.**

**Information and communication equipment is the other end of the range.** In 2023, November ran 2.5% above the baseline and the observation window fell to 1.3% below it, **averaging minus 0.3% over four months**. 2024 was 1.2% and minus 0.9%, averaging to minus 0.4%. **The volume visible in the promotion month is, seen a year later, volume that was never added.**

**Food, beverages and tobacco is the reference line:** November ran only 0.4% and 0.7% above baseline — no promotion spike — and the observation window held at 1.0% and 1.4% above it. **That is real growth: nothing extra in the promotion month, nothing missing afterwards.**

**Averaging across all four months is the cleanest answer: cultural and entertainment goods +0.4% in 2023 and +1.7% in 2024; information and communication equipment −0.3% and −0.4%; food, beverages and tobacco +0.8% and +1.2%.**

**The pattern is clear: the harder the promotion month jumps, the harder the observation window falls, and the smaller the net increment after averaging.**

**The split has three steps. Strip seasonality** with the adjusted figures, to remove "this month is busy every year" — this layer removes the most volume and is the one most easily skipped. **Then read the give-back:** compare the three months after the promotion with the three months before it, and if the difference is near zero or negative, the volume was moved rather than added. **Then average for the net,** weighting the promotion month's excess over the baseline together with the observation window's deviation from the baseline. **That number is the volume the promotion actually left behind.**

**One more check runs alongside it: whether there is a payback.** If the third or fourth month after the promotion climbs back above the baseline, those buyers came back for more, and they are new customers who span periods. **If nothing returns, the volume was pulled forward permanently, and the later months never had it to begin with.**

## A 40% week that lands back where it started

**Back to the opening question.** One promotion week lifts the report by 40%, and three weeks later it is back where it started — **that is not a failed promotion. That is a promotion working as designed.**

**Here is what a "successful-looking" Black Friday actually looks like once the numbers are spread out:**

| | Seen in the promotion month | Next three months | Net increment after averaging four months |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cultural and entertainment goods 2023 | +2.4% | -0.3% | +0.4% |
| Cultural and entertainment goods 2024 | +3.6% | +1.0% | +1.7% |
| Information and communication equipment 2023 | +2.5% | -1.3% | -0.3% |
| Information and communication equipment 2024 | +1.2% | -0.9% | -0.4% |
| Food, beverages and tobacco 2023 | +0.4% | +1.0% | +0.8% |
| Food, beverages and tobacco 2024 | +0.7% | +1.4% | +1.2% |

**The rows where the promotion month column and the last column diverge most are the rows with a promotion spike.** The two food, beverage and tobacco rows are almost identical in those two columns, because their growth was real to begin with.

**What does that gap mean for the business?** A promotion that only moves orders from later into the earlier period has a net increment near zero, **which means it washes out on the books:** the extra gross margin from the days that sold more does not cover the margin given up in discounting plus the operating cost. **That does not make it worthless.** It may have brought in new customers, and that part has to be verified through repeat purchase.

**So the point of this page is narrow and precise: the lift in the promotion month carries almost no information about whether the promotion worked.** It mixes new demand with future demand that was moved, and the ratio changes with the category, the year, and the competitive situation. **The only way to know the real effect is to wait for the promotion to end and look at what comes after.**

## Price the promotion on the post-promotion books

**A promotion has three numbers attached to it: the gross margin given up in discounting, the incremental revenue it brought in, and how much of that increment was real. The third one has to be settled first.**

**First, the denominator of the promotion ROI has to change.** The usual method divides promotion week revenue by the promotion spend. **That denominator is loaded with the pulled-forward volume, so the ROI reads high.** Replace it with the net increment of the three months after the promotion relative to the baseline.

**Second, the baseline cannot be last year's promotion month.** Last November was a promotion month too, so using it as the baseline treats the promotion itself as the normal. **The baseline has to come from a period with no promotion** — August to October, or the same months a year earlier.

**Third, the three months after the promotion cannot be eaten by the next promotion.** If December has another big sale, the observation window has no clean stretch in it. **Either move the observation window earlier, or use a category with no following promotion as the reference.**

**One cost rarely gets mentioned: demand pull-forward trains customers to wait.** Once customers learn to buy during a sale, conversion outside promotion periods stays permanently a notch lower. **That loss does not show up in the promotion month's report. It shows up in the baseline of every month after it.**

**Finally, the test itself.** A promotion is worth what its net increment over the three months after it, relative to the baseline, is worth at that margin, minus the gross margin given up in discounting and the operating cost. **If that number comes out negative, the promotion did not fail. It spent next year's business early.**
**Data**: All figures come from the Eurostat monthly turnover and volume of sales in wholesale and retail trade, dataset `sts_trtu_m`. Scope is the EU27 (EU27_2020), the index base is 2021=100, and two versions are used: seasonally and calendar adjusted (SCA), and calendar adjusted but not seasonally adjusted (CA). The three categories map to NACE codes G476 (cultural and entertainment goods), G474 (information and communication equipment) and G472 (food, beverages and tobacco). The baseline block is the three-month average from August to October; the observation block is the three-month average of December, January and February. The net increment after averaging four months is computed as (promotion month above baseline + 3 × observation window deviation from baseline) ÷ 4. The adjusted month-on-month figures come from the PCH_PRE series in the same dataset; the unadjusted December month-on-month figure is derived by dividing the calendar-adjusted index month by month. Data runs through October 2025, the last month of the adjusted index series; November 2025 and later have not been published. February 2020 through 2021 was hit by the pandemic and produced extreme swings in every category, and no comparison here uses that period. No paid data source was used.

**Reproduce**: `x dbnomics data Eurostat/sts_trtu_m/M.NETTUR.G476.SCA.I21.EU27_2020` (swap G476 for G474 or G472 for the other two categories) returns the adjusted index, `x dbnomics data Eurostat/sts_trtu_m/M.NETTUR.G476.SCA.PCH_PRE.EU27_2020` the adjusted month-on-month, and `x dbnomics data Eurostat/sts_trtu_m/M.NETTUR.G476.CA.I21.EU27_2020` the calendar-adjusted index. The numbers in the charts are direct readings and differences of these three series; change the series inside the d= parameter of the link to swap in your own data.
