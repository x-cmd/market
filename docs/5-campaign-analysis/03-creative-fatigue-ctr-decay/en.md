# Your creative is not getting worse. Your audience is getting tired of it.

**Same image, same budget, same channel, and the CTR still slides down. The creative did not get worse and the traffic did not get worse. The same people have seen it too many times, and their attention is spent.**

**This is the measured curve from Yahoo Gemini native advertising. A person's first repeat exposure to the same ad set leaves CTR at 82% of the first impression. By the seventh time, CTR has fallen to 50%.**

**One line to take away: when CTR drops below half of what the creative delivered in its first week, that is the signal to replace it. Not when it drops below some industry average.**

## A CTR that slides down on its own

**The creative did not change, the ad copy did not change, the landing page did not change, and CTR kept falling. That is not an illusion. It was measured.**

Yahoo's data comes from real delivery logs on Yahoo Gemini native advertising, aggregated over a 30-day window and covering billions of impressions. Every impression is bucketed by how many times that user had already seen this campaign, and each bucket is normalized against bucket 0, the users who had never seen it.

**The result: average CTR loses 20% after a single repeat exposure. It loses half after seven.**

**The other half of the finding matters more.** The fatigue curve read out of the model's weight vector declines monotonically with frequency, and the last bucket (v≥50) sits far below the curve's extrapolated value. The people who have seen it a lot do worse than the curve predicts. That curve is credible because it came out of a model running in production for years, not a lab survey. The paper puts it bluntly: fatigue brings a "dramatic drop". That is why Yahoo built frequency into a model feature and let the model learn how much to subtract on each repeat exposure.

## Fatigue is made of repetition

**The raw material of fatigue is repetition. The same person sees the same ad set over and over, and their attention gets shaved down layer by layer.**

**"Attention gets shaved down" needs unpacking.** It does not mean the user got annoyed and stopped looking, because then there would be no CTR drop to measure. What happens is that the eighth time someone scrolls past the same ad in a day, they put less into it than the seventh time. **The industry calls this wear-out: the creative loses effectiveness as exposure count rises.**

**Yahoo's data says most of that decay happens in the first three to five exposures.** 47% of impressions came from people who had never seen the campaign, 10% from people who had seen it once, and only 6% from people who had seen it twice. **More than nine in ten impressions fall in the "not seen much" buckets, so the decay lands on a narrow slice of the audience.**

**Fatigue does not care about gender. It cares about age.** Yahoo's segment cuts show the male and female curves are nearly identical, while users of unknown gender tolerate far more. **After five exposures, the 50-to-60 group shows more than twice the fatigue of the 20-to-30 group.** So the fatigue curve is not one line: the same creative can still be worth running for a young audience and already be due for replacement among older ones.

**Fatigue also spreads between creatives.** A Japanese paper from CyberAgent computes a fatigue score by multiplying how many times a user has seen a creative by how similar that creative is to the others. Their example: a user who saw the blue creative 3 times and the yellow one once, where blue and yellow have a similarity of 0.39, faces a fatigue score of 3 + 0.39 ≈ 3.4 the next time the blue creative comes around. **A replacement does not have to be a completely different piece. Changing the dominant color or swapping the image can be enough.**

## The curve drops hard and then flattens

**It drops hard and then flattens. That detail matters, because plenty of people explain CTR declines with the wrong curve.**

**Look at the shape.** The first repeat exposure costs 18%. From the second time to the seventh, it slides from 69% down to 50%. After that it goes essentially flat, sitting near 43% at the 24th exposure. The paper's own wording is that the rate of CTR decline decreases as exposure count rises.

**That shape is the opposite of a saturation curve.** A saturation curve starts slow, speeds up, and settles toward a ceiling; it fits scenarios where an audience is being covered progressively and each increment buys less. **The fatigue curve runs the other way: steep first, flat later.** Mix the two up and the conclusion inverts. Explain a CTR drop with a saturation curve and you get "we are about to hit the ceiling, more spend is pointless." Explain it with a fatigue curve and you get "the worst part is over." **The reality is that a creative loses the most in its first few exposures.**

**One comparison shows how steep fatigue really is.** The CyberAgent paper ran a live experiment in a real display ad environment. The old approach predicted a click rate for each candidate creative and picked the top one; the new approach folds the fatigue score into the prediction and swaps in a different creative when fatigue is high. ## Replace the creative when CTR falls below half of its first week

**One rule: when CTR drops below half of what that creative delivered in its first week, replace it. Not when it hits an industry average. Not when it feels off.**

**Those three words, "its first week," carry the whole rule.** It means the creative's own first-week CTR. The creative's baseline quality, its category appeal, and its fit with the audience are all baked into that number. **Using your own first week as the denominator measures how much punch is left in this creative. Using an industry average measures how this creative compares with competitors. Those are different questions.**

**Why half, and not "swap it after a 20% drop"?** Because fatigue does its damage in the first few exposures and then flattens. **Swap too early and you keep replacing creatives during the steepest part of the curve, never reaching the flat stretch where a creative has settled in. That low-frequency stretch is also where conversion quality tends to be highest.**

**Before replacing, separate a tired creative from a tired audience.** Fatigue means one person got sick of one image, so the damage is layered. **A new creative gets treated as a new face by the people who saw the old one many times, and CTR comes back.** If CTR fell because targeting changed, the season turned, the landing page broke, or competition got heavier, a new creative will not fix it. **Swap a hundred creatives and the curve still slides down.** A workable check: if average exposure frequency per person is about the same as last week and CTR still fell by half, that is not fatigue.

**One more caution: when CTR has fallen by half, do not look only at CTR.** The CyberAgent paper has a counterintuitive finding: **CTR declines monotonically with fatigue, but conversion rate does not.** Their explanation: users who click the ad but do not go on to play the game are the ones who have not been persuaded yet. **Judging a creative on CTR alone can retire it while its conversion rate is still steady.**

**The setup.** An e-commerce brand runs a 30-second video, creative A, in a feed channel. Targeting is narrow, weekly budget is 100,000 yuan, CPM is 40 yuan, so 2.5 million impressions per week. Because the audience is narrow, each person averages 4 impressions per week. Creative A's first-week CTR is 1.80%.

**Put the Yahoo curve on it. Each week adds 4 to each person's cumulative exposure:**

| Week | Cumulative exposure per person | CTR | Clicks | Cost per click | Relative to week 1 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Week 1 | 4 | 1.246% | 31,149 | 3.21 yuan | 100% |
| Week 2 | 8 | 1.091% | 27,262 | 3.67 yuan | 87% |
| Week 3 | 12 | 1.036% | 25,894 | 3.86 yuan | 83% |
| Week 4 | 16 | 1.007% | 25,182 | 3.97 yuan | 81% |
| Week 5 | 20 | 0.981% | 24,525 | 4.08 yuan | 79% |
| Week 6 | 24 | 0.948% | 23,704 | 4.22 yuan | 76% |

**Six weeks cost 600,000 yuan and bought 158,000 clicks. Cost per click went from 3.21 yuan to 4.22 yuan, a 31% increase.**

**It does not look catastrophic, but there is a problem hiding inside it.** In weeks 4 through 6, CTR sits at 81%, 79% and 76% of week 1. Swap in a new creative B, reset the frequency counter, and CTR goes back to 1.246%, 1.091% and 1.036%. **Those three weeks would have produced 10,894 more clicks.**

**What does that cost? The production cost of one new creative.** If shooting and editing a 30-second video runs 20,000 yuan, then 10,894 extra clicks cost 20000 ÷ 10894 = 1.84 yuan each. **That is less than half the roughly 4 yuan per click you pay by staying with the tired creative.** But this example hides an even more expensive problem: creative B will fatigue too once it goes live. Replacing a creative is not the end. It pushes the same problem a few weeks down the road.

## Schedule by creative lifespan, not by one creative to the end

**Read those six weeks backwards and you get a lifespan curve to schedule against.**

**The key move is converting frequency into weeks.** In that example each person sees the creative 4 times a week, so the fatigue inflection point at "the 7th exposure" lands in week 2. With wider targeting and 1 impression per person per week, it lands in week 7. With very narrow targeting and 8 impressions per person per week, it arrives in under a week. **So "how many weeks does a creative last" has no single answer. It depends on your frequency.**

**Three things need scheduling. First, the expected lifespan of a creative.** Take average weekly impressions per person, multiply by the target frequency, and divide by 7. That gives the number of weeks the creative holds up.

**Second, the lead time for the replacement.** The steepest part of fatigue happens in the first four weeks, so **the new creative should go live before the old one falls to half. You want them to hand off to each other, not to wait for the halfway mark and then react.** The 76% to 81% stretch in that example is the window where an early replacement takes effect immediately.

**Third, the size of the creative library.** Run the lifespan curve backwards: **the library has to cover the rotation period times the number of creatives running at once.** If one creative lasts four weeks and three are running concurrently, that means a library of at least 12. If the library only holds four, week 5 starts recycling the same batch, which pushes fatigue up across the whole account.

**One homework item is not worth skipping: do not make the new creative look too much like the old one.** **If the new creative only changes the copy and keeps the same scene, fatigue carries over and the replacement changes nothing.** The Japanese paper weights text similarity at three times the weight of image similarity when computing fatigue.

**Last thing about frequency capping: capping and replacing are two ends of the same lever.** In Yahoo's test, turning frequency into a model feature (soft capping) bought a 7.3% CPM gain, and removing hard capping rules while keeping only soft capping added another 3.6%. **Frequency capping works on "show this person fewer times." Replacing creatives works on "show this person something they have not seen too many times."**

**Data**: The fatigue curve comes from Eshel, Shahar, Singer and Zlotnik, "Soft Frequency Capping for Improved Ad Click Prediction in Yahoo Gemini Native" (CIKM 2020, arXiv:2312.05052), real Yahoo Gemini native delivery data aggregated over a 30-day window covering billions of impressions. The normalized CTR curve was read point by point off the axis ticks in Figure 3 of that paper (first impression = 100) and matches the text description (CTR falls 20% after the first repeat exposure, about 50% after the seventh, the rate of decline decreases with frequency, and there is an anomalous jump at v=25 caused by the [25:∞) binning). The impression shares of 47%/10%/6%, the gender and age segment findings, the 7.3% CPM gain from soft frequency capping (3.5% for the mixed bucket, 3.6% after removing hard capping), and the monotonic decline of the frequency weight vector are all quoted from the body of that paper. The creative selection experiment comes from Moriwaki, Fujita, Yasui and Hoshino, "Fatigue-Aware Ad Creative Selection" (arXiv:1908.08936, ACM CCS 2019), a one-week live A/B test in CyberAgent's production environment: 1,097,261 impressions for the fatigue-aware algorithm, 1,081,393 for the baseline and 1,087,931 for random, with CTR relative to random of 1.08 / 1.04 / 1.00, CVR of 1.12 / 1.10 / 1.00, and per-campaign CTR of 1.21/0.95/1.00, 1.04/1.03/1.00 and 1.32/1.11/1.00. The fatigue score of 3.39 in the worked example and the 24-hour history window are also from that paper. In the six-week example, the CPM of 40, the weekly budget of 100,000 yuan, the 20,000 yuan creative cost and the first-week CTR of 1.80% are demonstration figures built on public benchmarks, not real campaign data.
