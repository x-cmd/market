# Revenue minus ad spend is not profit

"We spent 290,000 on ads this month and booked 900,000 in sales. Subtract the ads and we still have 610,000."

**That 610,000 is not profit.** The store actually lost 112,240 last month.

Nothing is miscalculated. Only two of the three numbers were counted. Revenue minus spend tells you what is left to pay for goods, shipping, and people — not what you earned. What you earned is what remains after the third number comes off.

**Revenue minus ad spend only tells you what is available to pay the remaining costs.** To know whether you earned anything, all three accounts have to sit on one table.

## Good-looking turnover can hide no profit at all

"Revenue 12.46 billion dollars, gross profit 3.77 billion, gross margin 30%." The numbers are right — Wayfair's gross margin in its 2025 filings was 30.2%. But many stores report turnover rather than margin.

Turnover has a first flaw: some of it is other people's money. National statistics call the total value a business handles **gross output**, including goods bought in from upstream suppliers — a 300 yuan T-shirt gets counted at the factory, the wholesaler, and the retailer. The figure matching "what did I keep myself" is **value added**: output minus the inputs bought in.

US retail handled 2.95 trillion dollars in 2025, but only 1.81 trillion was value the industry created itself. The other 61.5% was goods and services bought in from others. EU wholesale and retail sat at 52.8% in 2022. Applied to one store this becomes **gross margin**: sell 100 yuan, subtract the purchase price, and the rest covers ads, shipping, and payroll.

Turnover has a second flaw: it ignores returns. Part of the booked amount gets refunded later. The money comes back, but the return shipping is yours and the goods have to be shelved again. A refund is not negative revenue — it is a slice of revenue that comes off first. A DTC store reporting "monthly turnover 900,000" may have net revenue of 828,000.

Wayfair's 2025 gross profit was 3.765 billion dollars against 1.425 billion of ad spend, so ads took 37.8% of gross profit. Chewy's fiscal 2025 (ending 2026-02-01) showed 3.754 billion of gross profit against 825 million of marketing spend, or 22.0%. These two compare; turnover does not.

## Three accounts have to be read together

**Revenue, spend, cost. Drop one and what you compute is not profit.** Spend is what you buy: ads, platform commissions, logistics, payment processing. Cost is what the business itself carries: goods for resale, warehouse rent, customer service, returns handling. Spend is negotiable; cost is the price of doing this business at all.

### Revenue

Booked sales are not net revenue. Net revenue = booked sales − refunds. On e-commerce the two routinely differ by 8% to 10%, further in peak season when more orders come back. And order time is not payment time: cash on delivery, pre-authorisation, and seven-day no-questions returns separate "the sale closed" from "the money arrived". The most common reconciliation error is comparing the platform dashboard against the bank statement, and it never matches.

### Spend

Ad spend is one line inside spend. Platform commission runs 5% to 15% of booked sales per order — higher than many people expect. Payment processing runs 0.3 to 0.6 dollars per order. Logistics, peak surcharges, and returns handling each have their own number. Added together, they often exceed ad spend.

Watching only ad ROI while treating commission and logistics as "costs every store pays anyway" produces a fake account. Fixed costs get left out too: warehouse rent, operators, support agents, product selection. Spread across orders, they tell you how healthy a store is: at 120,000 a month, 600 orders carry 200 each and 1,500 carry 80 each.

### Cost

This line is goods, fulfilment, and after-sales: purchase price plus shrinkage, the people picking and packing, the boxes, courier fees, support time and return shipping.

## Getting all three onto one table

**Same year, same month, same definition — align those three and the table means something.**

The time window has to match. The most common mistake is comparing April's ad spend against March-and-April revenue. Ads bill by click day, platforms pay by order settlement day, and a few days of difference puts money in a different month. Pick one full calendar month, pull the ad bill and the order settlements into the same table, and check first whether total revenue reconciles with the bank statement. If it does not, chase refunds and unsettled payouts.

The definitions have to match too. Inside one row, "revenue" cannot be booked sales on the left and net revenue on the right; "ad spend" cannot be account burn on one side and media cost on the other. The first row of the table is not a title — it is a definition note: for every line, write down where the number comes from and which day it is booked against. When the definitions do not line up, leave the cell blank rather than filling in a rough figure. Blank means "not checked yet"; an estimate means "checked but uncertain".

**Ad spend as a share of gross profit beats ROAS.** ROAS says how many yuan of booked sales one yuan of ad spend returned, and it flatters itself whenever revenue rises. Ad spend as a share of gross profit says how much of the budget for everything else has been eaten.

## A store whose gross profit got eaten by ad spend

The store below is a worked example combining the common cases. It sells pet supplies at 400 yuan per order, 42% gross margin.

Booked sales rose 50%. Gross profit rose 43.7%. Net profit went from +21,920 to −112,240. Report only booked sales and this reads as a company taking off.

Ad spend went from 100,000 to 290,000, up 190%, while revenue grew only 50%. Ad spend per order went from 67 yuan to 129 yuan. The extra 62 yuan splits into more expensive auctions and buying volume in costlier places. More telling still, the extra money bought worse orders than the money before it: the audience overlapped with itself, part of the new customers had never encountered the brand, and the rest were existing customers who would have found the store through organic search anyway.

Spread across orders, the story is sharper: gross profit per order fell from 161 to 155, driven by the refund rate going from 4% to 8%. Ad spend per order went from 67 to 129, total spend per order from 147 to 204, net profit per order from earning 15 to losing 50 — and volume still grew 50%, so the extra 750 orders each widened the loss.

The refund rate rose from 4% to 8%, worth 48,000. It appears in neither sales nor ad spend — it hides in the gap between booked sales and money received. Whether the cause was "buyers did not understand the sizing" or "the ads reached the wrong people", the two look identical here.

The cost is a net loss of 112,240, plus cash locked up in returns and payment terms, plus extra inventory in the warehouse. The most expensive part is not the money lost but the decisions made on the wrong numbers. The problem is not that ads got expensive — that is an industry matter. It is that nobody put these three numbers on one table, so "scaling up" looked like chasing a 900,000 number when it was pouring money into a pool losing 50 yuan per order.

## Revenue minus spend is not profit, and cost comes off as well

"Revenue minus ad spend still leaves 610,000" — that figure is **contribution margin**, and it answers only whether an individual order is worth making. It cannot answer whether the store earns anything, because 48,000 of goods, 45,000 of commission, 30,000 of shipping, and 75,000 of payroll are still not subtracted. Subtract all three and what is left is what was earned.

Put all three accounts on one table, same time window, same definitions. Over the same period, Wayfair's ad spend was 37.8% of gross profit and Chewy's was 22.0%. Those are the numbers worth comparing across companies.
