# Revenue minus ad spend is not profit

"We spent 290,000 on ads this month and booked 900,000 in sales. Subtract the ads and 610,000 is left."

**That 610,000 is not profit.** The store lost 112,240 last month.

The numbers are right. Only two were counted. Revenue minus spend tells you what is left for goods, shipping, and people, not what you earned.

## Good-looking turnover can hide no profit at all

Wayfair's 2025 gross margin was 30.2%, on revenue of 12.46 billion dollars and gross profit of 3.77 billion. But plenty of stores report **turnover** instead.

Turnover has a first flaw: some of it is other people's money. National statistics call the total value a business handles **gross output**, and it includes goods bought in. A 300 yuan T-shirt gets counted at the factory, the wholesaler, and the retailer. The figure matching "what did I keep myself" is **value added**: output minus the inputs bought in.

US retail handled 2.95 trillion dollars in 2025, and only 1.81 trillion of that was value the industry created itself. The other 61.5% was goods and services bought in. EU wholesale and retail sat at 52.8% in 2022. For one store this becomes **gross margin**: sell 100 yuan, subtract the purchase price, and the rest pays for ads and freight.

Turnover has a second flaw: it ignores returns. Part of the booked amount gets refunded later: the money comes back, but the return shipping is yours and the goods go back on the shelf. A refund is not negative revenue, it is a slice of revenue that comes off first. A store reporting "turnover 900,000" may have net revenue of 828,000.

Wayfair's 2025 gross profit was 3.765 billion dollars against 1.425 billion of ad spend, so ads took 37.8% of gross profit. Chewy's fiscal 2025 (ending 2026-02-01) showed 3.754 billion of gross profit against 825 million of marketing spend, or 22.0%. These two compare across companies. Turnover does not.

## Three accounts have to be read together

**Revenue, spend, cost. Drop one and what you compute is not profit.** Spend is what you buy: ads, platform commissions, logistics, payment processing. Cost is what the business carries: goods for resale, warehouse rent, customer service, returns handling. Spend can be negotiated; cost is the price of doing the business.

### Revenue

Booked sales are not net revenue. Net revenue = booked sales − refunds, and on e-commerce the two routinely differ by 8% to 10%. Order time is not payment time either: cash on delivery and seven-day no-questions returns pull "the sale closed" away from "the money arrived".

### Spend

Ad spend is one line inside spend. Platform commission runs 5% to 15% of booked sales per order, higher than many people expect. Logistics and returns handling added together often exceed ad spend. Watching only ad ROI while calling commission and logistics "costs every store pays anyway" produces a fake account.

### Cost

Goods, fulfilment, and after-sales: purchase price plus shrinkage, the people picking and packing, the boxes, courier fees, and return shipping.

One conversion beats the rest: spread everything across orders. Gross profit per order is the most you can spend on everything else. Subtract ad spend, commission, and logistics per order and you get net profit per order. Below zero, every extra order loses money.

## Getting all three onto one table

**Same year, same month, same definition. Align those three and the table means something.**

The time window has to match. The most common mistake is comparing April's ad spend against March-and-April revenue. Ads bill by click day, platforms pay by order settlement day, and a few days of difference lands in a different month. Pick one calendar month, pull the ad bill and settlements into one table, then check the total against the bank statement.

The definitions have to match too. Inside one row, "revenue" cannot be booked sales on the left and net revenue on the right, and "ad spend" cannot be account burn on one side and media cost on the other. The first row of the table is not a title but a definition note: for every line, say where the number comes from and which day it books against. When the definitions do not line up, leave the cell blank rather than filling in a rough figure. Blank means "not checked yet"; an estimate means "checked but uncertain".

Track revenue and spend as separate lines, each against the previous period. **Ad spend as a share of gross profit beats ROAS.** ROAS says how many yuan of booked sales one yuan of ad spend returned, and it flatters itself whenever revenue rises. Ad spend as a share of gross profit says how much budget everything else lost.

## A store whose gross profit got eaten by ad spend

The store below is a worked example combining the common cases. It sells pet supplies at 400 yuan per order, 42% gross margin.

| Booked sales | 600,000 | 900,000 |

| Net revenue | 576,000 | 828,000 |
| Cost of goods | −334,080 | −480,240 |
| **Gross profit** | **241,920** | **347,760** |
| Ad spend | −100,000 | −290,000 |
| Platform commission | −30,000 | −45,000 |

Booked sales rose 50%. Gross profit rose 43.7%. Net profit went from +21,920 to −112,240. Report only booked sales and this reads as a company taking off.

Ad spend went from 100,000 to 290,000, up 190%, while revenue grew only 50%. Ad spend per order went from 67 yuan to 129 yuan. The extra 62 yuan splits into more expensive auctions and buying volume in costlier places. More telling still, the extra money bought worse orders: the audience overlapped with itself, part of the new customers had never heard of the brand, and the rest were existing customers.

Spread across orders, the story is sharper: gross profit per order fell from 161 to 155, total spend per order went from 147 to 204, net profit per order from earning 15 to losing 50. Volume still grew 50%, so the extra 750 orders each widened the loss.

The 6 yuan lost per order came from the refund rate going from 4% to 8%, worth 48,000. It appears in neither sales nor ad spend, only in the gap between booked sales and money received. Whether the cause was "buyers did not understand the sizing" or "the ads reached the wrong people", the two look identical here.

The cost is a net loss of 112,240, plus cash locked up in returns and payment terms, plus extra inventory in the warehouse. The most expensive part is not the money lost but the decisions made on the wrong numbers. Ads getting expensive is an industry matter. The problem is that nobody put these three numbers on one table, so "scaling up" looked like chasing a 900,000 number while it poured money into a pool losing 50 yuan an order.

## Revenue minus spend is not profit, and cost comes off as well

"Revenue minus ad spend still leaves 610,000" is **contribution margin**. It answers only whether a single order is worth making, not whether the store earns anything: 48,000 of goods, 45,000 of commission, 30,000 of logistics, and 95,000 of payroll and after-sales are still not subtracted.

Put the definition next to the number whenever you report one: "Booked sales 900,000" and "net revenue 828,000" differ by 72,000, and that gap is not small. Spread all spend across orders rather than watching ad ROI alone. If only ads are counted, "the campaign worked" can be a hallucination.

Look at the two half-years of that store one more time: the numbers went up, and the business lost money.
