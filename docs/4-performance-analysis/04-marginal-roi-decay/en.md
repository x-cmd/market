# Your average ROI says nothing about whether the next dollar pays

**One channel's average ROI has sat at 4.0 for months. That number describes how the whole pot of money performed in the past. It says nothing about what the next pot brings back.** The two often move in opposite directions.

**Marginal ROI (also called marginal return) is the return on the last dollar itself:** spend 1 more, get back so many. To find your channel's next number, slice the spend up and check what each slice returned.

## A good average does not mean you can spend more

Here is a worked example: one month of search advertising for an e-commerce brand (**a worked example, not a real company's data**). Spend is split into bands of 100,000, each credited with its own return:

| Spend band | Return from band | Marginal ROI |
|---|---|---|
| 0–100,000 | 660,000 | 6.6 |
| 100,000–200,000 | 540,000 | 5.4 |
| 200,000–300,000 | 430,000 | 4.3 |
| 300,000–400,000 | 330,000 | 3.3 |
| 400,000–500,000 | 240,000 | 2.4 |
| 500,000–600,000 | 160,000 | 1.6 |
| 600,000–700,000 | 90,000 | 0.9 |
| 700,000–800,000 | 40,000 | 0.4 |

**The distance between the two lines is the whole question.** Cumulative average ROI slides from 6.6 down to 3.11, half of what it was. Marginal ROI falls from 6.6 to 0.4, a drop of 94%. The average spreads the money won in the early bands across every dollar, so it always looks respectable.

**Average ROI also has a floor: 1.** While the channel as a whole is profitable, the early bands keep padding the average, so it cannot drop below 1. Marginal ROI has no padding: it can sit below 1, at 0, or below zero. **So "average ROI is still 3.11" never implies "spending more pays."**

## What one more dollar actually brings back

**Marginal ROI has a one-line definition: the money a band brings back, divided by the money that band spent.** The denominator counts only that band. Band 8 spent 100,000 and returned 40,000, so marginal ROI is **0.4x**.

**Marginal return is not incremental return.** Marginal ROI is a bookkeeping figure: this band spent 100,000 more and brought back 40,000 more. But part of that extra 40,000 comes from orders that were going to happen anyway, because the customer would have clicked regardless. That part is **substitution**. eBay's brand-keyword ad experiment is the hardest evidence: the observational number came out above 1400%, while the randomized test measured a true incremental return of **−63%** ([the two ledgers side by side](../../4-performance-analysis/03-platform-metrics-vs-incremental-metrics/en.md)).

## How to cut the curve

**A marginal curve is not drawn. It is cut.** Three steps.

**First, split the spend into bands of equal width.** Wide bands give steadier numbers. Narrow ones pin down the turning point but pick up more noise. Start with six to eight.

**Second, match each band with a return.** This is the hard part, because **ad effects lag**: money spent today keeps working days later. Match "this band's spend" against "today's sales" and the curve jumps with normal sales swings. The fix is **to align time before taking any difference**: spread each unit of spend along its own decay shape, then read every band's return over that same window ([adstock decay](../../8-analysis-toolbox/04-adstock-decay/en.md)).

**Third, take the difference.** Marginal ROI = return in band ÷ spend in band. Join the bands and you have the curve.

## Where the next dollar stops paying

**Find where the line crosses 1.0.** That is the turning point. Above 1, the band brings back more than it costs. Below 1, adding it loses money. At 0, it is pure waste.

**Back to the worked example: band 7 has a marginal ROI of 0.9 and band 6 has 1.6. The turning point sits between them, so this channel's spend stops growing around 600,000.**

**The line crosses the break-even line of 1.0 exactly where spend reaches 2.0k.** Below that point every extra unit brings back more than one unit; above it, the return falls fast to 0.6, 0.38, 0.17.

**Watch the opening stretch: at α=2 the marginal return does not fall straight away.** It climbs from 2.77 to 4.0 before turning down, and the larger α is, the more pronounced that early climb gets, with α=3 peaking near 794. That is a property of the curve, not a plotting error. The α=1 version declines steadily, its peak right at x=0.

One algebraic property is worth keeping: **reaching 90% of the ceiling takes 9 times the saturation point** (at α=1), 3 times at α=2, 2.1 times at α=3. **"One more push and we'll hit the top" usually means you are still far from it.** (See [Hill saturation curve](../../8-analysis-toolbox/05-hill-saturation-curve/en.md))

**The chart plots efficiency. The share has to be worked out separately.** Marketing spend as a share of revenue holds between 6.4% and 6.8% from FY2022 onward, barely moving (FY2020 was 7.18%, FY2021 was 6.90%, then it settles into that range). Yet revenue returned per 1 of marketing spend falls from 15.58x in FY2022 to 14.75x in FY2024. The share holds because numerator and denominator grow together; efficiency falls because the extra money buys dearer, thinner volume.

**There is harder evidence in the filings.** From FY2022 to FY2023, marketing expense rose from 649,400,000 to 742,460,000, and revenue rose from 10,119,000,000 to 11,147,700,000. The extra 93,060,000 brought back 1,028,700,000, a marginal return of 11.06x, under that year's 15.02x average. **One caveat: this is a mixed figure from public filings, not banded marginal returns**, so it points in a direction rather than setting a budget.

**Second, the average mixes in something else.** Brand organic search volume gets booked against paid search, and ROI looks stable. Turn paid brand keywords off and organic search picks up the clicks, 99.5% of them in eBay's experiment, with only 0.5% genuinely lost.

**Third, the average mixes new customers with returning ones.** New customers bring high order values and low repeat rates, so the money spent to win them lifts average ROI. They do not come back the next year, and the second year's average ROI collapses. **Read the curve across a cohort's whole lifecycle, not one month.**

**One more thing makes the curve unreliable: too little data.** The last bands hold only a handful of impressions and orders, so the marginal ROI swings wildly. Interval width scales with the square root of sample size, so 40 times the sample means 6 times the width ([sample size and interval half-width](../../8-analysis-toolbox/08-confidence-intervals-and-fit/en.md)). **The band next to the turning point is the one you have estimated worst.**

## Decide on the last dollar, not the total

**Whether to spend more comes down to the last dollar, not the average.** The turning point sits between bands 6 and 7, and that is the ceiling.

**This curve is worth more than an ROI ranking.** A ranking tells you which channel earned the most in the past, not who gets the next dollar. That answer lives in [the highest-ROI channel is not the one that deserves the most money](../../4-performance-analysis/02-roi-ranking-vs-budget-allocation/en.md), and where two channels have equal marginal return, the money goes to both, per the [equal-marginal principle](../../8-analysis-toolbox/07-equal-marginal-returns-principle/en.md).

**One line to keep: average ROI answers "how did the past money perform?" Marginal ROI answers "does the next dollar still pay back?"**
