# One more dollar on that channel, will it still earn its keep

"This channel averages 4.0x ROI. It's stable. Let's add another 200,000 next month."

Every number in that sentence is right. The "another 200,000" has no basis at all.

**Average ROI describes how one whole pot of money performed in the past. It says nothing about what the next pot will bring back.** Slice the spend into pieces and look at what each piece returned.

**Marginal ROI — the return on the very last dollar — is what the money you add next returns.** The two numbers move in opposite directions.

## A good average tells you nothing about the next dollar

Take a worked example: one month of search advertising for an e-commerce brand (**worked example, not a real company's data**). Spend is split into bands of 100,000, each credited with its own return:

| Spend band | Spend in band | Return from band | Marginal ROI |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0–100,000 | 100,000 | 660,000 | 6.6 |
| 100,000–200,000 | 100,000 | 540,000 | 5.4 |
| 200,000–300,000 | 100,000 | 430,000 | 4.3 |
| 300,000–400,000 | 100,000 | 330,000 | 3.3 |
| 400,000–500,000 | 100,000 | 240,000 | 2.4 |
| 500,000–600,000 | 100,000 | 160,000 | 1.6 |
| 600,000–700,000 | 100,000 | 90,000 | 0.9 |
| 700,000–800,000 | 100,000 | 40,000 | 0.4 |

**The gap between the two lines is the whole answer.** Cumulative average ROI slides from 6.6 down to 3.11, a halving. Marginal ROI falls from 6.6 to 0.4, a drop of 94%. The average spreads the money won early across every dollar, so it always looks respectable.

**Average ROI also has a floor: 1.** While the channel is profitable overall, the average can never fall below 1, because profit from the early bands cushions it. Marginal ROI has no cushion: it can go below 1, reach 0, turn negative. **So "average ROI is still 3.11" never implies "adding money is worth it."**

## What one more dollar actually buys

**Marginal ROI has a one-line definition: the money returned by a band, divided by the money spent in that band.** Band 8 spent 100,000 and returned 40,000, so marginal ROI is **0.4x**.

**Marginal return is not incremental return.** Marginal ROI is a bookkeeping number — this band spent 100,000 more and got 40,000 more back. But part of that extra 40,000 comes from orders that would have happened anyway (the customer clicked regardless). That part is **substitution**. eBay's brand-keyword ad experiment is the hardest evidence: the observational number came out above 1400%, while the randomized test measured a true incremental return of **−63%** ([the two ledgers side by side](../../4-performance-analysis/03-platform-metrics-vs-incremental-metrics/en.md)).

## How to build the curve

**A marginal curve is not drawn. It is cut.** Three steps.

**Step one: split the spend into equal-width bands.** Wider bands give steadier numbers; narrower ones pin down the turning point but pick up more noise. Start with six to eight.

**Step two: match each band with a return.** The difficulty: **ad effects take time**, so money spent today keeps working days later. Line up "this band's spend" against "this day's sales" and the curve jumps around with sales swings. The fix is **to align time before taking any difference**: spread each unit of spend along its decay shape, then read each band's return over the same window ([adstock decay](../../8-analysis-toolbox/04-adstock-decay/en.md)).

**Step three: take the difference.** Marginal ROI = return in band ÷ spend in band. Join the bands and you have the curve.

## Where the extra money stops paying

**Find where the line crosses 1.0.** Above 1, the band returns more than it costs. Below 1, adding it loses money. At 0, pure waste.

**Back to the worked example: band 7 has a marginal ROI of 0.9, band 6 has 1.6. The turning point sits between them, so this channel's spend stops growing around 600,000.**

A real saturation curve shows it better. The Hill curve (a mathematical curve with a fixed shape, parameterized by a saturation point k and a steepness α) gives this marginal return:

**Read the two lines.** Marketing spend as a share of revenue holds between 6.4% and 6.8%, barely moving. But revenue returned per 1 of marketing spend falls from 15.58x in FY2022 to 14.75x in FY2024. The share holds because numerator and denominator grow together; efficiency falls because the extra money buys thinner volume.

**Harder evidence in the filings.** From FY2022 to FY2023, marketing expense rose from 649,400,000 to 742,460,000 while revenue rose from 10,119,000,000 to 11,147,700,000. The extra 93,060,000 brought back 1,028,700,000 — a marginal return of 11.06x, under that year's 15.02x average.

**One caveat: this is a mixed accounting from public filings, not banded marginal returns.** **Each channel needs its own curve, from its own data.**

**The second way: other things get mixed into the average.** Brand organic search volume gets booked to paid search, and ROI looks stable. Turn paid brand keywords off and organic search picks up the clicks — in eBay's experiment, 99.5% of them.

**The third way: new and returning customers get mixed together.** New customers have high order values and low repeat rates, so the money spent to win them pulls the average ROI up. They do not come back the next year, and the second year's average ROI collapses. **Read the curve over a cohort lifecycle, not a month.**

**One more thing makes the curve untrustworthy: too little data.** The last bands hold only a handful of impressions and orders, so the marginal ROI swings wildly. Interval width scales with the square root of sample size: 40 times the sample means 6 times the interval width ([sample size and interval half-width](../../8-analysis-toolbox/08-confidence-intervals-and-fit/en.md)). **The band nearest the turning point is the least accurate.**

## Decide on the last dollar, not the total

**Take the channel that matters most, draw its marginal curve, read off the turning point, and stop the budget there.** The ceiling sits between bands 6 and 7.

**The curve is worth more than an ROI ranking.** A ranking tells you which channel earned the most in the past, not who gets the next dollar — see [the highest-ROI channel is not the one that deserves the most money](../../4-performance-analysis/02-roi-ranking-vs-budget-allocation/en.md) and [the equal-marginal principle](../../8-analysis-toolbox/07-equal-marginal-returns-principle/en.md).
