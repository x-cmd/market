# A 1% lift three weeks in is not a verdict

**Three weeks after a campaign ends, sales are up 1%. Do you keep spending?**

Most managers cut. That number might be early.

**A promotion's effect arrives in batches.** Week one catches the buyers already going to order, week three the ones who shopped around first, week eight the customer who replaced the TV and the sofa at once. **A three-week window is a receipt that arrived late.**

**An effect that has not shown up yet and an effect that never happened are two different things. Separate them by fixing the window first.**

## The report shows a running total, not a campaign result

**The number on a dashboard is "everything that has happened up to now," not "what this campaign produced."** **US furniture and home furnishings stores, 2023, implied sales volume (sales divided by the price index): September 94.9, October 92.4, November 96.3.** **Nothing changed, and the number moved 4% on its own.**

**That is the size of ordinary noise.** A campaign with a real 3% lift would land in the tail of this swing, indistinguishable from nothing at all. But that is not the main problem. Even at a 30% lift, week three does not show 30%. **It shows the part that has already arrived.** "Up 1% in week three" can mean the effect was small, or that it has not all landed — **the reading cannot tell these apart.**

## Three reasons the effect arrives late

**An ad's effect does not end the day you stop paying for it. It drags on, for three reasons.**

**The first is the ad's own leftover effect.** When the ads stop, the effect keeps fading, but slowly. **The model for "how much is left each day" is called Adstock**: past spend is discounted day by day and summed up to today, with the discount rate set by a parameter λ, where **a larger λ means a longer tail.**

**The three lines are far apart.** Search with λ = 0.3 has 2.7% left on day 3 and is essentially gone by day 7. Social with λ = 0.7 still has 8.2% on day 7. Television with λ = 0.9 still has 47.8% on day 7 and **10.9% on day 21**.

**The same "week three" is too late for search and too early for television.** One window across all channels produces numbers that do not mean the same thing. For what Adstock means, see [→ Adstock decay: how long an ad's effect drags](../../8-analysis-toolbox/04-adstock-decay/en.md).

**The second is the buyer's decision cycle, which has nothing to do with advertising: buying a sofa takes three weeks, buying a bag of chips does not.** They measure, compare prices, ask the family, wait for a weekend to visit a store. **Furniture, renovation, weddings and B2B procurement sit in that band; snacks and drinks are decided in hours.** **The third is approval:** in B2B a lead clears a budget approval before it becomes an order. **Three weeks in retail and three weeks in B2B are not the same three weeks.**

## A short window turns seasonality into your result

**EU retail turnover (Eurostat, calendar-adjusted): February is 90.1 and December is 106.1 in the same year, a gap of 17.8%.** Nobody managed that. It is Christmas and New Year.

**Run a campaign in December and read December: how much of that 17.8% is yours?** December sits 17.8% above February with or without your ads. The other way around, **a February campaign read in March gets a 13% negative number from the season**, and you may kill a campaign that was working.

**Promotions move demand forward in time the same way.** A big November sale pulls forward the customers who would have bought in December: November spikes, December collapses. **Both readings are wrong.** For how far a promotion moves demand, see [→ Promotions pull demand forward](../../5-campaign-analysis/02-promotion-pulls-demand-forward/en.md).

**A short window has one more cost: it mixes two kinds of error.** Natural noise is ±2%. A delayed effect means "has not arrived yet." **Both look like "below expected," and the right response is the opposite** — one calls for waiting, the other for stopping. Stop when you cannot tell them apart, and waiting has silently become cancellation.

## How to find out whether the campaign worked

**First, put a contemporaneous control next to it.** Not "last month versus this month" but **the same period performance of the group that did not get the ads.** Without one, the difference is not precision — it is whether the claim can stand at all. See [→ What a control group is](../../3-experiment-design/01-what-a-control-group-is/en.md). **The control waits the same amount of time:** in week three, the control's effect has also travelled three weeks.

**Second, look at the time series, not a single point.** **A real effect lifts, holds a plateau, then decays.** At the week-three point alone, you cannot separate "lifted but has not peaked" from "never lifted."

**The same data, three weeks in, has paid out half.** ROI computed on the week-three number is 0.5 times the true value — **cutting there throws away half the effect that has not arrived and lets the other half, already earned, go with it.**

**Third, rule out other things that ran at the same time** — new packaging, another platform, a livestream, a price change. **Without a contemporaneous control, no single chart separates them.** See [→ Two ledgers, side by side](../03-platform-metrics-vs-incremental-metrics/en.md).

## A promotion that only shows up in week three

**A US furniture retailer ran a big promotion in September.** Start with the category's season: implied sales volume in 2023 was 94.9 in September, 92.4 in October, 96.3 in November. **September to October falls 2.7% on its own, because October's demand gets eaten by November's Black Friday.** That is the season, not management.

**Now the campaign.** The incremental units arrive week by week: **88 in week 1, 114 in week 2, 130 in week 3, 151 in week 4, 177 in week 6, 231 in week 8, 260 in week 12.**

**Three weeks in, the owner asks: how is it going?**

**Pull up the dashboard and October is down 2.7% against September.** The season alone accounts for that drop, and the first two weeks of the promotion added 130 units — **but October's natural decline ate part of the increment, and in the dashboard both land in the same cell as the same number.** That is not a real number but what is left after two things are mixed together. The only way to separate them: **compare against a group that got no ads in the same period.** With the season removed, **the promotion is visible in week 1, reaches 130 cumulative units by week 3, and only approaches nine-tenths of its full effect at week 8.** And **October's 2.7% seasonal drop is there the whole time** — neither the campaign's credit nor its fault.

**Now the cost.** Say the campaign cost 260,000. On week 3's 130 units, ROI is 0.8 times — **a loss.** On week 8's 231 units, it is 1.78 times — **a profit.** Same money, same campaign, **two answers, and the only difference is which week you collected the data.**

**An even more expensive mistake is waiting.** Suppose you saw −2.7% in week three and stopped the campaign. **You killed a campaign already shown to work, in a category whose decision cycle had not finished, and wrote off the effect that had not yet arrived.** The mistake is also hard to spot afterwards, because on the dashboard it looks like saved budget.

## Fix the window before you start, not after

**"Wait for the effect" is not a window until it has a number attached.** The effect never fully finishes arriving — the tail of an ad is asymptotic, and a little of it is always still running.

**Set the window from three things. How often the category buys:** three weeks is the floor for furniture and waste for snacks. **How long the effect drags for the channel:** search's tail is three days, television's is weeks, so one window does not fit every channel. **How small an effect you are trying to detect:** separating 3% from 30% differs by 100x in sample size, and the window length scales with it.

**Write the window down on the campaign brief, before the spend starts**, not as a "let's check again in two weeks" added afterwards.

**One last warning: if the window ends and there is still no visible effect, that is also an answer.** Was this never worth running, did someone change something else midway, or was the window set too short for this channel? **But do not extend it indefinitely in the name of caution** — that is turning a decision into no decision.

**An effect that has not shown up yet and an effect that never happened are two different things. Telling them apart takes a window fixed in advance and a control group inside that window.**

**Data**: The EU retail turnover chart uses Eurostat `sts_trtu_m`, series M.NETTUR.G.CA.I15.EU27_2020 (net turnover in wholesale and retail trade, calendar-adjusted), monthly from 2015 to 2023. The seasonal index is computed on this page as each month's average across those nine years divided by the overall average. The furniture figures use BEA `NIUnderlyingDetail-U00500/T05008-M` (sales) and `NIUnderlyingDetail-U00700/T07008-M` (chain-type price index); implied sales volume is computed on this page as sales divided by the price index, not an official published series.
