# Three weeks in, a 1% lift is not a verdict

**The campaign is over. The report says sales are up 1%. Do you spend more, or cut it?**

Most managers cut. The number is probably early.

**A promotion's effect arrives in batches.** Week one brings the buyers already going to order, week three the ones who compared prices first, week eight the customer who replaced the TV and the sofa at once. **Reading the whole campaign off week three is like settling an invoice against a late receipt.**

**An effect that has not shown up yet and an effect that never happened are two different things. Fix the window first.**

## The dashboard shows a running total, not a campaign result

**A number on a dashboard means "everything that has happened so far," not "what this campaign produced."** One time period sits between the two.

**US furniture and home furnishings stores, 2023, implied sales volume (sales divided by the price index): September 94.9, October 92.4, November 96.3.** **Nothing changed, and the number moved 4% on its own.**

**That is the size of ordinary noise.** A real 3% lift lands in the tail of a swing like that. Even at a 30% lift, week three shows only the part that has already arrived. "Up 1% in week three" can mean the effect was small, or that it has not all landed — **the reading cannot tell them apart.**

## Three reasons the effect arrives late

**An ad's effect does not end the day you stop paying for it. It drags on, for three reasons.**

**The first is the ad's own leftover effect.** When the ads stop, the effect keeps fading, but slowly. **The model for "how much is left each day" is called Adstock**: past spend is discounted day by day and summed to today at a rate set by λ, and **a larger λ means a longer tail.**

## A short window turns seasonality into your result

**The most direct cost of a short window is that it reads the season as your effect.** EU retail turnover (Eurostat, calendar-adjusted): February is 90.1 and December is 106.1 in the same year, a gap of 17.8%. Nobody managed that — it is Christmas and New Year.

**Run a campaign in December and read December: how much of that 17.8% is yours?** December sits that much above February whether you spend or not. The other way round, **a February campaign read in March gets a 14.3% negative number from the season**, and you may kill a campaign that was working.

**Promotions move demand forward in time the same way.** A big November sale pulls forward the customers who would have bought in December: November spikes, December collapses. **November looks like a huge success and December like a huge failure. Neither is right.** See [→ Promotions pull demand forward](../../5-campaign-analysis/02-promotion-pulls-demand-forward/en.md).

**A short window also mixes two kinds of error.** Natural noise and a delayed effect both look like "below expected," and the right response is opposite — one calls for waiting, the other for stopping. Stop when you cannot tell them apart, and waiting has become cancellation.

## How to find out whether the campaign worked

**First, put a contemporaneous control next to it.** Not "last month versus this month" but **how the group that did not get the ads performed over the same period.** Without one, the difference is not precision — it is whether the claim can stand at all. See [→ What a control group is](../../3-experiment-design/01-what-a-control-group-is/en.md). **The control waits as long as you do.**

**Second, look at the time series, not a single point.** Put the 8 weeks before the campaign, the campaign, and the 8 weeks after on one axis. **A real effect lifts, holds a plateau, then decays.** At week three you cannot separate "lifted but not peaked" from "never lifted."

**Third, rule out what else ran at the same time** — new packaging, another platform, a livestream, a price change. **Without a contemporaneous control, no single chart separates them.** See [→ Two ledgers](../03-platform-metrics-vs-incremental-metrics/en.md).

**Fourth, the window has to be long enough to see the effect you care about.** **For the same 5% lift, the half-width of the 95% confidence interval is 9% at a sample of 2000, 29% at 200, and 56% at 50.** Interval width scales with the inverse square root of sample size. **Going from 3 weeks to 12 weeks quadruples the sample and halves the interval.** See [→ Confidence intervals](../../8-analysis-toolbox/08-confidence-intervals-and-fit/en.md).

## A promotion that only shows up in week three

**A US furniture retailer ran a big promotion in September.** The category has a long decision cycle and the season is turning. Incremental units arrive week by week: **130 in week 3, 231 in week 8, all 260 by week 12.**

**Three weeks in, the report shows October down 2.6% against September.** The season drops that much on its own, **and that is the season, not management.** October's natural decline eats part of the promotion's increment, and **both land in the same cell of the dashboard as the same number.** To separate them: **compare against a group that got no ads in the same period.** Strip out the season and **the promotion shows up in week 1, reaches 130 cumulative units by week 3, and only approaches nine-tenths of its full effect at week 8.**

**Now the cost.** Say the campaign cost 260,000, and an incremental unit carries 1600 in gross margin. On week 3's 130 units, ROI is 0.8 times — **a loss.** On week 8's 231 units, 1.42 times — **still a loss.** On all 260 units by week 12, 1.6 times. Same money, same campaign, **three answers, and the only difference is which week you collected the data.**

**Suppose you saw −2.6% in week three and stopped the campaign. The cost is worse than missing a gain.** **You killed a campaign already shown to work, in a category whose decision cycle had not finished, and wrote off the effect that had not yet arrived.** It is also hard to spot later: on the dashboard it looks like saved budget.

## Fix the window before you start, not after

**"Wait for the effect" is not a window until it has a number attached.**

**Set the window from three things, not from when you feel like looking.** **How often the category buys:** three weeks is the floor for furniture and waste for snacks. **How long the effect drags for the channel:** search's tail is three days, television's is weeks. **How small an effect you are trying to detect:** separating 3% from 30% differs by 100x in sample size, and the window scales with it. **Write it on the campaign brief, before the spend starts**, not as a "let's check again in two weeks" added later.

**If the window ends and there is still no visible effect, that is also an answer.** Was this never worth running, did someone change something else midway, or was the window too short for this channel? **But do not extend it indefinitely in the name of caution** — that turns a decision into no decision.
