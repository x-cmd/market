# The 260 orders search ads "brought in" are a rule, not a finding

"This campaign drove 320 orders, and 260 of them came from search ads."

The 260 was carved out of a conversion path. **The carving is not a fact. It is a rule.** Change the rule and the same 320 orders split a different way. Neither rule is wrong; each slices the credit differently.

Two things matter here: **the rule attribution splits on, and whether the resulting number can be treated as credit at all.**

## Attribution splits touchpoints, not orders

**Before a buyer converts, they may have met you several times.**

They see your short video in a social feed. They search a category term. They open your landing page. They type your URL and buy.

Four channels, one question: **who gets how much of this order?** What attribution splits is the **touchpoints**. The order is still one order; the credit is a slice cut out of it.

Attribution is always three decisions. The observation window: how long back you look, and which actions count. The order of the touchpoints. The allocation rule: who takes what share. Once those are fixed, every order gets a score. **That score is not the revenue the order produced.**

## Five common rules, one path, five different answers

**The same four-touchpoint path splits five ways.**

Take one buyer who touches four things before converting: a brand keyword search ad, organic search, a social ad, and a direct visit.

Each rule in one line:

- **Last click**: everything to the final click. The default on most platforms.
- **First click**: everything to the first one. Good for seeing who brought the buyer in.
- **Linear**: 25% to each of the four points.
- **Time decay**: the closer a touchpoint sits to the purchase, the more it takes.
- **Position based**: 40% to the first and last, 10% to each of the two in the middle.

Compare the first column with the last. On the same path, the brand keyword ad takes 40% under first click and 0% under last click. The social ad takes 33.3% under time decay and 10% under position based.

**Nobody miscounted. These are five cuts of the same set of orders.** A report saying "search drove 260 orders" is missing half a sentence: **which rule produced the 260.**

### Change the rule and the channel ranking changes with it

How the cake is cut does not change its size, but it does change who takes the biggest piece.

Suppose your conclusion is "brand search produced 30% of conversions, so put more budget into brand keywords." Switch to first click and it may no longer hold. Same data, different rule, different answer — **which means the conclusion came from the rule, not from the data.**

## Three ways attribution breaks down

**Two are structural, and the third is buried in a setting most people never read.**

### Exposure that leaves no record

The buyer scrolls past your ad in the feed and never clicks. In many systems that impression is not recorded, so it does not enter the split. You believe they first saw you on social; the system says they came from search.

**An unrecorded touchpoint is a touchpoint that does not exist.** The pool is smaller, so the remaining channels each take a larger share of it.

### Demand that already existed gets claimed

This one is more expensive. Someone searches your brand name and buys. They were going to buy either way. Last click hands the whole order to the brand keyword ad, because on that path it was the final click.

**The rule did not invent that number. The order genuinely looked like that.** The rule recorded the credit faithfully against the last click.

### One experiment measured the gap

In 2015, eBay ran a large randomized experiment with three economists. They turned off eBay's brand keyword ads on Yahoo and MSN, kept them running on Google as a control, and read the effect on purchases and revenue.

Brand keyword ads had **no measurable short-term benefit**. Of the clicks lost when the brand ads stopped, the paper estimates about **99.5% were picked up by organic search** — the clicks still happened, they stopped being paid for.

**In an attribution report, all 99.5% of that sits under the brand keyword ad's name.** Last click asks who made the last tap, and this buyer was going to tap it anyway. That is not an order an ad created. It is an order that already existed, renamed.

**The 99.5% carries conditions, and it does not travel to other brands.** eBay's own name ranks first in organic search and almost nobody else bids on that keyword, so when organic search takes over the ad slot goes to it. For a brand whose organic result ranks lower, or whose keyword has competitors bidding on it, the rate is much lower. **It measures the clicks lost when an ad gives way to organic search, not the uselessness of brand keyword ads.**

### The window, which nobody checks

**The attribution window.**

The buyer who first sees you on social converts three weeks later. With a 7-day attribution window, that first touch is outside the path. The longer the window, the more points qualify, and the less each channel takes. Same data, 7-day window and 30-day window: two different numbers.

**The N in "channel X drove N orders" is set by three things: how long the window is, which actions count, and which rule allocates.** With none of the three stated, that N cannot be compared with anyone else's N.

## Two uses where attribution earns its keep

**Use one: reallocating inside a single platform.** The question is "which toggle do I turn to spend more effectively," and every channel in that platform's data uses the same rule, so the comparison holds.

**Use two: deciding whether a channel deserves more money.** Attribution is not enough. It counts orders that touched an ad. What you are buying is orders that would disappear if the spend stopped. Those are different numbers, and the size of the gap, channel by channel, has to be measured against a control.

For how to set that up and read it, see [→ Without the ads, would these orders have happened?](../../1-report-overview/06-incrementality-chart/en.md).

### A cheap cross-check

**Run the same data through two rules and see whether the channel ranking moves.**

If it moves, the ranking is sensitive to the rule and the conclusion is weak. If it does not move, the ranking is solid. First click against last click is enough for one pass. It costs nothing, takes ten minutes, and blocks most reports that flip when the model changes.

## Attribution assigns credit, not causation

**What attribution does:** one order, several channels touched it, one rule splits the order between them. The number it produces **is not causation**.

Unseen exposure is not on the path. Buyers who were going to buy anyway get claimed in full. The window decides who is even eligible. Stack those three and the number says **who touched this order**, not **who created it**.

Attribution can rank channels. It cannot price one. **Last click is not a fact. It is a rule.**
