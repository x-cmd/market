# A budget split is only optimal when every channel's next dollar earns the same

**One million yuan, four channels.** There is no single right answer, but there is a test: **after the split, every channel's marginal return has to be the same.**

Not the same ROI. The same return on the *next* yuan you add.

## The test, in one rule

**The budget total is fixed.** One million, no more, no less, and all of it gets spent.

**The rule: total return is largest when the marginal return of every channel is equal.** Marginal return is what one more unit of spend buys back. It is a local rate, not an average over everything you have already spent. Two channels can have very different average ROI and still sit at the same marginal return.

**Same 1,000,000. All of it into search returns 1.14. Splitting on marginal returns returns 2.30.**

**More than double.** That is the price of the "give the most money to the highest ROI channel" reflex. Ranking by average ROI feeds a saturated channel and leaves a starting-up social channel with nothing.

## Where the rule comes from

**You can derive it without a formula.**

Suppose the current split is not optimal: search has a marginal return of 2.0, social has 1.2. **Now move 1 unit from social to search:**

- Search gets 1 more unit and earns 2.0 more.
- Social loses 1 unit and earns 1.2 less.
- **Net gain: 0.8.**

**The budget did not change, and total return went up.** So the original split was not optimal. **Keep moving until the two marginal returns match.** There, one more unit gains on one side exactly what it loses on the other, so the net effect is zero.

**Move another unit beyond that?** The channel you took from now has a higher marginal return, and the channel you added to has a lower one, so the net effect turns negative. **The equal point is the boundary between "moving more helps" and "moving more hurts."**

### The inequality behind it

In math: fix the budget at B, let each channel take xᵢ, and let total return be F = Σ fᵢ(xᵢ). Maximize F.

The optimum satisfies **f₁′(x₁) = f₂′(x₂) = … = λ**, meaning **every channel's marginal return equals the same number λ.**

**λ has a name: the shadow price.** It is how much total return rises when the budget grows by one unit, and **it is also the most you would pay for one more unit of budget.**

### With one channel, the rule says nothing at all

**A single channel has one marginal return. Equal to what?** To itself. The rule is empty in the one-channel case. It only has meaning when there are several channels and real trade-offs. With one channel the question is different: is this unit of money worth spending at all? See [→ Marginal ROI versus average ROI](../06-marginal-vs-average-roi/en.md).

## Where it stops holding

**The rule rests on four conditions. Break one and the conclusion does not follow.**

### The marginal cannot be estimated

**The whole rule rests on knowing each channel's marginal return.** If it cannot be estimated — spend is not broken into levels, each level has too few observations, or the number was guessed — **then you do not know where the crossing is, and there is nothing to equalize.**

**Go estimate the marginal first.** Optimizing toward a crossing that does not exist is worse than not optimizing at all.

### Hard constraints exist

**The real world has constraints: social advertising cannot exceed 40%, television has to take 15% of the brand budget, a new channel has to keep 10%.** With constraints in play, equal marginal returns may be unreachable.

**Add the "social advertising stays under 40%" constraint, and social gets pinned at 40% while search has to absorb 100,000 more. Total return falls from 2.30 to 1.97.**

**The two marginal returns are no longer equal.** Social is pinned, so its marginal return sits above search's. In principle you would give social more, but the constraint forbids it.

**A constraint costs you some return, and that cost is obvious. But constraints usually buy something else** — a brand requirement, a channel contract, room reserved for a new channel — **so do not let the optimizer treat them as mistakes and optimize them away.**

### The total is not fixed

**The rule assumes the 1,000,000 has to be spent.**

If the budget can flex — spend 800,000 and earn 1.6, spend 1,000,000 and earn 2.30, spend 1,200,000 and earn 3.2 — then whether the extra money earns its keep is a separate question.

**The test changes: keep adding as long as one more unit has a marginal return above zero.** That is not equal marginal returns. It is "add while the marginal is positive." **Equal marginal returns governs how to split; it says nothing about how much to spend in total.** The two get mixed up constantly.

### Channels affect each other

**Equal marginal returns assumes each channel runs on its own.** Often that is not true. More money on search advertising can take clicks away from organic search (the eBay 99.5% figure is exactly this). More money on television can lift branded search volume, and that benefit gets booked under search. Two channels can also complement each other.

**With cross-effects, per-channel marginals are not independent, and the equalized point is not necessarily the optimum.**

**This one is hard to quantify, but it is not idle theory.** Every platform's attribution already spreads these cross-effects around. Change the spreading rule and the allocation conclusion changes with it. See [→ Attribution: crediting each touchpoint](../01-attribution/en.md).

## What to do with the allocation you get back

**The rule is a test for the split, not for the size of the budget, and not for whether a channel is worth funding on its own.** For it to hold, the marginals have to be estimable, the constraints have to be written down, and the total has to be fixed. **The most common failure is taking a guessed marginal ROI, equalizing it, and walking away with an allocation table that looks precise.**
