# Your channel's ROI is 2.28x. That number cannot tell you whether to spend more

"This channel returns 2.28x. Is it worth putting more money in?"

**Average ROI cannot answer that.**

Average ROI tells you what a whole pile of spending has returned so far. It is a **backward-looking** number.

Your question is **forward-looking**: what does the next yuan buy back? That is marginal ROI — the return from the last unit of money you add.

**The two numbers can sit far apart, and they can even point in opposite directions.** A channel can show an average ROI of 1.87x, look perfectly healthy, and have a marginal ROI of 0.2 — meaning the next yuan is close to wasted.

## One number looks backward, the other looks forward

**Average ROI: total return divided by total spend.**

Search ads spent 500,000 last month and returned 1,140,000. Average ROI is 2.28x.

**That number is fine. It answers a different question.** It says: "the last 500,000 was well spent."

**Marginal ROI: what the last slice of money returned.**

Push another 100,000 on top and get 40,000 back. That slice has a marginal ROI of 0.4x.

**The catch: marginal ROI is computed per band, and every band differs.** Spending 0 to 500,000 and spending 500,000 to 600,000 are two different businesses on the same channel.

**The gap between the two lines is the whole difference.**

The average ROI line barely falls. It stays above 1.8x the whole way and always looks fine. The marginal ROI line drops the entire way, and the last band is already negative.

**Watch only the average ROI cell and you keep feeding this channel money — until marginal ROI goes negative.**

### Why average ROI refuses to fall

**Because it is cumulative.** The money won in the first three bands stays in the denominator and the numerator, propping up the later ones.

**Average ROI has a floor of 1.** As long as the channel is still profitable overall, average ROI will not drop below 1.

**Marginal ROI has no such floor.** It can be 0. It can be negative.

**So "this channel still returns 1.87x on average" can never imply "adding more money is worth it."**

## Working the numbers

**Take one set of figures and compute it twice. Same data, two completely different numbers.**

Search ads for one month:

| Spend band | Spend in band | Return in band | Marginal ROI |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0–100k | 100k | 480k | 4.8 |
| 100k–200k | 100k | 310k | 3.1 |
| 200k–300k | 100k | 200k | 2.0 |
| 300k–400k | 100k | 110k | 1.1 |
| 400k–500k | 100k | 40k | 0.4 |

**Average ROI:**

Total spend 500k. Total return 480 + 310 + 200 + 110 + 40 = 1,140k.

Average ROI = 1,140 ÷ 500 = **2.28x**.

**Marginal ROI:**

Take the last band, 400k to 500k. 100k in, 40k back. **Marginal ROI = 0.4x**.

**Same channel, same month, two numbers: 2.28 and 0.4.**

### Which one to look at

**Look at the one that matches the decision in front of you.**

- Judging how the channel has performed → average ROI.
- Deciding whether to add money next month → marginal ROI.

**Neither question can be answered with the other's number.**

### A more common trap: ROI ranking is not budget order

**The channel with the highest ROI should not get the most money.**

That sounds odd, but it follows. A channel can have a high average ROI because its early money earned exceptionally well and the channel has since saturated. Another channel with a slightly lower average ROI can still have a high marginal ROI — and that is where the next yuan belongs.

**Average ROI cannot set the order money gets spent in. Marginal ROI can.** That is the subject of [→ The equal marginal returns principle: the test for optimal allocation](../07-equal-marginal-returns-principle/en.md).

## Four ways this breaks down

### Spend that never got split into bands gives you no marginal number

**Marginal ROI is a function, not a number.** It answers "what happens if I add one more yuan", so you need observations at two different spend levels.

**One total spend and one total return give you exactly one average ROI.** In that case you do not have a marginal ROI at all.

**This is the most common situation in practice.** A channel with a single monthly budget gives you only the average.

**The workaround**: cut the data into time segments, compute average ROI for each, and check whether it is trending down. **That is a crude version of the marginal curve, but it shows the direction.**

### Bands too fine leave each one without enough data

**If each of ten bands holds only a few weeks of data, every marginal ROI in them is noise.**

Finer bands make the estimate less stable. **The goal is not finer bands. The goal is enough volume per band.**

A common approach: let each band cover a meaningful share of total spend — say no less than 10% — then check whether the curve moves down monotonically.

### Average ROI is booked return, not incremental return

**This one matters most and gets skipped most often.**

That 2.28x came from "spent 500k, got 1,140k back". **But how much of that 1,140k did ads actually cause?**

Ads can pull in people who were already going to buy. The order gets credited to the campaign, but it would have closed anyway if the ads stopped. **The channel's true incremental return can be far below 2.28x.**

**Platform reports use the platform's own attribution rule, not an incremental one.** How big the gap is, see [→ Would these orders have closed without the ads](../../1-report-overview/06-incrementality-chart/en.md).

### Marginal ROI is "this yuan today", not "this yuan forever"

**Marginal ROI moves over time.** The same channel can show a marginal ROI of 2.0 this month and 1.2 next month, because a competitor raised their bids.

**So marginal ROI is not a number you can put away.** It is recomputed every day.

## One rule to carry away

Average ROI looks backward: what a whole pile of spending returned in total. Marginal ROI looks forward: what the next yuan returns.

**On the same channel, the two numbers can sit far apart and point in opposite directions.** Average ROI has a floor of 1, so it always looks acceptable. Marginal ROI can be 0 and can be negative, so it is the one that tells the truth.

**One thing is worth remembering: for "should I spend more?", read marginal ROI, not average ROI.**
