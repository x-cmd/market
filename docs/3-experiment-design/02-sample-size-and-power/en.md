# A small sample can invert the answer, not merely blur it

**The danger of a small sample is not that you see less. It is that you see the opposite.**

The real effect is positive and the test says negative. The real effect is zero and the test says significant. Two mistakes that look different, one cause: with too few people, random noise outweighs the effect you are hunting. And nothing warns you. You get a tidy number like **-8.3%**, with a standard error and a p-value, looking more respectable than anything else.

Control, no ad seen: **0.059%**. Treatment, ad actually seen: **0.104%**. Treatment, no ad seen: **0.020%**.

The randomised comparison gives a lift of **73%**. That is the truth: the ad did work. Compare "saw the ad" against "did not see the ad" and you get **316%**. Match on age and gender exactly and it drops to **222%**. **One dataset, three numbers, a spread of more than four times.** The difference is not in the data. It is in how the groups are formed.

The starkest pair is 0.104% against 0.020%. One group saw the ad, the other did not — but exposure was not randomly assigned. The platform serves ads to people already more likely to buy, so that group converts better anyway. Compare them and you measure how much more the would-have-bought people buy, not how much extra the ad bought.

## How big a difference needs how many people

**Standard error**: how far the number you computed may sit from the truth. **Confidence interval**: the estimate plus its standard error, a range where the truth probably sits. 95% is the default.

**Sample size sets the standard error. The standard error sets the interval width.** Too wide an interval and you cannot separate "the ad works" from "the ad does nothing" — zero sits inside the range.

At 50 users per arm the half-width is **56%** of the relative lift. At 200 it is **29%**. At 2,000 it is **9%**. Same effect size in all three rows: the sample grew 40-fold and the interval narrowed by a factor of six.

**Why so slow?** The standard error falls with the square root of the sample size. Double the sample and the interval narrows by roughly 30% (1/√2 ≈ 0.707). **Buying precision through sample size costs square-root scale.**

**The smaller the difference you want to catch, the more people you need.** The smallest difference a design can detect — the minimum detectable effect, MDE — depends on the effect size and the sample size. Take a 2% baseline, α=0.05, 80% power: a 10-point difference needs about 100 users per arm, 5 points about 270, 2 points about 1,140, 1 point about 3,830. **Detecting a 1-point effect takes close to 4,000 people.** Advertising A/B tests often land in the 1–3 point range. Halve the difference and the sample quadruples.

## How the direction flips

The real effect is positive and the test says negative. Not mysticism. Arithmetic.

Baseline conversion 32%, true effect +2 percentage points, 200 users per arm. The standard error of the difference is 4.66 percentage points, so the measured value wanders across roughly **+2 ± 9.1 percentage points**. With luck you measure +10; with bad luck, -7.

**The truth is +2, and roughly 38% of the time you measure zero or below.** That is what "the direction flipped" means.

**You are not measuring the effect. You are picking a lucky draw.** And **a negative number is not the same as no effect, and significance is not the same as effect.**

## Size the run before it starts, not after

**Before the run.** Sizing afterwards looks like this: the data is in, you stare at the number and ask whether it is big enough. But you hold one number, and the truth is not observable. The only things left to compare against are other experiments' sample sizes, whose effect sizes differ from yours.

Sizing beforehand means estimating three things from historical data: the baseline conversion rate, the smallest difference you care about, and how many new users arrive each day. From those you get the people required and the days required. **If the answer is 4,000 people, you learn then that the experiment is not happening, or that it takes months.** It also **forces you to write down how small is still worth acting on**, which retires experiments that would have produced a 0.3% number after a week of traffic.

**Size first, launch second.** Write down three things before the run:

- **The smallest difference that matters.** How large a difference has to be, in business terms, before anyone moves budget. This sets the sample size and decides whether the experiment is worth running.
- **How many people per arm and how many days.** Feed the baseline conversion rate and the smallest difference into the standard formula or a calculator, take the number, divide by users per day.
- **How you will read the result.** Write down in advance what counts as a win and what counts as a failure. **This is the one most often skipped, and the only guardrail that stops a flipped number becoming a decision.**

The answer lands in one of three places, and none is a bad outcome:

- Affordable and quick enough: run it
- Acceptable duration, sample too big: change the design — raise the share of traffic treated, or lean on historical data for a quasi-experiment
- Absurd, say 4,000 people to run for a quarter: **do not run it.** The effect is too small to change any decision

**The sample size you compute is not only a cost estimate. It is the test of whether the experiment is worth running.**

## A three-day test that came back negative

A consumer brand ran a homepage A/B test in its own app: bigger product images on the first screen, and the "add to cart" button changed from grey to orange. The app gets 1,200 users a day, split evenly. The boss wants an answer in three days.

On day three, the redesigned group converts at 2.05% and the control at 3.05%. **The redesign cut conversion by 32.8%.** The boss reads it and says: kill it, roll back.

Each arm has 600 × 3 = **1,800 users** over three days, and the standard error of the difference is 0.53 percentage points. The 1.00-point gap sits at the edge of significance (z ≈ -1.9), so the **interval barely avoids straddling zero and looks "certain."**

**Here is the trap: an interval that avoids zero is not the same as a correct reading.** It says only that "-1.00 percentage points is far enough from zero", nothing about whether your redesign caused the gap. The real problem sits underneath: **this app's daily conversion rate moves**, and this window straddled a weekend promotion.

**The cost:** three days of traffic, the development time, a "the redesign is harmful" verdict already out the door, and **the most expensive item of all — the rollback.** If the redesign was good, rolling it back throws away its gains. On day four, conversion was back to 2.38% against 2.40% — the weekend promotion had steered traffic to the old homepage.

**The real error was not "the sample was too small." It was the requirement "an answer in three days."** Three days came from the boss wanting a result fast, not from the size of the effect. At a 2.5% baseline, 1,800 users per arm resolves differences of about 1.7 percentage points, and this redesign's real effect was under 1 point. **The order was backwards: work out how many days you need, then take that to the boss — not fix three days and gamble on them.**
