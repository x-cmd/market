# A number without its interval is half an answer

**"This channel returns 4.0 on every dollar." Not wrong — but it leaves out the half that usually matters more.**

4.0 is a **point estimate**: the number this batch of data produced. Rerun the same experiment a hundred times and you get a hundred different numbers; 4.0 is one of them. A **confidence interval** — the range your experiment would plausibly produce — covers the rest: the true value lands inside it 95% of the time.

The first two are contaminated by how they were observed. The ads reached people already going to buy, so more people bought because they were targeted. The third one is the sting. **The whole −63% interval sits below zero.** These ads failed to pay back at all — they lost money.

**It puts a number on how much was lost.** −63% sounds mild, like "a small loss". But the lower bound is −124%: **spend one dollar, lose one dollar and twenty cents.** That kills the "let's put in another round" plan.

**And it puts a number on how certain that is.** The upper bound, −3%, sits three percentage points from zero. **Run a larger experiment and the top of the interval may cross zero**, turning "certainly a loss" into "possibly a loss" — shut it down, or keep watching, depending on which one you believe. **A bare −63% cannot tell the two apart.**

## A point estimate is the best guess, not the most reliable number

Arguments stall on "is it 4.0 or 3.4", each side holding a point estimate and nobody moving. The reason: **the gap between two point estimates is usually smaller than the width of either interval.** **The interval swaps the question from "whose number is right" to "how big would the difference have to be".**

But an interval is not a verdict either. **Its width comes from your experimental design, not from the data.** And a subtler layer sits on top: **the interval only covers the assumption that the model form is right. It says nothing about whether the model form is right.** The premise behind [−124%, −3%] is that incremental attribution was measured correctly. If the measurement approach is off, a narrow interval is still a biased answer. **Report the best of twenty models and the interval is a fiction.**

## What the interval actually claims

**A 95% confidence interval, read properly: the true value has a 95% chance of falling inside this range.** The claim is about the **true value**, not the number you computed. Suppose the real effect is +2 percentage points and you test 200 people per group. The half-width is about 29 percentage points (the worked example in [sample size and power](../../3-experiment-design/02-sample-size-and-power/en.md)). Your computed number landing anywhere from +31% to −27% is normal.

**Roughly once every 20 reruns, the 95% interval misses the true value.** That 5% is already deducted when someone writes "95% confidence interval". **It is not "95% sure this time". It is "this method has 95% coverage".**

Two common misreadings. One treats the interval as "the range the true value might take" — it is actually "the range your computed number scatters across". The other reads an interval that crosses zero as "no effect" — **crossing zero only means "not detectable"**, so a true effect of zero and one of +12 percentage points look identical at small sample sizes.

Same effect size: at 50 people per group the half-width is **56 percentage points**, at 200 it is **29**, at 2000 it is **9**. **Forty times the sample buys a sixth of the width.** The relationship is a square root — quadruple the sample and you halve the width.

**Variability.** The first chart already said it: 97 million dollars of half-width in 2019, 1249 million in 2020. **More sample helps a little and cannot fix the root of it — high variability is a property of the business, not the study.**

**Effect size.** The most counter-intuitive of the three: **the smaller the effect you want to detect, the more sample you need, and the relationship is squared** — halve the effect and you need four times the sample.

## Overlapping intervals do not block a comparison

**They do not block it, and the comparison is worth making — but compare the difference, not the two point estimates.** Overlap describes each side's absolute level; your question is "how much better is A than B".

Channel A (search ads) has a point estimate of **4.0** and a 95% interval of **[1.2, 6.8]**, a half-width of 2.8. Channel B (feed ads) has a point estimate of **3.4** and a 95% interval of **[2.1, 4.7]**, a half-width of 1.3.

**A's wider interval does not mean A is worse. It means A's experiment was sloppier.**

The difference is 4.0 − 3.4 = **0.6**. Take the square root of the two standard errors squared and added, multiply by 1.96, and you get ±3.09. So **the 95% interval for the difference is [−2.5, 3.7]**. **It crosses zero. With the data on hand, you cannot tell whether A or B is better.**

**So can that 0.6 justify more budget?** Look at the scale of the business. If channel A spends 1,000,000 a month and channel B spends 500,000, a 0.6 ROI gap is 600,000 in monthly incremental revenue on A — **far larger than either half-width**, so it is worth separating. **If each channel spends 50,000 a month, that 0.6 is only 30,000, and it is not worth another round of traffic to resolve.** The size of the business decides.

## ROI 4.0, and an interval running from 1.2 to 6.8

A consumer brand ran a search-ads incrementality test inside its own app in 2024. Search ads spent **1,000,000 yuan** during the flight, and the randomized control measured **4,000,000 yuan** of incremental revenue. **The point estimate for ROI is 4.0.** The 95% confidence interval came out at **[1.2, 6.8]**.

**In money:** 4,000,000 of incremental revenue and an ROI of 4.0 at the point estimate; 1,200,000 and an ROI of 1.2 at the lower end; 6,800,000 and an ROI of 6.8 at the upper end.

**With ROI 4.0 alone:** every yuan spent returns four. Budget from that number and next month you add 500,000, expecting 2,000,000 of incremental revenue. **That is treating a point estimate as a promise.**

**With the interval [1.2, 6.8]:** the worst case returns 1,200,000, **still positive, but only 200,000 of net gain**. The best case returns 6,800,000, for 5,800,000. **At the bottom end you barely break even; at the top end a small business becomes a good one.**

**The interval's real job here was to kill one specific plan.** The team had intended to add 2,000,000 of spend after seeing ROI 4.0. They read the interval and did not. **Because if the true value sits at the 1.2 end, that 2,000,000 brings back incremental revenue that, net of the spend itself, comes out roughly flat.** The interval turned a "looks like a sure thing" decision into a "go in small steps" decision. **And the cost is on the record too:** six weeks of runtime, 10% of traffic in the treatment group, about one percent of natural traffic exposure lost. **Rigour has a bill attached.**

## Report every number with its interval

**Every number comes with its interval.** Say three things together: **the point estimate, how wide the interval is, and the sample size and measurement setup it came from.** The third gets dropped most often, and **without it, half a year later nobody knows how big the experiment behind the number was.**

A format you can copy:

```text
Incremental ROI 4.0, 95% confidence interval [1.2, 6.8]; n = 12,480 per group, 6-week
experiment, randomized split, metric = incremental order value within 14 days.
```

Two traps to avoid. One is reporting only the interval that flatters you — test five slices, publish the one that came back significant, and **that interval is a fiction**; see [multiple testing](../../3-experiment-design/03-multiple-testing/en.md). The other is treating a wide interval as "we cannot decide" and then deciding on the point estimate instead. **A wide interval means "not usable yet", not "so go back to that number".**

**And the worst move of all is to throw the interval away and keep one round slide number.** 4.0 is easy to say out loud. It is also the 1.2 that saved that brand 2,000,000.

import sys, math, statistics as st
d = {}
for l in sys.stdin:
    l = l.strip()
    if not l or l.startswith('period'): continue
    k, v = l.split('\t'); d[k] = float(v)
for y in ('2019','2020','2021','2022','2023'):
    v = [x for k, x in sorted(d.items()) if k.startswith(y)]
    m, sd = st.mean(v), st.stdev(v)
    hw = 1.96 * sd / math.sqrt(len(v))
    print(y, 'n=%d mean=%.1f halfwidth=%.1f CI=[%.1f, %.1f]' % (len(v), m, hw, m-hw, m+hw))
