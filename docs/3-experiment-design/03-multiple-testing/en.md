# Twenty combinations, three "winners", and at least one fake

**A team tested twenty combinations in one round. Three came back significant. At least one of those three is fake.**

If every combination gets its own separate test, a few "significant" hits out of twenty can be nothing more than luck. Combination five looks up 8%, combination eleven up 4%, combination eighteen up 11%. Three screenshots go into the chat, green and red side by side.

The problem is not a miscalculation. **Every number can be fine on its own. The error is treating twenty separate tests as twenty separate lotteries, when they are twenty draws from the same bag.**

## How three "significant" results appeared

A SaaS company rebuilt its landing page. Rather than one A/B test, they split the work into five changes and tested each on its own: headline, hero image, button text, page length, trust badges. Three of the five moved the same way.

```
Variant           Conversion    vs control
Control (original)     3.05%             —
A short headline       3.42%        +12.1%
B real photo           3.51%        +15.1%
C button text          2.88%         -5.6%
D shorter page         3.11%         +2.0%
E trust badges         3.47%        +13.8%
```

The report said: "Three variants beat control significantly. Ship them together."

Each p-value looks clean in isolation. The report skipped the part that mattered most: **all five groups drew from the same pool of visitors, so A and B competed for the same people.** The 0.37 points A gained and the 0.46 points B gained may have come from the same visitors in a good mood. One person sees version A today and version B tomorrow, and both count as evidence that they "were more likely to convert".

"Ship them together" is the worse half of that sentence. Whether three separately significant variants reinforce or cancel each other once stacked is invisible to tests run one at a time. **Three things that are each "significant" can stack into something significant in none of them.**

## Where the false positives come from

A **false positive** is a test calling an effect that is not there. Every test carries a significance level, conventionally 0.05: with zero real effect, it calls that a win 5% of the time. That sounds small. **The problem is that it gets multiplied by the number of tests.**

Take twenty combinations, every one with zero effect. Each test misfires 5% of the time, so across all twenty the chance that at least one is wrong is:

**1 − 0.95²⁰ ≈ 64%**

In other words, **twenty zero-effect combinations are more likely than not to produce one "significant" hit.**

That is the likely case, not the rare one. Twenty draws at a 5% win rate and you will almost certainly get one winning message, and it says nothing about whether that combination works.

The other side of the coin: **among twenty combinations, the biggest significant effect has already been selected.** You are not looking at twenty independent p-values, you are looking at the smallest of twenty, and the smallest of twenty is biased low. **Picking creates the effect, not necessarily the change.**

**The expected number of false positives is the group count times 0.05.** Run 100 groups and expect 5. Those 5 are not bad luck, they are what the rule produces.

A second loss comes with them: **real effects get diluted.** Suppose one of twenty combinations really lifts conversion by 10% and the other nineteen are flat. It ranks first and stands out. But if it ranks fifth and the four above it are fake, attention goes to the four first. **Four fakes sit above one real one, and the most significant one gets reported.**

**Double the groups and false positives rise with it. Your odds of finding something real do not, because the fakes in front push the real effect out of view.**

## Three ways to handle it

### Decide which comparisons to look at before the data arrives

The cheapest option: **write down the hypotheses you will test before the experiment starts, and let that list be the list.** After the run, look only at those. This is pre-specification, and it works because **it removes "pick the result that looks best" from the procedure.**

The list is short: "does a shorter headline lift conversion", "does the button text lift conversion". Three hypotheses means three tests, and three tests carry a 14% chance of at least one error, not 64%. The cost is real: **you may look at the data, find a place you never expected to work beautifully, and miss it.** That is what pre-specification buys.

### Correct: let the threshold move with the group count

The second idea: **if more tests means more false positives, tighten the bar until it cancels out.** The commonest version is the **Bonferroni correction**: to test m hypotheses, drop the significance level from 0.05 to 0.05/m. Testing 20 groups moves it to 0.0025, so every group has to clear a much higher bar.

Bonferroni is conservative and misses real things. Its looser cousin is the **Holm correction**: sort the 20 p-values smallest to largest, then require the first below 0.05/20, the second below 0.05/19, and so on. The bar relaxes step by step, ending near 0.05.

The **Benjamini-Hochberg procedure** controls something else: the share of false positives among everything called positive, not the probability of at least one error. At 100 groups it is far less strict than Bonferroni and recovers many real effects. The cost: **it no longer holds the "at least one error" probability for the family under 5%, only the false-positive proportion.**

### Test all the groups in one pass

The third option is the most thorough: **do not test combination by combination, test them all at once.** A traditional A/B test asks "is B better than A". An omnibus test asks a different question: **"is there at least one difference among these groups?"** The two common ones are ANOVA, the analysis of variance, and Kruskal-Wallis, its version for data that is not normally distributed.

If the overall test is not significant, the answer is "no detectable difference among these groups", and every pairwise comparison is protected at once. Only if it is significant do you go on to the pairwise comparisons.

What makes this work is **that it keeps the selection inside the procedure**: the overall test, the follow-up comparisons, and the correction are all rules written down in advance, not a person reading data and choosing. All three options share one thing: **the decision of "which one" is made before the data arrives.** A calculation error can be fixed afterwards. Picking one of twenty results and calling it a discovery cannot.

## Decide the question first, then the number of groups

Back to the SaaS example. The real question was never "which three of the five variants won". It was:

**"What question am I trying to answer?"**

If the question is "does the redesign help", the right design is one experiment, two groups, one pre-registered hypothesis. If one change has to go first, take the one with the strongest historical evidence, confirm it works, then split the rest apart.

If the question is "do these five changes each work", that is a five-way test. You have to accept a 23% false positive rate, or move the threshold to 0.01 and say in advance that only results above that bar count.

**How many groups to run is not set by how many you can run. It is set by how many questions you need answered.** Get that backwards and you land where this article started: start running, produce a pile of numbers, then go hunting for a question. The one you land on will be the brightest few in the pile — **they are bright mostly because you picked them first.**

Everyday number-reading is the same thing: look at twenty channels, twenty ad slots, twenty landing pages, then put more budget behind the significant ones. **Same trap, different coat.**
