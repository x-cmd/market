# Twenty combinations tested, and three of them are noise

**A team ran twenty combinations at once. Three came back "significant" — and at least one of those three is almost certainly fake.**

If every combination gets its own separate test, a few "significant" hits out of twenty can be nothing more than luck. Combination five looks up 8%, combination eleven up 4%, combination eighteen up 11%. Post three screenshots in the chat, green and red side by side.

Nothing is miscalculated. **Every number is fine on its own. The error is treating them as twenty independent lotteries when they are twenty draws from the same bag.**

## Where three "significant" results came from

A SaaS company rebuilt its landing page. Instead of one A/B test, they split the work into five changes and tested each as its own variant: headline, hero image, button text, page length, trust badges. Three of the five pushed the same direction — shorter headline, real person instead of illustration, button changed from "Free Trial" to "Start Free Trial".

```

Control (original)     3.05%             —

D shorter page         3.11%         +2.0%
E trust badges         3.47%        +13.8%
```

The report that came out said: "Three variants are significantly better than control; ship them together."

**Each p-value looks clean on its own.** But the report left out the one thing that mattered most: **all five groups split the same pool of visitors, and groups A and B were competing for the same people.** The 0.37 percentage points group A gained over control, and the 0.46 points group B gained, may have come from the same visitors who happened to be in a good mood that day. The same person saw version A today and version B tomorrow, and both times it counted as evidence that he "was more likely to convert".

"Ship them together" is the worse half of that sentence. Whether three separately significant variants amplify or cancel each other once stacked is invisible to tests run one at a time. **Three things that are each "significant" can stack into something significant in none of them.**

## Where false positives come from

A **false positive** is a test saying "there is an effect" when there is none. Every test carries a significance level, conventionally 0.05: even with zero real effect, there is a 5% chance the test calls it a win. Five percent sounds small. **The problem is it gets multiplied by the number of tests.**

Take twenty combinations, every one with zero effect. Each test misfires 5% of the time on its own. Looked at all twenty together, the probability that at least one is wrong is:

**1 − 0.95²⁰ ≈ 64%**

In other words, **with better than even odds, twenty zero-effect combinations will produce one "significant" hit.**

That is not a rare event, that is the likely one: twenty draws, each with a 5% win rate, and you will almost certainly get at least one winning text message — and the message says nothing about whether that combination really has an effect.

Now the other side: **among twenty combinations, the biggest significant effect has already been selected.** You are not looking at twenty independent p-values but at the smallest of twenty, and the smallest of twenty is biased low by construction. **The act of picking creates the effect — not necessarily the change itself.**

**The expected number of false positives is the group count times 0.05.** Run 100 groups and expect 5 false positives. Those 5 are not bad luck. They are what the rule produces.

There is a second loss on top: **real effects get diluted.** Suppose one of twenty combinations really lifts conversion by 10% and the other nineteen are flat. The real effect ranks first and stands out. But if it ranks fifth and the four above it are fake, your attention goes to the four first. **Four fakes sit above one real one, and you report the most significant.**

**Double the groups and false positives rise with it. Your odds of finding something real do not — the fakes in front push the real effect out of view.**

## Three ways to fix it

### Decide which comparisons to look at before the data arrives

The cheapest option: **write down the hypotheses you will test before the experiment starts, and let that list be the list.** After the run, look only at those and ignore the rest. This is called pre-specification, and it works because **it removes "pick the result that looks best" from the procedure.**

The list is short: "does a shorter headline lift conversion", "does the button text lift conversion". Three hypotheses means three tests, and three tests carry a 14% chance of at least one error, not 64%.

The cost is real: **you may look at the data, find a place you never anticipated working beautifully, and miss it.** That is the price of pre-specification.

### Correct: let the threshold move with the number of groups

The second idea: **if more tests means more false positives, tighten the bar until it cancels out.** The most common version is **Bonferroni correction**: to test m hypotheses, drop the significance level from 0.05 to 0.05/m. Testing 20 groups moves the threshold to 0.0025, so each group has to clear a much higher bar.

Bonferroni is conservative, and it misses real things. Its looser cousin is the **Holm correction**: sort the 20 p-values smallest to largest, then require the first to be below 0.05/20, the second below 0.05/19, and so on. The bar relaxes step by step while the total stays put, back to close to 0.05 by the last group.

There is also the **Benjamini-Hochberg procedure**, which controls the share of false positives among everything called positive, rather than the probability of at least one error. At 100 groups it is far less strict than Bonferroni and recovers a lot of real effects. The cost: **it no longer guarantees that the "at least one error" probability across the family stays under 5%. It only keeps the false-positive proportion under control.**

### Test all the groups in one pass

The third option is the most thorough: **do not test combination by combination; test all the combinations at once.**

A traditional A/B test asks "is B better than A". An omnibus test — the two most common are ANOVA, the analysis of variance, and its version Kruskal-Wallis that does not assume a normal distribution — asks a different question: **"is there at least one difference among these groups?"**

If the overall test is not significant, the answer is "no detectable difference among these groups", and every comparison is protected at once. Only if it is significant do you go on to the pairwise comparisons to see which groups differ.

What makes this work is **that it keeps the selection inside the procedure**: the overall test, the follow-up comparisons, and the multiple-comparison correction are all rules written down in advance, not a person reading data and choosing. All three options share one thing: **the decision of "which one" is made before the data arrives.** A calculation error can be fixed afterwards. Picking one out of twenty results and calling it a discovery cannot.

## Decide the question first, then the number of groups

Back to the SaaS example. The real question was never "which three of the five variants won". It was:

**"What question am I trying to answer?"**

If the question is "does the redesign help", the right design is one experiment, two groups, one pre-registered hypothesis. If one of the five changes has to go first, take the one with the strongest historical evidence, confirm it works, then break the rest apart.

If the question is "do these five changes each work", that is a five-way test. You have to accept a 23% false positive rate, or move the threshold to 0.01 and state in advance that only results above that bar count.

**How many groups to run is not decided by how many you can run. It is decided by how many questions you need answered.** Get that backwards and you land where this article started: start running, produce a pile of numbers, then take the pile and go hunting for a question. And the one you land on will be the brightest few in the pile — **they are bright mostly because you picked them first.**

Everyday number-reading is the same thing: look at twenty channels, twenty ad slots, twenty landing pages at once, then put more budget behind the significant ones. **Same mistake, different coat.**
