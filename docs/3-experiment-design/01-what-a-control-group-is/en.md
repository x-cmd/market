# A bad control group is worse than no control group

**"We paused the ads for a while and sales dropped 18%."**

**That 18% is not ready to report.**

Sales fall when ads stop. Winter arrives, a rival cuts prices, your site breaks, the warehouse runs dry. Any of these can take 18% off sales. Without a control group, you cannot tell how much of that drop your ads caused.

The worse problem: **a broken control group is worse than none.** With none, you at least know what you do not know. With a bad one, you get a professional-looking number and build on it.

## Why cutting cities in half can void the result

Run ads in half the cities, leave the other half alone, compare sales. That is the standard setup, and it is fine. The problem is the word *pick*.

**The half you pick and the half you leave were never the same.**

Before the split, the treatment cities were already converting **1.3 percentage points** better than the control cities. **Those 1.3 points have nothing to do with the ads.** They were there first. Subtract the test-period numbers straight and all 1.3 points land in the "ad effect" you compute; turn the gap the other way and it eats into the measured effect instead.

**Whatever the two sides differ by is how far your number is off** — and you never know the size in advance. If you did, the experiment would be unnecessary.

### Random is what makes the split work

Random assignment guarantees one thing: **the differences you know about get spread evenly across both sides.** The draw ignores city size, past sales and how much you like the market. Google's 2011 paper *Measuring Ad Effectiveness Using Geo Experiments* did this, assigning at random at the DMA (Designated Market Area, the US census geography used for ad markets) level, narrowing the interval by more than 10%.

Random does not fix everything. It cannot separate geographic isolation, customers who never visit the same store, or one side fed by organic traffic and the other by paid ads. **Each of these turns "not comparable" into "looks comparable".**

## Four conditions a control group has to meet

**Miss one and the result does not hold.**

### Random and comparable

**Any difference between the two sides should come from the randomisation and nothing else.**

Without randomisation, get as close as you can to **comparability**: stratify by size, pair on past performance. But comparability has a ceiling. City size, historical sales and average order value are matchable; customer mix, brand awareness and offline foot traffic are not. **Random beats matching because matching only fixes the differences you thought of.**

### The same period on both sides

**Both sides have to live through the same stretch of time.** Not "the treatment ran for a month, compare it against last month". It is **the same month, one side running the ads and the other not.** Timing mismatches hide well: use last month as the control and this month contains a major promotion, and the two numbers are incomparable — and you recall the promotion only afterwards, when the finding is out.

### Nothing else differs

**Change nothing except the ads.** This is the condition most often broken: prices changed during the test period, the control side ran out of stock, someone moved budget into another channel, the website was redesigned mid-test. **Any one of these puts something other than the ad into the gap**, and these biases are **one-sided**, so no statistical correction removes them.

The two sides also have to be the same kind of sample. Say treatment is 10 cities at 50,000 each and control is 10 cities at 10,000 each — both are "half the cities got the ads", but the two sides see very different intensity. Check **impressions per capita** or **spend per capita**, not total spend. A store test where store A gets the new design and store B keeps the old one, but A sits in a shopping district and B in a residential area, is not an experiment at all: it is grouped observation. One test settles it — if the two sides were not separated at random, the design is wrong.

### Enough sample

**Only this last one decides whether the number is precise enough.** The first three make the number meaningful; this one decides whether it is worth reading. With too small a sample the interval is too wide to use: "it probably helps a bit", not "it helps by this much". For the arithmetic, see [→ A small sample can invert the answer](../02-sample-size-and-power/en.md).

**One set of campaigns, four ways to split, four numbers.** The real lift is around **18.2%**. The three wrong designs each run higher, and the worst one more than doubles the truth.

### Comparing before and after the pause

**The setup: run ads for three months, pause for one, compare sales across the two periods.** The most common mistake, because it looks cheapest: no cities to cut, no control group, the data is already there.

**The problem: the two periods sit in different market conditions.** Season, competitor campaigns, promotions, holidays, weather. The gap you measure is half ads and half the two periods being different to begin with. It gets worse: **the business environment shifts while the ads are off** — pauses often come with budget cuts, so you credit the ads for a decline they did not cause.

**The hard limit: before-and-after cannot measure "how much the ads created", only "was anything going on in this period that relates to the ads".**

### Comparing against the neighbouring city

**The setup: run ads in this city, use the city next door as the control.** It looks like the random split, but it is not — **you chose it by hand.** Your criterion was "it looks like our side", and you set it yourself. It lands you a control that matches on what you can see, while the difference sits where you cannot.

### Using people who did not buy as the control

**The setup: everyone got the ads, but the control group is "the customers who did not buy during this period".** This one fails most quietly, because it sounds reasonable.

**Not buying is the same as not being affected.** A customer who did not order may have seen the ad and decided against it, or may never have seen it. Mix those two into one control group and the number has no clear meaning. **"Did not receive the ad" and "was not affected" are not the same thing** — you cannot use the outcome as the cause, and you cannot guarantee the control was never exposed elsewhere.

## A campaign destroyed by its own control group

A consumer brand ran a geo experiment on search ads, measuring how much sales fell in the two cities where it stopped advertising. **The problem was that the boss picked the two cities.** The reason given was "these two cities are similar in size, so they are easy to compare". The size was similar; the basket and category mix were not — one a university town, young and impulse-heavy, the other a family town, slower decisions.

**During the test period, a local competitor ran a big promotion that hit both cities and nowhere else.** So the number stacked three things: the ad effect, the difference between two cities that were never alike, and the promotion.

**The write-up said "pausing the ads caused sales to fall 31%".** The truth shows up once you pull out the pre-test baseline: the paused cities' weekly conversion rate was already **1.3 percentage points** higher than the cities that kept running, before the ads ever stopped. Take that gap out and what is left is the part the ads can explain.

**The money was spent for nothing.** The finding was wrong, and it was discovered only after it had gone out. **Keeping the pre-test data and normalising by the baseline would have saved it.** The bill: the revenue lost in the paused cities, two months of labour, one wrong finding already out the door, and the next budget decision made on the back of it.

## No control group, no lift — and a bad one is worse than none

**The value of a control group is not "one more number to compare". It is the only thing that separates the ads from everything else.**

**One, do not report a lift without a control group.** The number can exist, but label it as a before-and-after difference with no control behind it. **Two, a bad control group is worse than no control group** — it hands you a number that can go straight into a report and needs no explanation. **Three, do not skip the pre-test period.** It is what separates "the two sides were never alike" from "the ads caused this much"; without it, you never find out the control was invalid.

Back to "sales dropped 18% after we paused the ads". That sentence is not wrong. The sentence after it is: "so the ads created 18% of growth."

**Those two 18% numbers are separated by a control group.**

**Data**: The numbers come from a synthetic scenario built to show the gap between four ways of splitting the same campaign. The true lift is set at 18.2%, and the three wrong designs each run high because a baseline difference, a seasonal difference and a self-selection difference are mixed in. The ruined campaign uses the same numbers: inside the reported 31% drop sits a 1.3-point baseline difference.
