# How loud is a brand? Three numbers answer it, and none of them answers it for the others

**"Is this brand loud?" is three questions wearing one coat: how much of the market it holds, where it ranks, and whether the people who see it like it. One brand can rank #2 in search, #4 in revenue and top the controversy list — three rulers, three rankings, none covering another's ground.**

## A share above 100% points at the denominator, not at bad data

In 2023, US shoe store retail sales (row 21 of the BEA retail sales table) came to $41.557 billion. NIKE's revenue for the same period was $51.217 billion, fiscal year ended May 2023. Divide: **123.2%**.

A share cannot exceed 100%. Numerator and denominator are not on the same ground: NIKE's revenue covers the globe and every category, wholesale and direct, while the denominator covers shoe stores in the United States.

**Nothing here is bad data. The word "share" demands a numerator and a denominator covering the same scope.** Fixing it means the global footwear market, which costs money, or NIKE's US shoe store sales, which no public table breaks out by brand.

## What each of the three rulers measures, and where each one breaks

### Share answers how much of the market

The denominator is a market, the numerator is this brand's sales inside it, and the scopes have to line up. **Official statistics** give the industry total but stop at the format — a brand cannot be located inside them. **Backing out from the earnings report** divides the revenue the company reports itself (SEC XBRL tag `RevenueFromContractWithCustomerExcludingAssessedTax`) by a published industry total, **and the number ships with its formula.**

Divide FY2023 SEC revenue by the same BEA denominators — $41.557 billion for shoe stores, $333.604 billion for clothing stores. NIKE's $51.217 billion gives **123.2%** and **15.4%**; Skechers' $8.000 billion gives 19.3% and 2.4%; Columbia Sportswear's $3.487 billion gives 8.4% and 1.0%.

**Same numerator, different denominator: NIKE goes from 123.2% to 15.4%.** **True share is not in this column — public sources never line up a numerator and a denominator.**

### Rank answers how easily the brand gets found

**Search rank** is where a brand lands in a category-term search. Query the public Wikipedia search API for six category terms (snapshot 2026-11-09) and take the earliest brand entry in the top 20: sportswear gives Columbia Sportswear at #2, shoe brand Adidas at #1, sneakers Air Jordan at #8, running shoes On at #5, basketball shoes Air Jordan at #3, hiking boots Merrell at #4. **Change the term and the ranking changes completely.** On "sneakers", Adidas is #9 and Nike #15, ahead of NIKE sit ITZY's song "Sneakers (song)" and "Sneakers (band)". On "running shoes", neither reaches the top 20.

### Sentiment answers whether readers like it

Brand-level reputation data sits behind a paywall, so public sources offer **the sentiment of the text itself**: pull each brand's Wikipedia article body and count how densely a fixed list of conflict words appears. The list has 23 words, matched case-insensitively on word boundaries: criticism, criticised, controversy, controversial, lawsuit, sued, settlement, allegations, boycott, protest, backlash, investigation, accused, convicted, fraud, misleading, failed, slump, decline, declined, bankruptcy, layoffs, recall.

## Top of the search results is not the same as named in an AI answer

Columbia ranks #2 on "sportswear" and Adidas ranks #1 on "shoe brand", yet neither says anything about whether an AI answer names the brand.

**One, retrieval gives a list, generation gives a summary.** The gap between #2 and #15 on a search engine is a position. An AI answer is a paragraph, and which brands it names depends on the narrative it assembles from what it read. **The #2 name on the list may never reach that paragraph.**

**Two, what gets into an answer is corpus density, not rank.** Being recognized by a search engine means the brand counts as a "brand entity" — a distinct named thing — which says nothing about how often it appears in the corpus. Columbia's article body runs 2,206 words, Adidas' 9,688. **A thin corpus lowers the odds of being carried into a summary.**

**Three, this cell has no public number.** Brand mention share inside AI answers is computable only by running a fixed set of questions and recording who showed up, and **every question, model and time window produces a different number.** So "Adidas ranks #1 on shoe brand" does not explain "why does no AI answer mention it". **The rank ruler measures whether a retrieval system recognizes the brand as an entity, not whether a generation model writes it into an answer.**

## Visibility is three numbers, not one

"Is this brand loud" holds three questions, each with its own ruler, scope and failure mode: the share ruler fails when numerator and denominator do not align, the rank ruler fails when a list position is read as a place in an answer, the sentiment ruler fails on the denominator.

**Report the three numbers separately, each with its scope and its date.** Blending them into one score looks tidier, but the weights behind it are invented, the reader cannot see them, and the verdict flips the moment they are nudged.
