# A fourfold mention spike is only good news if the mentions are kind

**800 mentions this week against 400 last week. Good news or bad? The count cannot tell you. Volume without sentiment is a ruler with no markings.**

On the US App Store, Pokémon GO went from 12.3 reviews a day over the preceding 12 days to 52 reviews on 2026-10-19 — 4.2 times the baseline. Of those 52, 53.8% carried 1 or 2 stars. **On the day volume quadrupled, nearly every extra review was a complaint.**

The bar for October 19 towers over its neighbours. The 12 days before it (10-07 to 10-18) produced 148 reviews, 12.3 a day.

Read the chart on its own and the best you can say is that a lot of people were talking at once. You cannot tell whether they came to praise or to complain. Add sentiment and the answer flips. **Of those 52 reviews, 28 were 1 or 2 stars — a 53.8% negative rate, 5.2 points above the previous 12 days' 48.6%.** Mentions rose fourfold, and the part that rose was complaints. **Volume carries no direction of its own.**

### The spike lines up with a release

Reviews carry the app version number. Group by version and the jump has a cause.

| Version | Reviews | Mean rating | Negative rate (1-2 stars) | Review dates |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.431.1 | 79 | 2.85 | 46% | 10-07 to 10-13 |
| 0.433.0 | 25 | 2.44 | 52% | 10-13 to 10-16 |
| 0.433.1 | 223 | 2.75 | 51% | 10-16 to 10-27 |
| 0.435.0 | 29 | 3.24 | 34% | 10-27 to 10-29 |
| 0.435.1 | 144 | 2.95 | 47% | 10-29 to 11-07 |

Version 0.433.1 landed on October 16 and drew 223 reviews, more than twice any other version — the same day volume started climbing. The 1-star titles from October 19 spell out the reason: "Issues after recent update", "Update is trash", "Bugged Update", "Constantly crashing". **One release, four times the mentions in a week, and 54% of them complaints.**

Report "mentions up 320%" and it reads as good news; report the sentiment too and it reads as "200-plus complaints in a week".

The reverse case sits in the same data. When 0.435.0 shipped on October 27 the negative rate fell to 34% and 1-star reviews dropped from 71 to 28. **Fix the bug and volume falls while sentiment rises.**

The complaint was not aimed at one update. One sentence keeps recurring in the body text: "Ever since Scopely bought this app they have made changes that make basically everything worse" — **users pointed at the new owner that took over the game in 2019.** A fourfold peak, a negative rate up from 48.6% to 53.8%, and users concluding that the company is wrecking the product: **this spike was a negative asset, not exposure.** Add the sentiment and that 320% becomes "one update drew 200-plus complaints in 48 hours, 71 of them 1-star". **Sentiment turns "more people know about us" into "we annoyed 200-plus people."**

## Whoever writes the label decides what your number means

Nobody analysed that 53.8%. Every reviewer tapped their own stars. **The person labelling is the person speaking.** Of the three labelling schemes available, that one carries the most weight: the labeller pays for it, and the stars stay public on the store page.

**The user labels it.** One to five stars. Label and content come from the same person, so no machine has to guess. The cost is coverage: it reaches only the people who bothered to write.

**A machine labels it.** A dictionary or a model decides positive versus negative. The dictionary approach — VADER, proposed by Hutto and Gilbert and published at ICWSM in 2014 — pairs a hand-built list of sentiment words with rules for punctuation and intensifiers, so "not good" scores negative. It covers text with no stars, but sarcasm fools it.

**The platform labels it.** This layer gets missed most often. Apple says it plainly: the review summary on an App Store product page **is generated by a large language model**, pulling "highlights and key information" out of user reviews into a paragraph that "refreshes periodically". **The "what people say about this app" blurb is machine-written prose**, and nothing marks it as such. Three label sources, and they do not always agree.

## One brand, three different sentiment numbers

The rule that matters most: **sentiment in a single review and sentiment across a body of conversation are not the same measurement.**

Pokémon GO's cumulative App Store rating is **4.00 stars**, backed by **656,621 ratings** since the July 2016 launch. The most recent 500 reviews average **2.84 stars**, with a 48.2% negative rate.

So "the sentiment of Pokémon GO" has three numbers — 4.00 lifetime, 2.84 for the last month, 2.69 for the last week (W43). **All three are correct and each answers a different question.** Publish one alone and the reader cannot tell which.

Three choices move the number, and each has to travel with it. **The window** — lifetime, last month, or this week. **The region** — Apple calculates the total rating "by region", so 4.00 is the US figure and the UK, Japan and Germany carry their own. **The sample** — Apple's reviews RSS feed **paginates only 10 pages**, 50 reviews per page, which caps it at **the 500 most recent reviews**. The feed says so itself: "CustomerReviews RSS page depth is limited to 10". "The most recent 500" is not a window you picked; it is everything this interface hands you.

Each limit carries a consequence. **Recent, not complete** — a 32-day window shows the October 19 spike and says nothing about last year. **An outage inflates volume and the negative rate together**, so 48.2% cannot be read as "half the users are unhappy". **The window moves with popularity** — Pokémon GO's 500 reviews spread over 32 days, Facebook's 500 cover only 5, so **the two apps' "recent" covers different spans**.

Lifetime averaging dilutes recent bad news: **Facebook's store rating sits 1.25 stars above its recent window, WhatsApp's by 1.32, Pokémon GO's by 1.16.** The lifetime number is not fixed either — Apple notes the total rating "can be reset when you release a new version".

## Report volume and sentiment together

Volume is the denominator. Sentiment is the sign. Without the sign, the denominator cannot say whether the brand got better or worse.

**Pull labels and content in the same pass.** Counts on their own turn "how many people spoke" into a report that reads like "how the brand is doing".

**Write the scope beside the sentiment.** "48.2% negative" is unusable without three things attached: which period, which region, and where the sample came from. **Spot-check labels against content on a schedule** and report the mismatch rate: **that rate is the uncertainty on your sentiment number.**

**A fourfold spike can be a crisis or a victory. The volume number alone cannot tell them apart. Only sentiment can.**
