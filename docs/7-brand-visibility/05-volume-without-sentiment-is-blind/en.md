# Volume tells you how many people talked, not whether they were happy

**800 mentions this week against 400 last week. Good news or bad? The count alone cannot say. Volume without sentiment is a ruler with no markings.**

On the US App Store, Pokémon GO went from 12.3 reviews per day over the previous 12 days to 52 reviews on 2026-10-19 — 4.2 times the baseline. Of those 52, 53.8% carried 1 or 2 stars. **On the day volume quadrupled, nearly every extra review was a complaint.**

The bar for October 19 towers over its neighbours; the 12 days before produced 148 reviews, 12.3 a day.

The chart gives one conclusion: people were talking in bulk. Add sentiment and it flips. **Of those 52 reviews, 28 were 1 or 2 stars — a 53.8% negative rate, 5.2 points above the previous 12 days' 48.6%.** Mentions rose fourfold; the part that rose was complaints. **Direction is not a property of volume.**

Version 0.433.1 landed on October 16 with 223 reviews, more than twice any other version — the day volume started climbing. The 1-star titles on October 19 say why: "Issues after recent update", "Update is trash", "Bugged Update", "Constantly crashing". **One release, four times the mentions in a week, and 54% of them complaints.** After 0.435.0 shipped on October 27 the negative rate fell to 34% and 1-star reviews dropped from 71 to 28: **once the bug was fixed, volume fell and sentiment rose.**

## Three parties write the label, and their labels disagree

That 53.8% came from no analysis. Every reviewer tapped their own stars. **The person labelling is the person speaking.** That makes it the most trustworthy of three schemes, because the labeller carries the consequence: the stars stay public. The same fact can be labelled three ways, and they disagree.

**First, the user labels it.** One to five stars. Label and content come from the same person, so no machine has to guess. The cost is coverage: only willing writers are reached.

**Second, a machine labels it.** A dictionary or a model decides positive or negative. The dictionary approach — VADER, proposed by Hutto and Gilbert and published at ICWSM in 2014 — pairs a hand-built word list with rules for punctuation and intensifiers, so "not good" scores negative. It reaches text with no stars, but sarcasm fools it.

**Third, the platform labels it.** Apple states it plainly: the review summary on an App Store product page **is generated by a large language model**, pulling "highlights and key information" from user reviews into a paragraph that "refreshes periodically". **The "what people say about this app" blurb is machine-written prose**, unmarked.

### Catching a mislabelled review by sampling 20 of them

Labels cannot be trusted alone, so 500 reviews' star ratings are compared against their body text, looking for inverted cases. **Pull 20 at random and read the stars next to the body** — that is where the two percentages came from, from a two-way word list that assigns a direction to the body text and compares it against the stars. The list is crude, so the numbers are order-of-magnitude only, but they answer what matters: can the label be trusted.

## The same sentiment has three different numbers

The rule that matters most: **sentiment inside one review and sentiment across a body of conversation are not the same measurement.**

Three choices move the number. **Which window** — lifetime, last month, last week — is the difference computed above. **Which region** — the total rating is "calculated by region", so 4.00 is the US number; the UK, Japan and Germany carry their own. **How the sample was drawn** — Apple's reviews RSS interface **paginates only 10 pages** of 50 reviews, so it yields at most **the 500 most recent reviews**. "The most recent 500" is not an arbitrary window. It is everything this interface offers.

Each limit has a consequence. **It is "recent", not "all"** — a 32-day window shows the October 19 spike and nothing about last year. **It is "who bothered to write", not "who has an opinion"** — and **an outage inflates volume and the negative rate together**, so 48.2% is not "half of users are unhappy". **The window length moves with popularity** — Pokémon GO's 500 reviews spread over 32 days, Facebook's 500 over 5, so **the two apps' "recent" covers different spans**. Lifetime averaging dilutes recent bad news, which is why all three store ratings sit far above their recent windows: **Facebook by 1.25 stars, WhatsApp by 1.32, Pokémon GO by 1.16.**

## Volume only counts when sentiment travels with it

Volume is the denominator. Sentiment is the sign. Without the sign, the denominator cannot say whether the brand improved or deteriorated.

**The cost.** The extra volume was complaints. One sentence recurs: "Ever since Scopely bought this app they have made changes that make basically everything worse" — **users aimed the complaint at the new owner that took over the game in 2019, not at one update.**

**The lesson.** "Pokémon GO mentions up 320%" is a number worth celebrating. Reporting sentiment produces "one update brought in 200-plus complaints in 48 hours, 71 of them 1-star". **With sentiment attached, it turns "more people know about us" into "we annoyed 200-plus people".**

**Pull the label and the content together.** Counts without sentiment turn a measurement of "how many people spoke" into a report that reads like "how the brand is doing".

**A fourfold spike can be a crisis or a victory, and the volume number alone cannot tell them apart.**
