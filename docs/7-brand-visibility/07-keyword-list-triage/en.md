## A keyword list fails from being too long, not too short

We pulled volume for 42 running-shoe terms across the first half of 2026, using English Wikipedia page views as a stand-in for search attention. The largest, Adidas, drew 410,887 views; the smallest, Gym shoe, drew 33. **The gap between them is twelve thousand times.**

**Adding a term is free; removing one costs a person.** Terms arrive by asking around, mining comment sections, copying a competitor's ad keyword list. Adding a row takes seconds. Removing one takes someone explaining why.

**Most added terms do not answer the same question.** Some people search your brand name, some the product category, some a competitor, and some a phrase with nothing to do with running shoes. Four different questions sit in one table, so "mention count" sums four unrelated things and dividing it by the total gives an average. **An average that looks like an answer says nothing.**

**The longer the list, the less anyone checks it.** A term added three months ago no longer returns what it returned then, and one visibly stale row makes readers question every row. **Rows that should not be there are not "kept for reference". They are contamination.**

## Three checks decide what stays in the list

**The first check: who is this term asking about.** A **brand term** comes from someone who already knows you and types your name. A **category term** names the thing itself, from someone who does not know you yet. A **competitor term** is a search for this kind of product that puts you beside your rivals.

Each needs its own reporting line. Brand-term volume rising says awareness is rising. Category-term volume rising says the category is expanding. Competitor-term volume rising says you are being used as a reference point. **Pool them into one "total search volume" and a rising number tells you nothing about which one rose.** See [A brand term and a category term answer two different questions](../06-brand-terms-vs-category-terms/en.md).

Of those 42 terms, only four are brand terms (Hoka, Clifton, Bondi, Hoka One One): 9.5% of the rows, 1.8% of the volume. Competitor terms number 16, at 38.1% of the rows and 57.3% of the volume. **Sort by volume and you start with competitor terms; sort by row count and you start with category terms. Only one order works: brand terms first**, the only part you directly own.

**The second check: does this term deserve a row.** Priority does not follow search volume. It follows one question: can this term tell you apart from everyone else? A term with ten thousand searches that every brand ranks first for is worth less than a term with three hundred searches where you rank second. **The first tells you nothing about who you are; the second tells you exactly that.** The test is the results page: if neither of the top two is you, the term is worth a row.

**The third check: is this term still alive.** Freshness has two layers: **is anyone still searching this term** (seasonal terms, event terms), and **does it still mean what it meant**, even when the words hold while the people searching and the results page move.

**Marathon** recorded 112,501 views in April 2026 and fell to 64,266 in May, a 42% drop. **Leave a seasonal term unlabeled and every month is wrong.** The other direction is **Hoka**: 16 views a day on May 22, 805 on May 29, 23,142 for the whole of June. **That is not seasonal. The meaning changed**, from "a thick-soled running shoe" to "a mass-market brand".

A running-shoe brand starts tracking visibility with 300 terms from three places: the search suggestions on its own brand name, the terms shoppers type themselves in comment sections, and a competitor's ad keyword list copied over. Laid out, the highest-volume terms turn out to be the least useful.

Bucket by type first. That deletes nothing, it only separates. Then keep the terms that tell you apart: a term that ranks you in the same slot as nine other brands does not deserve a row, and 241 of the 300 go out here. Then freshness-check the remaining 59, and twelve survive.

**These 12 are not "the 12 most important". They are "the 12 that work".** Do not delete the other 288. Move them to a second sheet with the reason for exclusion next to each one. **The exclusion reasons are worth more than the 12 terms.** Six months later you can see why a term was cut: too little volume, no way to tell you apart, or gone stale.

Why 12 and not 30? Every extra row is another to re-run, re-check and re-explain each period. The split: four or five brand terms, three or four category terms, three or four competitor terms.

## Sorting costs forty times what collecting does

Sorting a 300-term list takes three things: **labelling each term's type** (about 1 minute per term), **checking whether it tells you apart** (about 1 minute), **checking whether it is still alive** (about 2 minutes). **Four minutes per term, 300 terms, 20 hours.** Collecting is three bulk imports, half an hour.

**The 241 terms that got cut were supplying a fake share.** With 288 rows that should not be there mixed in, both the numerator and the denominator of "we hold 34% of mentions in this category" are contaminated. **Sorting a keyword list is not about making a number look good. It is about making the number right.**

**Stale terms are the expensive kind.** Hoka rose 33x in a year: 693 views for the whole of June 2025, 23,142 in June 2026. A list sorted three months ago never recorded that shift. **The cost of staleness lands on money.** Hoka's net sales, from Deckers, were $2.587 billion in FY2026, up 15.9%. FY2027's two quarters were $703.5 million (up 7.7%) and $677.2 million (up 6.8%).

**Put the attention chart next to the sales chart.** The term with the fastest attention growth is not the one with the fastest sales growth. Hoka kept climbing while sales slowed, because much of the new volume comes from people who are not the core runners the brand started with.

**The channel mix is moving too.** In Q1 FY2027 Hoka's wholesale sales grew 2.9% and direct-to-consumer grew 17.3%. By Q2, wholesale grew 8.5% and direct-to-consumer only 3.7%. **Direct-to-consumer went from the fastest grower to the slowest.** The shape of the brand-term volume changed, and the channel followed. **Without a re-sort, this change stays invisible.**
