# Every brand number comes with a receipt, or it does not count

A brand turns out to have far more public numbers behind it than most people expect. Each one has to answer a prior question: who reported this, and under what definition.

A public signal is not a verdict. It is a catalogue line — one brand, one date, one definition, one number. The catalogue never says whether a brand is good. It says what can be looked up, and what cannot.

## Where a brand's position actually comes from

"Where does my brand stand?" is not one question. It is at least five: what share of the market, at what price, how many people look it up, who is paying to talk about it, whether it makes money. Each has a public number behind it, and each has its own definition.

## The seven fields a brand entry has to fill

A brand entry that someone else can check has to fill these fields:

| Field | What goes in it |
|---|---|
| Company and brand | Which company owns which brands |
| Category | Which statistical definition it falls under |
| Share | What share of which market |
| Price premium | Priced against whom, list price or transaction price |
| Search and volume | How often it is searched, how often it is mentioned |
| Public financials | Revenue, marketing spend, regional split |
| Source and date | Who reported it, as of when |

**A field that cannot be filled has to say why it is empty.** An empty field with a reason is information. An estimate dressed up as a measurement is not.

## Company and brand are not the same thing

One company can own several brands; one brand can sit inside several companies. Get this wrong and every other field is wrong too.

Deckers Outdoor shows it in one row. The company owns UGG and HOKA, but SEC has a single CIK for it, 0000910521, and the two brands turn up under completely different names in public data.

The reverse case is worse. The SEC company name is `NIKE, Inc.` and the Wikipedia article is `Nike, Inc.` — and `Nike (company)` is a separate article for the same brand. One comma of difference. In the chart above, `Nike, Inc.` has 3.67 million cumulative visits and `Nike (company)` has 3,000. A factor of **1212**. Use the wrong article name and the brand's measured visibility is zero.

`On Holding` fares no better. Among eight peers its article barely exists — 1 cumulative visit. Its revenue is a filing away; its visibility field is empty.

So that field keeps three things apart: **who the company is** (CIK, legal name), **which brands it owns** (the brand list in the annual report), and **which name each public trail is filed under**. When they disagree, the filing wins.

## Three public routes to a share number

Share is the field everyone wants and the one most often filled wrong.

**Official statistics.** Eurostat's enterprise e-commerce survey puts online sales at 19.82% of total enterprise turnover across the EU 27 in 2024, and 8.49% of the part sold to consumers. The definition is clear and anyone can rerun it. It stops at the industry level.

**Third-party estimates.** Research firms publish brand share numbers whose definitions are usually not public. Is the denominator revenue or units? Does it include e-commerce? How big was the sample? Usually unanswerable. Either find the methodology note or leave the field empty. A third-party number with no definition goes into the entry, gets quoted elsewhere, and comes back as official data.

**Backing it out of the filings.** Take one company's revenue, divide by a published industry size. Every input traces to a source, but the result is computed, and if numerator and denominator are counted differently the share is worthless.

This page uses the third route: each company's own reported revenue from SEC XBRL over BEA retail sales. A derived share has to travel with its arithmetic. A bare percentage hides which denominator you chose.

## How to price a premium so it does not mislead

"How much more expensive than peers" turns on three choices, and the answer changes with each one.

**Against whom?** The whole category, or the few brands in the nearest price band? NIKE and HOKA averages are not comparable — one sits in the 100-dollar range, the other in the 150-dollar range. Compare like with like, and name who you compared against.

**List price or transaction price?** List price is the tag on the website; transaction price is what the money moved for. Discounts, clearance and member pricing sit between them. A brand sold full-price direct keeps the two close; a brand that clears stock on discount can see a 30% gap.

**Priced off what?** Here is a real gap in public data: a brand's own price list is not reproducible public data. Website prices change, and once changed they are gone. What goes in the source field?

This page sidesteps brand price lists and uses an official price index instead. The BLS footwear CPI (seasonally adjusted, 1982-84=100) was 144.074 in January 2024 and 145.486 in January 2025. It covers all footwear, not one brand. So price splits in two: category trend from CPI, which has a number, and brand-against-brand premium, which stays empty.

## What search and volume can actually show

Brand-term search volume gets quoted more than anything else here, and misleads most often. The free, reproducible substitute is **Wikipedia article views** — a public Wikimedia API that needs no key. It counts how many people went looking for a brand on purpose. Much narrower than brand-term search volume, and far easier to define.

- **What it shows:** how much attention a brand draws when people go looking on purpose, and which way it is moving.
- **What it does not show:** sales, since the people who look a brand up on Wikipedia and the people who buy it barely overlap. Or sentiment — a pageview records an open, not a compliment.

Social media mentions are a different field, with different scale, sources and de-duplication rules at every vendor. Brand mention share inside AI answers is another field again — small sample, narrow time window, a single prompt that does not reproduce. None of these three are "search volume". Report them separately.

## What the filings give you

Filings are the hardest layer of public signals, because they are audited. The SEC XBRL interface pulls each tag with one curl.

**What you can get:** total company revenue (the `RevenueFromContractWithCustomerExcludingAssessedTax` tag — all company revenue, not one brand segment), marketing spend, and regional splits (NIKE's 10-K separates NIKE Direct from Wholesalers). The marketing tag name differs by company: NIKE and Deckers use `MarketingAndAdvertisingExpense`, Skechers and Columbia use `AdvertisingExpense`. One concept, two tag names.

**What you cannot get:** revenue at single-brand granularity, marketing spend at single-brand granularity, the latest fiscal year for some companies.

On single-brand revenue, Deckers' annual report states what UGG and what HOKA each sold — but it sits in a table inside a PDF, not in an XBRL tag. SEC's companyconcept endpoint returns figures with no segment definition attached, so the HOKA line cannot be pulled out. No company breaks out ad spend by brand. And Skechers' XBRL records stop at fiscal 2024.

Those fields stay empty, and no estimated value goes in. Guessing single-brand revenue by subtracting HOKA from Deckers' total mixes an estimate into a table that claims every number traces back to a source.

## What the catalogue is for

Every number answers one question: how much was recorded for this brand under this definition. It does not answer whether the brand is good, whether to invest, whether it can win. The first needs other judgements; the rest need a signed name on them.

The real value is that other people can check it. Every field carries its number, unit, source and date, and a field missing one of those cannot be used. The fields that stay empty were not skipped — public channels do not have them today. Naming the fields that could not be filled is worth more than a tidy-looking set of numbers.
