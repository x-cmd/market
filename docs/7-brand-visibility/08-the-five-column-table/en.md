# Five columns decide what a table is allowed to say

A table arrives with five headers: **term, channel, date, count, sentiment**. Drop one column and the answer shifts. Write one vaguely and the answer flips.

The data is real: the most recent 500 US App Store reviews from seven footwear and apparel apps. **One word, `On`, produces two opposite conclusions inside the Nike channel.** A naive string match says "On was mentioned 125 times, and 72% of them are negative." Open the reviews one by one and every one of those 125 is the English preposition.

## Check the headers before you read the numbers

| Column | What breaks without it |
|---|---|
| Term | A preposition gets counted as a brand |
| Channel | Your own app and a retailer's app get pooled together |
| Date | "Most recent 500" quietly spans four years |
| Count | Occurrences and mentions end up sharing one name |
| Sentiment | The negative rate lands on the wrong term |

Four of the seven apps are brand-owned; three are multi-brand retail. **The most revealing cell is `On` in the Nike channel.**

## Each column has to carry one fact

### Term: a string is not a thing being referred to

In English `on` is a preposition. `On` is the Swiss running-shoe brand. The naive matcher looks for `on` on word boundaries, case ignored, and finds 168 hits in Nike's 500 reviews. Open them one by one and all 168 are the preposition:

> "Love the app **on** my phone, but can't sign in on my iPad"
> "I order a pair of shoes to train for a marathon **on** Nov 1 and they tell me they are out of stock **on** Nov 5"

Demand the brand's own collocations instead — `On` or `ON` immediately followed by Cloud, Running, Shoes, Sneakers, Trainers, Holding, or `on.com` — and **the count in this channel drops to 0**. The same rule finds 47 real mentions across 38 reviews in On's own app.

So the term column does not hold `On`. It holds `On (brand: match On/ON + Cloud|Running|Shoes|Sneakers|Trainers|Holding or on.com)`. **A term you cannot write a matching rule for is a term you cannot use.**

Brand-owned apps: On 158, Nike 125, adidas 59, Under Armour 40 reviews mention `on`. Retail apps: Zappos 84, Finish Line 89, DICK'S 64.

**Not one of those numbers is "how often On was mentioned."** Of Nike's 125, zero are about the On brand; Finish Line's 89 are prepositions too. They measure how often the English word `on` appeared. Brand-owned app reviews name the brand most, but their praise and complaints aim at login, shipping, and returns rather than the shoes. **Brand-owned numbers run optimistic. Retail numbers carry the outside view.**

### Count: occurrences, mentions, and review rows differ

The raw corpus holds 3,484 reviews. In the On channel the brand term `On` appears 47 times across 38 reviews, and **7 reviews mention it more than once** — "This is my 6th **On** Cloud shoes that I owned. The **On** Cloud family is the BEST."

**Occurrences measure how dense the term is in the text. Mentions measure how many people said it.** One person saying it five times counts as 5 on both counts; five people each saying it once count as 5 occurrences but 1 mention.

The denominator moves too: 47 over 484 is 9.7%, 38 over 484 is 7.9%. **Both get called "mention rate." They sit 1.9 percentage points apart.**

### Sentiment: who did the labelling

This page uses App Store stars — **one to five, tapped by the reviewer themselves.** The person labelling is the person speaking, which makes this the most trustworthy of the three labelling schemes (the other two are compared in [Volume tells you how many people talked, not whether they were happy](../../7-brand-visibility/05-volume-without-sentiment-is-blind/en.md)).

What matters here is **which term the label sits on.**

Under naive matching, of the 125 Nike reviews containing `on`, 72.0% carry 1 or 2 stars, mean 2.02. That is a number with real news value: "On is heavily mentioned on Nike's official app, and 72% of it is negative."

After strict checking, that number does not exist. Those 125 reviews are Nike users complaining about login, stock-outs, and filters. Inside On's own app, of the 38 real On mentions, 10.5% carry 1 or 2 stars, mean 4.61. **Same word, two matching rules: the negative rate goes from 72% to 10.5%, and the direction reverses.**

## A zero is not the same as nothing there

A 0 in a table has at least three causes, and the table rarely tells them apart.

**First, the channel has almost no data in that window.** The Zappos 0 above is this case. **A coverage problem, not a market problem.**

**Second, the source does not support that granularity.** The 500-review ceiling is the example — **the shape of the interface picks the window length for you, and the table will not say so.**

**Third, it really is zero.** The closest thing here is HOKA's single mention in the adidas app — one mention is one mention, and writing it as 0 is wrong.

**So empty cells get written in three tiers,** and the three are not interchangeable: "0 (10 reviews in this channel's window, all checked, no match)," "0 (0 reviews in this channel's window, no coverage)," and "NA (the source does not support this granularity)."

Apple's documentation points at the same thing: **the store's cumulative rating is computed per territory**, so one app has two numbers in the US and in Japan. **A channel column with no region in it does not say which market you are talking about.**

## One missing column makes the table unusable

Term, channel, date, and count each block one way of misreading the data. Sentiment is the expensive one. Attaching a 72% negative rate to a brand nobody mentioned is not a wrong number — it is **a conclusion running backwards**, and the reversed version reads like real news.

**Every cell also carries uncertainty.** How tight the term-matching rule is, how dependable the sentiment call is — both ship with the numbers. Whether On's count is 47 or 0 turns on one rule about capital letters, and **that difference came from reading all 3,484 reviews.**

**Count the columns before you count anything.** Work only when all five are there, and write down why a cell is empty when it cannot be filled. **The most dangerous table is not the one with a missing column. It is the one with a missing column and a pretty number next to it.**
