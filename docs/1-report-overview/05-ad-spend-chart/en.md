# How much does one more dollar of ad spend buy back

"This channel is still at 2x ROI. Adding another 200,000 is fine, right?"

**The ROI in that sentence and the "adding another 200,000" ask two different questions.** ROI looks backwards: across all the money already spent, how many times did it come back. "Adding another 200,000" looks forward: for that extra 200,000, how many times does it come back. The two numbers can sit far apart and point in opposite directions. **This page computes that extra dollar.**

**The first band returns 4.8x. The last returns 0.4x.** Same channel, same month, same audience. Every 1 yuan comes back 4.8 at the left and 0.4 at the right — **add 100,000 and you get four yuan back for every ten spent.**

That line is the **marginal ROI**: put one more yuan into this channel and this yuan brings back so much. It differs from "how much did this channel earn in total." Push budget left to right and each band returns less than the last; the band where the return falls below what you spent loses money outright.

### Who needs this chart

Two questions, one action. **"Can I still scale?"** — the curve has to be located first, and that last band drops below 1, so money going in there does not buy back equivalent sales. **"Should this channel grow or shrink?"** — moving budget between channels, where you compare the two sides on their **current** marginal return, not their historical totals.

The reader has money to spend and wants to know where **the next dollar** goes. So the output is one number: add the 200,000 or do not.

**The average ROI line barely moves**, from 4.8 down to 1.87. **The marginal ROI line falls the whole way and ends negative.**

Why does the average hold up? **Because it is cumulative.** Profit from the first three bands stays in the numerator, propping up the bands after it. And average ROI has a hard floor — **1**. As long as the channel still makes money, it cannot drop below 1. Marginal ROI has no such floor: it can be zero, it can be negative. **So "average ROI is still 1.87x" can never support "more spend is worth it."**

### What marginal means, and where the turning point sits

**Marginal means "the extra one."** Slice the data into bands by spend, then divide each band's return by that band's spend. First band above: 0 to 100,000 returns 480,000, so marginal ROI = 480 ÷ 100 = 4.8. Last band: 400,000 to 500,000 returns 40,000, so marginal ROI = 0.4.

**Marginal ROI is a function, not a number.** The same channel going from 0 to 500,000 and going from 500,000 to 600,000 are two different businesses.

**The turning point is where marginal ROI crosses 1.** Above 1, the yuan comes back with more than you put in. Below 1, you come out with less than you went in with. That last band at 0.4 means **that 100,000 is a net loss** — 60,000 of it went nowhere.

**On a continuous curve, that crossing is exactly where total return is maximised.** Real curves step band by band, so: **find the band where marginal ROI first drops below 1 and stop the extra budget one band earlier.** On the chart above, that is 300,000 to 400,000.

## Put it in money

**One month of search ads cost 300,000.** Platform attribution says those ads brought back 600,000 in sales, so ROI looks like 2x. **But three tenths of it catches people who were going to buy anyway** — customers who **would have bought even without this ad**, because they searched your brand name, clicked in, or typed your URL from memory.

Three tenths of that 300,000 in spend, so 180,000 in sales, belongs to them. **Those 180,000 you were already going to get.** The real increment is 600,000 minus 180,000, which is **420,000**. **Real ROI is 42 ÷ 30 = 1.4x, not 2x.**

**Real marginal return can sit far below what the report shows.** The report calculates "how much sales got attributed to this band." You need "how much extra sales this band produced."

The gap is not invented. In 2015 eBay ran randomized experiments: shut off part of their non-brand search ads and watch how many sales fell with them. A standard regression put the return at **4100%**; adding a time trend and regional controls brought it to **1400%**. The randomized experiment put the true value at **−63%**, 95% confidence interval from −124% to −3% — **the ads brought no increment at all and crowded out sales that would have happened anyway.** Brand terms are worse: after eBay turned off brand-keyword ads on Yahoo! and MSN, **99.5% of the clicks they lost were picked up within seconds by organic search on the same site**. For the method, see [→ Would these sales have closed without the ads?](../06-incrementality-chart/en.md).

**So the answer to "how much does one more dollar buy back" is measured, not computed.** "Three tenths" can only come from an experiment.

width="760" alt="dtc_budget_up_orders_flat_then_down"></a>

This is the most common shape in real campaigns: **once budget passes a certain point, the extra money stops buying new orders.** The falling stretch is not flat — it is a reversal.

Three causes stack up. **The audience gets bought twice** — the first group you reached was always the most likely to buy, and the second pass reaches the same people. **The auction gets more expensive** — your bid stays put while competitors raise theirs, so each yuan buys fewer impressions. **Creative starts to fatigue** — the same creative seen again and again, and click-through rate slides; see [→ When the creative needs replacing](../../5-campaign-analysis/03-creative-fatigue-ctr-decay/en.md).

**This is what saturation looks like: flat first, then down.** A Hill curve writes that shape as a formula — effect(x) = x^α / (x^α + k^α), where k is the saturation point. To reach 90% of the ceiling, spend has to reach 9^(1/α) times k: with α = 2 that is 3x, with α = 3 only 2.1x. See [→ Hill saturation: how an effect hits its ceiling](../../8-analysis-toolbox/05-hill-saturation-curve/en.md).

### More companies are advertising every year

Among EU enterprises with 10 or more staff in NACE sections C–E, the share spending money advertising online went from 21.57% in 2014 to 26.97% in 2024. **Five percentage points in ten years.** But those points push into an already crowded road: more advertisers means more competitors at auction time. **This is how rising competition shows up on your own marginal curve** — not a sudden collapse, but the whole curve shifting left.

Countries do not move alike: Germany went from 18.18% to 34.64%, France from 11.81% to 22.87%. **Poland runs the other way** — 27.71% in 2014, 20.99% in 2024, a drop of nearly seven points. **Those seven points are the evidence that the curve does shift left.**

## The line you compute today can move tomorrow

**Marginal ROI is "this dollar right now," not "this dollar forever."** Two things push the curve right, making more budget worth it. **A bigger audience** — the product line expands or a new channel opens, and the same money covers a slice not yet touched. **Better creative** — the same money buys more clicks, and those clicks go deeper. This one moves the curve most, because creative is something you control.

Three things push it left. **More competition** — the EU numbers above are exactly this. **Creative fatigue** — the same creative, seen repeatedly, converts less, and this is the one lever you can pull yourself. **Seasonality** — peak season is crowded and off-season is empty, so the same yuan buys more in the off-season.

**One time lag is easy to miss: ad effects take time to arrive.** Money spent today works tomorrow, so part of this week's marginal ROI is last week's spend showing up. The model that handles this is adstock decay; see [→ Adstock decay: how long an ad takes to land](../../8-analysis-toolbox/04-adstock-decay/en.md).

## The last yuan decides whether you scale

**First: rank on average, spend on marginal.** "Which channel returns the most" and "who gets the next yuan" are two different questions. The channel with the highest ROI may be long past saturation — its early money earned beautifully, and adding more now returns 0.4 at the margin. **ROI rank is not a spending order**; see [→ Marginal ROI and average ROI: two different numbers](../../8-analysis-toolbox/06-marginal-vs-average-roi/en.md).

**Second: subtract the sales that were coming anyway, then compute the marginal.** Marginal ROI on a report is attribution, not increment. The 1.4x is what remains after removing three tenths, and those three tenths come from an experiment. **The platform's number and the real number can differ by forty times, dropping from 1400% to −63%.**

**Third: "we can still scale" needs a number behind it.** Any band where marginal ROI falls below 1 is money that goes in and loses. **Do not back an incremental decision with a single average.**

"How much does one more dollar buy back" — **the answer is one specific number, not "performance is still fine."**
