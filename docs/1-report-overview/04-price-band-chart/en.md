# Your price only holds up if the category agrees

"Is my price right?"

Simple question, and the easiest one in the report to get wrong. "Right" is not measured against your own costs — costs tell you how much margin you keep, not whether anyone buys. A price holds up on one thing alone: **within the same category, what do comparable things sell for, and how many of them sell?**

One example. A home appliance category, four bands, ordered by price. **The lowest band, 30 to 60 yuan, takes 42% of the volume. The most expensive band, 200 to 300 yuan, takes 6%.**

That answers half the question. **It does not say which band you are in.**

## Find your band before you read anything else

**Bands are ordered by price, each labeled with the share of volume it holds.** The bar your product lands on is your position in the category.

Find yourself first:

- **Band one** (42% of volume): you are fighting the same crowd as everyone else. Your price is not a moat, it is a threshold — anybody can sell at this price.
- **Bands two and three** (52% together): the **main battleground**. More than half the volume sits here, and so does the most competition.
- **Band four** (6%): you are selling positioning, not value. The rules here are not the rules of the other bands.

Two questions produce this chart: **"Is my price right?"** and **"Should I move up a band?"** It does not answer right or wrong. It hands you the reference frame — where your price sits among everyone else's, and what moving up costs before you pay it. Band two to band three takes volume from 34% to 18%. The reader wants a number, not a trend read, so the output is one decision-ready sentence: "Bands two and three are the main battleground, we are in band two, moving up means giving up half our volume."

## Draw the category boundary before you draw the bands

**Before you sort anything by price, write down what is in "this category."** Skip that step and the chart downstream is worthless. Two ways to frame a category, and they give **completely different answers**:

- **By product**: the same electric kettle is an electric kettle in every channel.
- **By channel type**: a supermarket's kettle, a specialty store's kettle, and a marketplace's kettle go into different categories.

**Same product, different frame, and the whole chart moves.** A specialty store sells service and assurance. A supermarket sells cheap and good enough. The two should never be priced against each other.

**One more thing is easy to miss: whether service and installation are included.** An air conditioner listed at 3000 yuan can differ by several hundred yuan at checkout, depending on installation, cleaning, and warranty length. A category definition has three parts: the frame, whether service is included, and what is excluded. Write those three lines down and other people can compare their bands to yours. **Once set, do not change it halfway** — redraw the frame mid-project and every band computed before it is void.

## Cut the bands by volume, not by instinct

**The one method worth memorizing: price bands are cut, not chosen.**

Picking three tiers by hand — entry, mainstream, premium — fails because **the boundaries have nothing to do with where volume sits.** Hand-picked tiers often put nearly all volume in the first tier and leave the second empty.

**Cut on volume quartiles instead.** Sort every item by price, then **find the three prices where cumulative volume reaches 25%, 50%, and 75%.** Those three prices bound four bands. **Each band is comparable in volume**: roughly 25% apiece, so you can tell at a glance whether you are mainstream or marginal. **The boundaries are set by the data, not by you**: two people working the same data independently get the same bands. The chart above was cut this way, and it is a worked example — a real project needs item-level price and volume detail, from your own transaction records or a third-party retail scanner dataset.

**One check is mandatory: how close does each band's median price sit to its upper edge?** If a median is pressed against the boundary, **that band is too narrow** — spillover from the tier above, not a standalone tier. Either merge it or move the boundary to the median.

## What a real category's bands look like

**First, the frame.** Take US furniture and home furnishings stores, one channel type only (NAICS 442). Home goods sold by supermarkets and online home stores are excluded. This is row 8 in BEA's official retail sales series. Sales divided by BEA's chain-type price index give implied volume — whether the things being sold are growing or shrinking month to month. Both raw series are official, and one command reproduces them.

**This one is worth slowing down for.** From 2019 to 2023, average monthly nominal sales at furniture and home furnishings stores went from 10.71 billion to 12.02 billion — up 12%. Over the same stretch **the price index went from 100 to 116.1** — up 16%.

**So volume fell: 96.6, below the 2019 level.**

That is why this page belongs in the report: **the "demand got better" you think you see can be the price going up.** When a category's whole band shifts upward, "we raised our average selling price" is subtraction, not addition, for volume.

**Same volume, measured in revenue: band one drops from 42% to 21.5%.**

**Volume share and revenue share are different things, and they can point in opposite directions.** By volume, band one is the biggest block at 42%. By revenue, it is the smallest at 21.5%. **Prices in band one are low, so 42% of the volume turns into only a fifth of the revenue.** If the goal is **shipment volume** (pushing units, filling channels, climbing rankings), band one is the main battleground; if the goal is **revenue** (building sales, funding cash flow), band one is the bottom band, and **the more you sell there, the less revenue you capture.** Cut the same category on revenue instead of volume and the boundaries land in different places — **cutting bands on volume and counting money on revenue mixes two definitions, and the conclusion comes out twisted.**

### Promotion runs deepest in the middle, and never at the top

**Promotions concentrate in the middle bands to clear inventory; the top band rarely discounts, because that would damage its positioning.**

Band two (60–100 yuan) holds 34% of volume and 30.9% of revenue, and it has the most sellers and the fiercest competition, so whoever cuts first takes the order and everyone follows. **Band three (100–200 yuan) mirrors band two**: only 18% of volume but 30.6% of revenue, discounting through bundles and sets rather than price cuts — the unit price holds while freebies lift the basket.

**Band four (200–300 yuan) almost never discounts.** 6% of volume, 17% of revenue. **Once this band discounts, buyers remember.** Something listed at 250 yuan that drops to 180 yuan is worth 180 yuan next time — **you are pre-announcing your own price cut.**

**So discount depth has to match your band. Bigger is not better.** In band one promotions barely help, because buyers there are comparing prices anyway and matching their discounts drives the price below your margin line. In a middle band promotion is the main battlefield, but it needs a **rhythm** — discounting all year means your list price is fiction. In the top band, **do not use discounts, use something else**: gifts, extended warranty, free installation, on-site service. None of these move the sticker price, so none of them hurt your positioning.

## How hard you discount has to be predictable

The second number on promotions: **how many big sale events the category has per year, how long each runs, and how deep the discount goes.** This number has a real source — BEA's chain-type price index breaks out monthly price changes. The furniture and home furnishings price index (2019=100): 99.8 in 2020, 104.9 in 2021, 116.0 in 2022, 116.1 in 2023.

**The 10.6% in 2022 did not come from one big sale. Prices rose every month of the year.** That is a different animal from "30% off on Singles' Day": **the first is a price level shifting up, the second is the price level holding while a few dates discount.** **Standing price increases** shift the whole band up, leaving your relative position unchanged but forcing buyers to readjust. **Dated discounts** hold the band in place, but on those dates you **drop into the next band for a while** — the people who buy from you belong to this band, and for a few weeks you are in the next one. **So ask which kind it is.** "30% off" with no duration attached says nothing.

**January 107.1, October 92.4 — peak is 16% above trough.** January runs high on the post-holiday moving wave. October runs low because that month's promotions get eaten by November's Black Friday. This sets your stocking and pricing rhythm. **Off-peak (92 to 95)**: few buyers, **so do not cut price here** — discounts buy little volume because few people are browsing anyway, and off-peak is for clearing stock and protecting cash. **Peak (above 100)**: lots of buyers, **so this is the window to raise price** — in a January month, the same goods sell for more.

**Many stores get this backwards**: off-peak looks quiet so they discount, and discounting drives away even more traffic; peak is busy shipping and nobody thinks about raising price, so the pricing power goes to waste.

## Four boxes decide whether your position holds

**Four things have to be filled in. Miss one and the position does not hold.**

| Box | The number to fill in | What breaks if you get it wrong |
|---|---|---|
| **Boundaries** | How the category is framed, whether service and installation are included | You are not comparing the same thing |
| **Bands** | The four quartile bands, and which one your price falls in | You do not know who you are competing with |
| **Promotion rhythm** | How many times a year, how long each, how deep, which kind | You compete on the wrong price band |
| **Seasonality** | How far peak sits from trough, and which months are off-peak | You cut price when you could have raised it |

**Fill in those four boxes and the pricing decision has an answer you can defend.** For the worked example at the top, that answer reads: home appliances framed by product, delivery included and installation excluded; bands 30–60 / 60–100 / 100–200 / 200–300 cut on volume quartiles; we sit in band two at 34% of volume and 30.9% of revenue; promotions densest here, 6 events a year, 7–10 days each, median discount 18%, concentrated on 618 and Singles' Day; peak January–February above 105, trough September–October at 92–95.

**The decision follows.** I am in band two, the most crowded band, so promotions have to keep up but cannot run all year. No price cuts in the off-peak; in peak season I can test band three. Moving up one band means giving up half my volume, so first find out whether another channel can replace that half. **That is not the same answer as "is my price right." It is a usable answer.**

**One last thing: price bands move.** In that BEA series, the price index went from 100 in 2019 to 116 in 2023 — **the whole band shifted up 16% in four years.** So the chart is not a one-time drawing: **recut the quartiles every quarter and check whether you have been pushed into a band behind you.**
