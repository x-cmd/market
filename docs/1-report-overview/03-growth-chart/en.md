# Is This Market Still Worth Entering

A market is worth entering when three numbers hold up together: **how big it is**, **whether it is still growing**, and **whether a handful of giants decide its fate**. Each is real, each has one or two ways to calculate, and the wrong choice reverses it.

Take twenty-nine years of US retail: the bars are scale, the red and teal lines are two growth rates.

## Read these two numbers first

### Who asks for this chart

"Is this market still growing?" opens every report, and the answer lives on two lines: the **scale line** shows where the ceiling is, the **growth line** shows whether the market is climbing or flattening. One line hides a trap: bars always rise, so readers assume the market was always good. **Stack the growth line on and the bars mean something.**

US retail in 2025: **gross output $2,945.2 billion, intermediate inputs $1,134.8 billion, value added $1,810.4 billion**.

**Output** is the total value of everything the industry sells. The same box of goods is counted once when a wholesaler sells it and again when a retailer sells it on. **Value added** is output minus the raw materials and goods bought in: what the industry created itself, and what GDP counts.

**Gross output runs 1.63 times value added.** The gap is double counting: the retailer resells the wholesaler's goods unchanged, so the wholesale layer lands in the total twice. The more **middlemen** a sector has, the wider the gap; in manufacturing a product passes through once and the numbers nearly coincide. **Only value added works across industries:** on output you measure who handles goods, not who creates wealth.

## Is It Real Growth, or Only Higher Prices

Treating nominal growth as real growth is the most common defect in industry reports. **Nominal growth** compares this year's money to last year's, leaving you no idea how much of the increase is inflation. **Real growth** strips prices out first: did more stuff get sold, or did the same stuff get pricier?

### What each growth rate is for

**Nominal is the invoice, real is the count of units.** The gap between them is the price level, and the wider it is, the more of the growth is "we raised prices" rather than "we sold more."

## Does a Few Giants Call the Shots

Big is not the same as open. **If a handful of giants run the industry, an outsider gets no share.** Two numbers measure this: the top four, and the whole field.

### CR4 measures the top four

**CR4 is the combined share of the four largest firms.** Four at 84.6% means the industry is four firms' business. Four at 20% means the other 80% sits with countless small ones.

Here are revenue shares for seven large US retailers. **The denominator is the $2,195.44 billion those seven total, not all US retail.** On total US retail every share drops to single digits.

And because **the retail giants' revenue is packed with goods the wholesaler sold in**, shares on revenue make the retailer vouch for the wholesaler's goods, so **retail concentration gets overstated**. Where **intermediate inputs are tiny** — software, chip design — the two nearly coincide. **A report has to say which basis it used.**

CR3 is 77.4%. CR4 is 84.6%. Amazon (32.65%) and Walmart (32.18%) split it nearly evenly, 64.8% between them.

### HHI measures how concentrated the whole field is

**HHI is the Herfindahl-Hirschman Index: square each firm's share in percentage points, then add all the squares up.** The extra step over CR4 is **counting the tail** — CR4 lumps everything outside the top four into "other," while HHI asks whether that "other" is a hundred small firms or one large one plus a few.

This example: 32.65² + 32.18² + 12.54² + 7.27² + 6.70² + 4.85² + 3.81² = **2395**. Below 1500 is unconcentrated, above 2500 highly concentrated, so 2395 sits at the top of the middle range.

**This HHI is necessarily too low.** These seven hold 26.3% of US retail; the other 73.7% is collapsed into one "other" contributing zero, so the real number is higher. **2395 is a floor, not a true value.** (Amazon's revenue also carries third-party seller sales it does not earn, so on value added HHI would fall further.)

## Read These Three Numbers Before Deciding

Put the three numbers side by side, every cell with its basis.

| Cell | US retail 2025 | Basis |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Scale | Value added $1,810.4 billion | BEA, NAICS 44-45, nominal current-year prices |
| Growth | Nominal +3.9%, real +0.2% | Year-over-year output, real deflated by the output price index |
| Concentration | CR4 84.6%, HHI 2395 (floor) | Denominator is the seven firms' $2,195.44 billion |

**Big, concentrated, and flat: the market has settled into its shape.** Retail is exactly that shape now — $2,945.2 billion of output, up 3.9% nominally and 0.2% in real terms, four giants holding 84.6%. The opportunity is not in fighting for the total. It is in the gaps between them.

**Small, fast-growing, unconcentrated: the most tempting shape, and the most dangerous.** Fast growth may only mean a small base. Low concentration may mean the industry has not formed yet, and the "unconcentration" is a pile of firms that have not died yet.

**Big and unconcentrated: anyone who enters can get a piece, which also means anyone who enters gets a small piece. And fast growth while concentration rises means the leaders are eating the increment** — enter two years late and the position is gone.

**The growth cell is the easiest to fool yourself with.** Nominal +19.7% may be real +8.0%. Add one scale number with no stated basis and the three steer you anywhere. **Only when all three state their basis is the decision finished.**
