# Eight tools, one table: pick by the question, not by the number

"How much did this channel actually bring in?"

There are eight ways to ask that, and eight tools that answer them. **Mixing up the tools causes more damage than getting the arithmetic wrong.** A wrong number gets questioned. A wrong tool produces a clean-looking number that goes straight into the budget sheet.

The table below puts all eight side by side: what each one does, what goes in, what comes out, and where it is covered. **None of the eight replaces another. Each one owns a different link in the chain.**

## What each tool owns

| Tool | What it does | Input | Output | Where it is covered |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Attribution | Splits an order's credit across its touchpoints | The touchpoint sequence for one order | Each touchpoint's share of every order | [→ Attribution](../../8-analysis-toolbox/01-attribution/en.md) |
| Incrementality test | Measures how many orders disappear if you stop spending | One region held back from ads, plus two sales periods | Incremental sales, clicks, cost | [→ Incrementality testing](../../8-analysis-toolbox/02-incrementality-testing/en.md) |
| MMM contribution | Pushes long-run sales changes back onto each channel | Weekly sales over years, plus channel spend | Each channel's contribution share | [→ MMM contribution](../../8-analysis-toolbox/03-mmm-contribution/en.md) |
| Adstock decay | Carries past spend forward along a decay curve | Daily spend and one decay rate | The accumulated effect series | [→ Adstock decay](../../8-analysis-toolbox/04-adstock-decay/en.md) |
| Hill saturation curve | Describes how returns taper as spend grows | Spend and return by tier | Saturation point k, elasticity α | [→ Hill saturation curve](../../8-analysis-toolbox/05-hill-saturation-curve/en.md) |
| Marginal and average ROI | Separates the backward-looking total from the last dollar | Spend and return by tier | Average and marginal ROI | [→ Marginal versus average ROI](../../8-analysis-toolbox/06-marginal-vs-average-roi/en.md) |
| Equal marginal returns | Decides how to split budget so nothing is wasted | Each channel's marginal curve | The optimal split | [→ Equal marginal returns](../../8-analysis-toolbox/07-equal-marginal-returns-principle/en.md) |
| Confidence intervals and fit | Rates how much a model can be trusted | Model output, sample size | Interval half-width, R² | [→ Confidence intervals and fit](../../8-analysis-toolbox/08-confidence-intervals-and-fit/en.md) |

**The column that matters most is "Output."** The first two output money and orders, the third a percentage, Adstock and Hill curves, marginal ROI and equal marginal returns multiples, the last an interval. **Their units differ, so they cannot replace each other and cannot be added together.** A channel with a 21% contribution share, a marginal ROI of 1.8, and a 73% incremental return are three different things, measured by three different rulers.

**Three kinds of tools, three sources of confidence.** Observational tools (attribution, MMM, marginal ROI) read relationships out of data that has already happened; they are fastest and cheapest, but a relationship you read is not a cause you proved. Experimental tools (incrementality testing) hold back one area and let the fact show up on its own; they are the most trustworthy, they cost money and time, and they answer one question per run. Mathematical tools (Adstock, Hill, equal marginal returns, confidence intervals) produce no numbers of their own — they dictate **how the other tools have to compute.** Without Adstock the MMM contribution is wrong; without Hill the model gets fat at high spend.

**Which one to reach for depends on whether you want money, cause, or confidence.** The three steps below run in that order.

### Looking back: who touched this order

**Attribution answers this step.** The input is the touchpoint sequence for an order; the output is how much each touchpoint gets. **Attribution counts who touched the order, not who created it.** Change the allocation rule and the same batch of orders splits differently — a choice about rules, not a difference in facts. It can tell you what share of these orders came from search. It cannot tell you whether this channel is worth more money.

**This chart carries the most important footnote in the tool table: change the tool, and the answer flips.**

In the eBay 2015 field experiment, brand keyword ads were turned off in randomly selected markets. **99.5%** of the lost clicks were picked up immediately by organic search, and the measured brand keyword return was **−63%** (95% confidence interval −124% to −3%). Read the same data through the observational lens and the return comes out at **1400% or more**.

In the Facebook checkout experiment, the observational lens produced **316%** against an experimental truth of **73%** — an overstatement of more than four times.

**Both cases point the same way: observational readings cannot recover the experimental effect.** Matching, regression correction, propensity scores — the papers have tried them all. **So if the question is "how many new orders did the ads bring," only the incrementality cell can answer it.**
