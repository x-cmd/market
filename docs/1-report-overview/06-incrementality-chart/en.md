# The ROAS on your dashboard is not the return on your next dollar

The dashboard says ROAS 4.0. The platform computed it: orders that touched an ad, divided by ad spend. Spend 30,000, get 120,000 in revenue. That number is real.

The number you actually want is different: **if you stop the 30,000, how much of that 120,000 survives?** The dashboard answers the first question. The second is the reason you bought ads.

Incrementality, in plain terms: **the orders that stop happening when the ads stop.** Orders that would have happened anyway do not count — those buyers had already decided to purchase; the ad only happened to be in front of them when they searched.

## Two ledgers, and where they part

The platform ledger records the whole funnel — impression, click, cart, purchase. Every order that touched an ad goes in, without exception. The incrementality ledger records one thing: the orders that go missing when the spend stops. To compute "how many go missing," you need a **place where no ads ran.** Without that place, the two ledgers never reconcile.

**In 2015, eBay ran a large randomized experiment with three economists.** They split users randomly in half: one group saw search ads, the other did not.

Same ads. Three methods. Three numbers. The most common method is regression — control for as few variables as possible — and it put the return on non-brand search ads above 4100%. Every dollar of ad spend brought back 41 dollars. Add time and geography as controls and it is still above 1400%. The randomized experiment on those same ads said: **ROI was −63%**, with a 95% confidence interval from −124% to −3%. Not sixty times smaller. The **sign flipped.**

## The gap can be enormous

**Facebook ran the same kind of check on itself.** In 2019, Facebook's researchers and colleagues at Northwestern University's Kellogg School of Business took the results of 15 randomized ad experiments and compared them against the observational methods advertisers use day to day.

One campaign is worth following. In Q2 2015, an omnichannel retailer ran ads for 17 days, with 25.5 million users split 70/30 into treatment and control. The metric was checkout-page conversion.

Only 25% of the treatment group actually saw the ad. Those users converted at 0.104%. The other 75%, who saw nothing, converted at 0.020%. The control group saw nothing at all and converted at 0.059%. **The true lift measured by the randomized experiment was 73%.**

Now throw the control group away. That is exactly the position an advertiser is in without an experiment: some of your audience saw the ad and some did not, and all you can do is compare the two. The paper reports that number as **316%** — more than four times the real effect. With Facebook's fullest variables used for exact matching, it drops to 222%. Still three times.

Control group 0.059%. Treatment group users who saw the ad 0.104%. The randomized experiment puts the true lift at **73%**.

The treatment users who did *not* see the ad converted at 0.020% — below the control group. These are people randomized in who happened not to be exposed. Same pool as the control group, but the two groups still differ in composition.

**That gap is the whole point.** People who saw the ad converted better, but not entirely because of the ad — they were already likelier to buy. A randomized experiment separates the two effects because not a single person in the control group saw an ad. Without an experiment, the gap between the unexposed slice and the control group gets folded into the ad's credit.

### Exposure is not random, and that is the source of the gap

People who see an ad are already more likely to buy. They are more likely to be on mobile, more likely to be married, more likely to be women. In the advertiser's dashboard none of that is visible — you do not know who saw the ad. Regression corrects part of it. The rest stays in the number.

Compare physical stores and you know which city each one is in; run a price A/B test and you know who landed in which arm. With ads you hold orders but no record of who saw what, so every unmeasured difference between those two piles ends up credited to the ad.

### So where exactly do the two ledgers differ?

The −63% from eBay does not mean search ads are useless. Split the paper's finding in two. Brand keyword ads — the ones you buy on searches that already contain your name — have **almost no measurable short-term return.** You are paying for people who were going to search your name anyway. Non-brand keywords do have a positive effect, but it comes mostly from new and infrequent users; **frequent users took the bulk of the ad spend, and their purchases were not changed by the ads.**

So the question is not "did ads work." The question is **which ads create demand and which ones harvest demand that already exists.** The ratio between the two decides whether next month's budget goes up.

Run your own version. Say you spend 300,000 a month on search ads. If three tenths of that buys people who would have ordered anyway, then 90,000 of it corresponds to revenue you were already going to collect. That is not lost income. It is revenue you booked as incremental and are now about to spend more on. Without a measurement you are guessing, and the cost of a wrong guess is **putting the next dollar into orders that were already going to happen while believing you are acquiring new customers.**

### The platform is not lying to you

The number it reports answers "which orders touched an ad." That question it answers fast and accurately. "How much did the ads create" is a different question, and it needs a different set of tools. Google has geo-lift and Meta has Conversion Lift for that, and neither sits in the dashboard you look at every day.

This is not the platform hiding anything: the data sits with the platform because the platform knows who saw the ad — the one thing you cannot do yourself.

## No control group, no incrementality

**Three things that cost nothing.**

First, **hold back a small control group.** Stopping a whole city is unnecessary — a few cities, one region, one stretch of time is enough. With a control and without one you are in different worlds; for how to build one, see [→ A control group is not merely "the people you did not target"](../../3-experiment-design/01-what-a-control-group-is/en.md).

Second, **do not read two ledgers as one.** The ROI in the platform report and the ROI you measured are not the same number. See [→ Platform metrics and incremental metrics are two different ledgers](../../4-performance-analysis/03-platform-metrics-vs-incremental-metrics/en.md).

Third, **remember which way each method leans.** Observational methods — platform reports, last-click attribution — **overstate.** They lean systematically high, and on brand keywords they turn a negative into a positive.

## Do not read harvesting as creating

The number the platform reports is not fake. The number incrementality produces is not fake either. The mistake is treating them as the same number. How much the ads created stays unknown until you measure it, and even without a measurement there is a floor: do not read harvesting as creating.
