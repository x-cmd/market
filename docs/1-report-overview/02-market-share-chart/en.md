# Two Numbers Called 23% May Not Be the Same Number

"We hold 23%."

For that sentence to mean anything, three things have to be settled: what sits in the **numerator**, who set the **denominator**, and whether you and the number sit **inside the same frame**. Miss one, and the same 23% can point at two unrelated things. Here is the smallest example: the same EU companies, the same year, the same turnover — split two ways.

The top bar reads 19.82%. The bottom reads 8.49%. **The denominator is identical**: total turnover for those enterprises. The entire 11.33-point gap lives in the numerator. The upper figure counts sales to wholesale distributors; the lower one counts only sales to consumers. The distance between two figures both called 23% can be a factor of two.

## A bar chart answers who leads — and hides what it divides by

Market share charts are the most basic chart in any market report. Bars side by side, longest wins, and you see your tier instantly. A boss asks "where do we rank?" An investor asks "who controls this market?"

Drawing it is the easy part. The hard part is **what the percentage was divided by**. The bar length is visible; the denominator is not.

## What the share is measured against

The 19.82% numerator is turnover from web sales: sales to consumers, but also to wholesalers, convenience stores, and business customers. The denominator is total enterprise turnover, covering companies with 10 or more employees in NACE sectors C-E (wholesale and retail, accommodation and food services, transport). The 8.49% figure keeps the denominator and strips out business-to-business (B2B) sales, leaving only sales to consumers (B2C).

**One denominator, two numerators, 11.33 points apart.** The gap widens by 2030: the all-in measure climbs from 19.82% to 41.40%, the B2C measure from 8.49% to 26.70%. One party quotes 19.82%, another quotes 8.49%, and **both are right**. What is wrong is setting them side by side and claiming the share doubled.

### Hungary's 30 points

Hungary pushes the effect to its extreme. In 2024, web sales were **30.85%** of Hungarian enterprise turnover — eleven points above the EU's 19.82%.

**But only 0.92% of that 30.85% went to consumers.**

Out of every 100 units of Hungarian enterprise turnover, 31 come from web sales. Of those 31, **3 reach end consumers**. The other 28 are wholesale — sales to convenience stores, shops, and other businesses. Those are real transactions, but they are closer to the plumbing of supply chains than to retail ecommerce.

Compare Cyprus, which runs web sales at 34.84% — higher than Hungary — but whose B2C share is 31.62%. **Two countries in the same statistical table, one at 3%, one at 91%.** The commercial structures are not that different; the word "ecommerce" covers two different sets of transactions in each.

## Where the number can mislead you

### The edges of the frame

Every number above carries a frame: **10 or more employees, NACE sectors C-E**. Nothing outside it gets counted — not a three-person shop, not a software company, not a pure logistics firm.

So "we hold 23%" also has to answer **whether you are inside that frame**. A company outside it cannot be compared to this 23% — not because it is smaller, but because it never shared a denominator. And **someone else set the denominator.** You can see your own turnover; you cannot see the rule deciding who counts around it.

### One metric, three shapes

Germany's web sales share went **27.69% → 15.96% → 42.01%** over 2023, 2024, and 2025.


A drop from 27.69% to 15.96%, then a jump to 42.01%. **No real economy does that.** Ecommerce penetration in a large country does not halve and then double in two years. This is a survey-method artifact: the sampling frame changed, response rates changed, and firms' willingness to report fluctuates year to year. Eurostat itself notes that 2025 coverage differs from 2024.

Spain tells a different story: it climbs from 20.94% in 2019 to 31.64% in 2023 and 31.81% in 2024, then eases to 29.8% in 2025. **That shape is credible** — a single dip, the earlier direction intact. The EU overall wobbles between — 24.86% in 2018, 19.82% in 2024, 28.5% in 2025.

**Germany's shape says one thing: this number cannot be read from a single year.** A single year may be the shape of the sampling frame rather than the shape of the market. Spain's climb is the business.

## Three sentences that make the number defensible

A market share chart holds up only if it answers three questions.

**Who set the denominator?** Not "the market," not "the industry" — "total turnover of enterprises with 10 or more employees in NACE sectors C-E." Name it down to the statistical definition, and someone else can reproduce it.

**What is in the numerator?** All web sales, or only sales to consumers.

**Am I inside the same frame as it?** The one that gets skipped most often, and the most dangerous. Your company, your competitors, and your denominator share one frame.

**With those three answers, your 23% becomes "within enterprises of 10 or more employees in NACE sectors C-E, counting web sales to consumers, we hold 23%."** The second half is verbose. Without it, the first half is a sentence nobody can check.

## The number you publish is a 23% inside some frame

"We hold 23%" was never a statement about fact. It was a statement about definition. **You did not say it wrong. You did not finish it.**

For anyone writing reports, that has an immediate use: **when someone challenges your 23% with a different 23%, you do not have to change your number. You have to find out what frame theirs is in.**
