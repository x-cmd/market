# Two companies cannot be compared until their numbers mean the same thing

Amazon's 2025 net sales were 716,924 million dollars. Walmart's FY2026 total revenue was 713,163 million dollars. Amazon's book revenue was 3,760 million dollars larger. On the street, everybody would have told you Walmart is far bigger.

Both numbers are labeled "revenue" and both are correct, but they do not count the same thing. More than half of Walmart's 713,163 million is food and grocery. 128,725 million of Amazon's 716,924 million is AWS, the cloud business that sells computing power instead of products. A head-to-head table has three columns — **size**, **share**, **growth** — and whether it is worth anything depends less on your arithmetic than on whether the two numbers were measured the same way.

## Every company leads by the measure it picked

Each company holds a filing that flatters it. Walmart says it is the largest retailer in the United States. Amazon says it is the largest e-commerce platform. Neither statement is false, and neither measures the same thing as the other.

The size column alone has more than one conversion: Walmart's FY2026 net sales are 706,413 million against total revenue of 713,163 million, and the 6,750 million difference is membership and other income — Sam's Club and Walmart+ dues. It counts as revenue, but no goods are sold for it. On Amazon's side, AWS is 128,725 million, or 17.9% of net sales, and sells no physical product at all. "Which one is bigger" is therefore a broken question: there is no single true value waiting to be found, and the answer depends on which cell serves as the denominator.

Three alignment rules make it usable. Size aligns on **whether the two sell the same category of thing**. Share aligns on **who the denominator is**. Growth aligns on **the start and end points, and the base they start from**. Underneath all three: **every number traces back to an original disclosure, and carries the date it was measured as of.** A cell that will not trace back stays empty.

## Mismatched definitions make two numbers incomparable

Amazon's 716,924 million of net sales splits into eight reported lines: 269,287 for its own online stores, 172,162 for third-party seller services, 68,635 for advertising, 49,619 for subscriptions, 128,725 for AWS, and more. Walmart's 706,413 million includes 482,975 for Walmart U.S. (285,482 of it grocery) and 130,423 for International, with online sales listed separately at 99,600 and 35,800.

**Amazon has no revenue line called "e-commerce."** Its online stores, third-party seller services, advertising and subscriptions are all online, and all reported separately. Walmart has a single 135,400 line for e-commerce sales instead. "Who is the larger online retailer" therefore depends on which of Amazon's lines you count.

One company, one year, and the word "online" produces three numbers: 269,287 million, 464,010 million, and 582,264 million. The spread is more than a factor of two, and the whole difference comes from what you put in the basket. Against Walmart's 135,400 million, Amazon is 1.99 times larger, or 3.43 times, or 4.30 times — all three correct, because none measures the same thing.

A subtler error hides here: **third-party seller services is not GMV.** "GMV" is the gross merchandise volume, the total value of goods transacted on a platform. The 172,162 million is Amazon's revenue from fees and services on third-party sales, not the value of the goods. Walmart's marketplace revenue sits inside the 135,400 and cannot be pulled out. **So that cell stays empty.** Sam's Club U.S. raises the same question: it sits inside the Walmart U.S. segment with its own e-commerce sales of 15,000 million, and the 10-K never says whether the 99,600 million includes it. **When the filing does not say what a number covers, do not merge it.**

## Growth only counts when both sides cover the same months

The first trap: **the two fiscal years do not cover the same period.** Amazon's 2025 runs 2025-01-01 to 2025-12-31; Walmart's FY2026 runs 2025-02-01 to 2026-01-31. They overlap for 11 of 12 months, and the extra month sits on opposite ends: Amazon has an extra January 2025, Walmart an extra January 2026. The holiday season lands on Walmart's side and inside Amazon's 2025. On calendar years: **Walmart FY2026 ≈ calendar year 2025.**

Walmart's 2020 bar of 66.0% does not mean Walmart got stronger. The pandemic pushed shoppers online and raised the base for the year after. Amazon runs the other way: its own online store revenue in 2022 was 220,004 million, a touch below the 222,075 million of 2021, or -0.9% year over year. A high base was pressing down on it, not a deteriorating business. **The answer to "who is growing faster" depends on the year you start from, and on whether that year was itself unusual.** When the endpoints do not line up, what you produce is a juxtaposition of unrelated windows, not a comparison.

## One company leads on share, the other on growth

**Share**, online sales over each company's own net sales at its fiscal year end: Walmart 135,400 / 706,413 = **19.2%**. Amazon reads 37.6% counting only its own online stores, 64.7% adding third-party seller services and physical stores, and 81.2% adding advertising and subscriptions.

**Growth**, most recent fiscal year: Walmart online +24.4%, Amazon's own online stores +9.0%. **Size**: Amazon 269.3 billion, Walmart 135.4 billion. Each company wins a different column: **Amazon's business is twice the size of Walmart's and growing more slowly; Walmart's is half the size and growing much faster.** Those are two questions, not a contradiction — one asks where a company stands today, the other where it is heading. **Put both into one word like "stronger" and there is no answer left.**

Most of Amazon's 81.2% is not money from selling goods. It is advertising and subscriptions, 118,254 million together — neither much of an e-commerce business, though both are generated on Amazon's website and app. **Including them makes Amazon's online share look far better.** That is a choice about definitions, not a fabrication, but it belongs in writing.

## Align first, then compare, and call the leftovers what they are

A usable table carries three things in every cell: what the numerator contains, who the denominator is, and the date it was measured as of.

| | Walmart | Amazon |
|---|---|---|
| Size | Total revenue 713,163 (FY2026, as of 2026-01-31) | Net sales 716,924 (2025, as of 2025-12-31) |
| Share | Online 135,400, 19.2% of net sales | Own online stores 269,287, 37.6% of net sales |
| Growth | Online +24.4% | Own online stores +9.0% |
| Excluded | Membership income 6,750 | AWS 128,725 |
| Left empty | Global marketplace revenue | U.S. online store revenue |

The two companies' book revenue is nearly identical, a 0.5% difference, but they sell almost disjoint things, so "who is bigger" **cannot be answered** until you say whether you are comparing retail or the whole company. On retail, Amazon has the bigger online business, Walmart the faster growth — and that edge holds only from a 2020 base.

Two cells are empty for a reason: Walmart does not break out global marketplace revenue, and Amazon does not break out revenue from its U.S. online stores. **A cell that will not fill stays unfilled.** An estimate dropped into a competitor table turns into fact the next time it is cited.
