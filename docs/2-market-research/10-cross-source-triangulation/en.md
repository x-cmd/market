# Two numbers that disagree tell you more than one number that looks right

One earnings report says the quarter brought in $201.9 billion. The three segments in the same document add up to $202.0 billion. Another report calls the year's revenue "flat" — and the second half of that sentence says revenue fell 2% on a constant-currency basis.

Nobody made an arithmetic error. Every number sits on unwritten definitions: its denominator, its coverage, its time window. **Cross-checking two sources is not about finding the one that is truly right. It is about locating where the definitions diverge.**

## A gap of several points usually means the definition changed

**Do not ask who is right first. Ask what each number is measuring.**

For FY2026, NIKE filed two documents: the earnings release (8-K exhibit 99.1) and the tagged financial data in the 10-K (XBRL). Both report full-year revenue of $46.398 billion. The release puts it plainly: "revenue was flat, down 2% on a constant-currency basis."

Push the same two definitions down to the segment level and the gap widens. EMEA reads +1% on the reported basis and −5% on the constant-currency basis. Same segment, same fiscal year: one says it grew, the other says it shrank. The cause is the exchange rate — most of NIKE's European revenue is booked in euros and pounds, which depreciated against the dollar in FY2026. The **reported basis** answers "how many dollars did the company book this year"; the **constant-currency basis** answers "if rates had not moved, would we have sold the same amount". Mix the two and one segment grows and shrinks at once.

### When the gap is a factor, the denominator changed

How big is US retail? Three official numbers exist. The Census Bureau put 2025 retail sales at **$8,351.0 billion**. The Bureau of Economic Analysis (BEA) put retail trade gross output at **$2,945.2 billion** and retail trade value added at **$1,810.4 billion**.

Three numbers for one row of the economy, and the largest is **4.6 times** the smallest. Census retail sales count what retailers took in, so a coat sold by a brand gets counted at the store, again at headquarters, and again at the wholesaler. BEA gross output strips those steps out; what remains is what the retail step itself created. **A gap of 30%, a factor of four, a factor of six — none of it means somebody lied. ## Two matching numbers can still be wrong

**Two sources landing on nearly the same figure usually feels reassuring. Sometimes that is exactly when to worry.**

Eurostat's 2024 e-commerce survey gives two: web sales at **19.82%** of total enterprise turnover, and sales to consumers alone at **8.49%**. Not double counting — two numerators inside one survey, the first including B2B and the second keeping only B2C.

Apply the test to a second source. Suppose the first is your own backend at 19.82% and the second an industry association reporting 18%. You would relax. But both probably draw on the same firms and questionnaire — **agreement between them only shows they share an upstream.** A real second source has an independent definition: different data, not the same data run differently.

Amazon's Q3 FY2026 release lists North America $112.9 billion, International $43.1 billion and AWS $46.0 billion, against a headline of $201.9 billion — **the three add to $202.0 billion, $1 billion more.** Not rounding: AWS sells cloud services to Amazon's own North America and International segments, so the segment table counts that revenue on both sides and consolidation eliminates it. Both figures are right. **When the same quantity appears twice, check whether it is the same quantity.**

## Pick a second source that fails differently

**Do not look for a more authoritative one. Look for one that errs somewhere other than where your current number errs.** If the second source uses the same method as the first, the two are wrong together or right together, and the cross-check is wasted.

A 2015 paper in *Econometrica* (Blake, Nosko and Tadelis) put three numbers on the return from eBay's branded keyword ads: a plain regression at **4100%**, one adding time and region controls at **1400%**, and a randomized experiment at **−63%** (95% CI: −124% to −3%).

**The spread between these three is wider than in any example above, and all three measure the same advertising spend.** The first two compare people who saw the ad against people who did not, but platforms serve ads to people already inclined to buy — selection bias. The third assigns people at random to two groups identical apart from the ad. −63% means that in the eBay case, turning off branded keyword ads saved more than it earned. Facebook's experiment (Gordon et al., 2019) says the same: 316% observational, 73% randomized.

> A second source that is the same data run through a different model cannot find that model's bias. The second source has to be independent in **method**.

Three criteria for choosing one: an **independent definition** (the denominator, coverage and population counted cannot be the same); an **alignable time window** (a quarter, a fiscal year, a calendar year, a rolling twelve months); and a **known direction of bias** (surveys overstate heavy users, censuses miss the smallest operators). Knowing the direction is what lets you use it as a ruler.

## When they do not match, check the definition first, then the timing

**The order matters. Check the definition first, then the timing, and only at the end check whether somebody made an error.** Reverse the order and you spend a day investigating someone who did nothing wrong.

**Denominator, coverage, population.** The 4.6-times gap in US retail sat entirely in the denominator and the population.

Eurostat's two own sources for EU retail growth in 2022 disagree: national accounts (`nama_10_a64`) put retail trade value added at **+5.24%**, short-term business statistics (`sts_trtu_m`) put the turnover index at **+7.97%**.

A gap of 2.7 points, both official, both describing "EU retail in 2022". National accounts work backwards from filings and administrative records, reaching what the short survey cannot; short-term statistics ask firms what they sold this month, so small firms drop out more often. **The question: are these two counting the same thing over the same population?**

**Then the timing: end-of-period snapshot or a period average? Cumulative or current-period value?** Take BEA retail trade gross output. Two algorithms give two growth rates for 2025. The annual series (`44RT-A`) goes from $2,834.1 billion to $2,945.2 billion, **+3.92%**; the quarterly series (`44RT-Q`) goes from $2,897.6 billion to $2,980.8 billion, **+2.87%**. The first compares averages of four quarters; the second compares fourth quarter to fourth quarter.

**Ask three questions:** is the figure an instant at the end of the period or an average across it, is it cumulative or single-period, and is growth measured against the same period last year or the period before? Amazon's Q3 release holds a ready-made example: net sales were "up 12%" year over year, and the 2026 Prime Day moved into Q2 while the whole of the 2025 event sat in Q3 — **excluding that shift, the gain would have been roughly 400 basis points higher.** Same quarter: 12% and about 16%. One follows the calendar, the other aligns the event. **When timing is off, the symptom is usually "right direction, wrong magnitude".**

## The second source tells you how the number can be wrong

**A second source on every key number exists so that you notice when that number is wrong.**

If the second source always agrees with the first, you feel safe while holding nothing but a pleasant confirmation. The useful practice is the opposite: **write down how far apart you expect the two sources to be, and in which direction, before you look.** Knowing that Census and BEA retail figures differ by a factor of several, you look for a second denominator before repeating any "retail as a share of GDP" claim.

**The gap between expected and actual is the information.** A smaller gap means the second source is not independent enough; a larger one means something is there you have not seen yet.

**A second source is not decoration. It turns "I thought this was right" into "I know how this number can be wrong".**
