# Umami leads on forks among open-source web analytics tools, and sits second on stars

Picking a web analytics tool for a self-hosted deployment? The shortlist is not hard: umami, PostHog, Matomo, Plausible, Fathom, GoatCounter, Countly, Ackee, Pirsch.

The tempting way to compare them is to look at one number, the GitHub star count. A star is a bookmark — a signal about how many people talk about a tool. **But for self-hosting, forks say more.** A star means "I might use this later". A fork means "I am changing this right now".

Set the two numbers side by side for all nine projects and umami moves.

umami has **8,501** forks. Second place, PostHog, has 3,605 — umami has **2.4 times** that. It is nearly three times Matomo's 2,928 in fourth place, and more than one hundred sixty times Pirsch's 51 in last place.

Second on stars, first on forks. That combination says: **a lot of people run it, and a lot of people are changing it.** For self-hosted selection this is the most concrete signal available — at some point you will change something (pipe in your own events, reshape a dashboard, add an internal metric), and the more people who have already made that kind of change, the fewer traps are waiting for you.

## The license column decides for some teams

umami is MIT with 39,983 stars. Plausible is AGPL-3.0 with 29,794. Matomo is GPL-3.0 with 22,012. **The highest-starred MIT-licensed project is umami** — and if your legal team is uncomfortable with AGPL, this single column settles the shortlist.

The scraping itself exposed another difference: some sites put prices in the HTML (`curl` with a browser user-agent gets them), others render them with JavaScript (which means a real browser). **Whether a vendor's pricing can be read by a script is itself part of the hidden cost of a self-hosted option.**

## What the numbers say about choosing

When choosing a self-hosted web analytics tool, stars measure attention, **forks measure how many people actually change the code**. umami is the only project that leads on both counts — first on forks, first on stars among MIT-licensed tools — with a star count in second place less than seven hundred off the leader.
