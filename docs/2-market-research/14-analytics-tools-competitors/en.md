# Forks, Not Stars, Tell You Which Web Analytics Tools People Actually Change

Nine open-source web analytics tools, one fork count, one star count. umami has 8,501 forks — more than twice what the runner-up has, and more than a hundred sixty times what the last-placed tool has. Its star count, 39,983, trails the leader by less than seven hundred.

The two numbers point at different things, and for anyone running one of these on their own server, only one of them describes what happens after install.

Stars measure attention. Forks measure work.

A star is a bookmark. It says "I might use this one day." A fork is a copy of somebody's repository in your own account, which says "I am working on this right now." For software you run yourself, the second number is the one with information in it. You are not installing a sealed product. You are installing a codebase that someone else has already bent to fit their own events, their own dashboards, their own deployment.

At **8,501** forks, umami leads the field. PostHog, in second place, has 3,605 — umami has **2.4 times** that. Matomo's 2,928 in fourth place is nearly three times smaller. Pirsch's 51, in last place, is a hundred sixty times smaller still.

One caveat on the fork number, because it overstates the work: a fork is a cloned repository, and people clone without ever pushing a commit. The count includes repos that were forked once and never touched again. It is not a headcount of active contributors.

The caveat does not explain away umami's lead. PostHog has the second-highest fork count at 3,605 and by far the highest star count. If the fork column were mostly abandoned clones, umami's 8,501 would put it at the top of a noise distribution, and no clone-and-abandon population looks like that. What 8,501 forks says is that people copy this code and then work on it.

Second on stars, first on forks by a wide margin. The pairing says **many people run it, and many of them have already changed it.** That is the most concrete signal available for a self-hosted pick, because you will end up changing something: pipe in your own events, reshape a dashboard, add one internal metric. Every fork out there is one more person who has hit that wall and left a trace of the workaround.

## The license column can end the search on its own

The license reads MIT for umami at 39,983 stars, AGPL-3.0 for Plausible at 29,794, GPL-3.0 for Matomo at 22,012. **The highest-starred MIT-licensed project in the set is umami.**

If your legal team is uncomfortable with AGPL — and copyleft licences carry obligations when a modified version is offered over a network — that one column settles the shortlist without any further argument. Fathom and Ackee are also MIT, but with 8,034 and 4,722 stars they are a different conversation from a tool with 39,983.

**Four rows stay blank. No estimated figures are filled in.** The blanks are a retrieval result, not a missing price. Plausible and PostHog publish prices; the pages render them with JavaScript, so a plain fetch returns an empty shell. GoatCounter and Ackee returned nothing parseable at all.

The billing units are not the same, and that trips people up. umami bills by events, Fathom by pageviews, Countly by Flex seats. **A "$20" and a "$20" are not the same thing.** Even with all nine prices in hand, you have to line up what is being counted before any comparison is honest.

The scraping itself turned up a difference worth recording. Some vendors put prices in the HTML — `curl` with a browser user-agent is enough. Others render them client-side, which means a real browser. **Whether a vendor's pricing can be read by a script is itself part of the hidden cost of running the tool yourself**, and it is also a proxy for how hard the product's own admin surface will be to automate.

## Which number to look at, and what to write down when a cell is empty

Two numbers, two questions. Stars answer "is this popular?" Forks answer "is this popular with people who run it and change it?" If you will install a tool and never touch it, stars plus the recent-push column will do. If you will modify it, forks carry the information.

Only umami leads on both counts in this set. It is first on forks by a factor of 2.4 over second place, and first on stars among MIT-licensed tools with a star count less than seven hundred off the outright leader.

Then there is the column nobody filled in. Four of nine pricing pages could not be retrieved at all. That is not the data being absent — it is the retrieval method being wrong. Write that down in the entry rather than dressing up a guess as a fact.
