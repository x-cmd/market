# A competitor profile nobody can check is worth nothing

One column of the competitor sheet is empty. Every other column is filled in. You scan it in the meeting, see the blank, and move on.

Six months later someone asks how big this company actually is. You pull up the sheet: the column is still empty. The numbers next to it are tidy, and nothing in them says when they were taken or on what basis they were counted.

The empty cell is not the real problem. The problem is that nothing in the sheet says which cell to trust. The name is there, the share cell is blank, and the half that exists carries no dates — which means the half you have is the half nobody can use.

Three things make a competitor profile other people can check and update: where each number came from, what date it is current as of, and how it was calculated.

## A half-filled sheet fails because its cells do not agree

**The problem is not missing cells. It is that the cells have no shared convention.**

The map below is the eight-cell schema this page fills in. Leaving a cell empty is not an error; leaving a cell without a source and a date is.

## What goes in each cell, and what it says

This page works through four website analytics tools: umami, Matomo, PostHog and Plausible. All four are open source, sell a hosted version and have public GitHub data — the right set for exposing the hardest cells.

|---|---|---|---|---|
| Entity | umami-software | Matomo (France) | PostHog Inc. | Plausible (11 people) |
| Category | Self-hosted website analytics | Self-hosted + hosted | Product + website analytics | Privacy-first analytics |
| Size | Not disclosed | Not disclosed | Not disclosed | Not disclosed |
| Growth proxy | 1406 commits | 1065 | 41943 | 610 |
| Price band | $0/$20/$200 per month | Cloud €29, self-hosted from €230 | Not readable from public pages | $9/$14/$19 per month |
| Ad intensity | No public signal | No public signal | No public signal | No public signal |
| Share of voice and sentiment | Not done | Not done | Not done | Not done |
| Source and date | GitHub API + official pricing | Same | Same | Same |

Two cells are worth reading closely. The "Growth" cell does not hold revenue growth — it holds commit counts over the past 12 months, which measures development activity, not business growth, and without the definition spelled out it gets read as revenue growth. And PostHog's price cell is empty while the other three are filled: its pricing page renders in the browser, so a static fetch only gets the skeleton. One blank cell beats one guessed number.

## Every cell has to name where it came from

**A number on its own says nothing. Its source says everything.**

Share needs a denominator. A price band needs the date it was taken, the channel and the product form. Ad intensity has to be labelled as a public signal or an estimate.

Look at the Matomo row: Cloud €29, self-hosted from €230. The same "Matomo", two price bands almost eight times apart. The hosted plan is someone else running the software, so the price is a service fee; the self-hosted plan is a licence, so the price is a buyout plus maintenance. Writing "Matomo €29" alone sells one thing and thinks of another.

### The three cells that get muddy fastest

Three cells get muddy fastest:

- **Size** — how big the opponent is. Revenue comes first; where it is unavailable, use public transaction values, store counts or headcount, and state which segment was measured and when.
- **Ad intensity** — how hard the opponent is pushing on advertising. Public signals include creatives in ad libraries, campaign plans in tender documents, or sales spend in earnings reports; an estimate works backwards from the industry average cost per acquisition. All four cells are empty because all four companies sell developer tools, mostly self-serve and paid, and no public channel shows their ad spend. "Not done" beats the industry average, which gets taken for this company's number.
- **Share of voice and sentiment** — how the outside world talks about the company: search interest, social mentions, the share of positive versus negative reviews. Free tools have one property: GitHub issue and fork counts are a kind of volume, though they cover only users willing to speak in a public repository.

The number fills the cell, but three things go alongside it: the window it covers (2025-11-01 to 2026-11-01), what is counted (commits to the main repository), and what it does not stand for (not revenue, not users). It is a proxy for development activity, and the mistake is reading a proxy as growth itself.

## Every cell carries the date it was current as of

**The date is not decoration. It is part of the number.**

A star count is a snapshot. 39990 today, possibly 39993 tomorrow. The date is both part of the value and the evidence that it has gone stale.

Do not delete stale cells. Once they are gone, nobody can tell "we checked and there is nothing" from "nobody checked yet" — the first needs no more work, the second goes on the schedule.

The lowest band to the highest spans a factor of 25. That spread hides three qualifiers — product form, region and billing unit. Leave one out and the chart gets read as "Plausible is 7× cheaper than Matomo".

### The entity cell has its own trap

"umami" is a GitHub repository name, not a company name. Behind it sits the umami-software organisation, and the entity is an individual developer. PostHog is PostHog Inc. Plausible is an 11-person self-funded team. Matomo is a French company running two lines, Matomo and InnoCraft. Get the entity wrong and every price and count underneath may hang on the wrong company.

## Leave it empty rather than fill in an estimate

**A false precise number is far more dangerous than an honest blank.**

A blank tells the next reader: nobody looked, or looking found nothing. An estimate cannot.

"Size" is empty for all four. Why not pull a market share out of an industry report, or multiply users by an average revenue per user? Those numbers have sources, but they are not these four companies' sources. Drop one in and nobody will chase the report's assumptions. They will see "Matomo market share 3.2%" and compare it against other numbers.

PostHog's empty price cell works the same way: other ways to confirm the price exist, but as long as the method is not written down, that cell stays empty. Writing "not available from public sources (pricing page renders client-side, fetched 2026-11-09)" is worth more than a number.

An empty cell is information, not a defect. Which cells are empty says where the gaps in your knowledge are, and where somebody else can pick up the work.

## Sources, definitions and dates are what make a profile checkable

**A competitor profile other people can check and update rests on three things: source, definition and date.**

Source, definition and date are the actual content of this table. Delete the value column and the table still works. Delete those three and the table is scrap.

The GitHub row shows this best. stars 39990, forks 8504, open issues 150, licence MIT, last push 2026-11-07 — five numbers from one API call, and anyone running the same command gets the same result. That is what "checkable" means.

The next update touches two things only: the values and the as-of dates. Source and definition stay put, so two people can hold the old snapshot next to the new one and see whether the company moved.

The value of a competitor profile is not in the day you read it. It is in whether you still dare to check it against the record three months later.
