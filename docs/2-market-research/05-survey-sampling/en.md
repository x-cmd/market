# A survey's credibility is decided by how the sample was drawn

A survey lands on your desk: clean charts, tidy conclusions, cheap quote. Before reading any of it, ask two questions. Who got drawn? And how many were willing to answer? Those two answers set the range in which its conclusions can be true.

## A flattering result can mean the sampler hid something

**"83% of users say they would recommend us" — the word "users" comes from the sampling frame, not the questionnaire.**

A **sampling frame** is the list of people you plan to draw from. People on it are in-frame; people off it are out-of-frame. Whoever the list misses, the survey never reaches. That is coverage bias.

Eurostat runs an annual ICT survey for enterprises, asking each how much of its turnover comes from online sales. Across the EU, the 2024 figure is **19.82%** — but the frame only covers enterprises with 10 or more employees. How many have fewer than 10? Eurostat's Structural Business Statistics (SBS) keeps the same books. Across EU 27, wholesale and retail plus motor trades (NACE G45), there were **836,811 enterprises** in 2020, of which **787,565** had 0 to 9 employees — **94.12%**. The survey asked the other 5.88%.

So a "19.82%" figure rests on a denominator that drops a fifth of EU wholesale-and-retail turnover — and small shops are the ones most likely to have no webshop at all. **What the frame leaves out is the small, marginal, cheap end. Every share on this base reads high; every growth rate reads low.**

## Four ways to draw a sample, four different populations

| Method | Who gets drawn | Cost and bias |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Simple random | A lottery over the frame | Slow, expensive; whoever the frame misses is unreachable |
| Stratified | Each stratum weighted by its true share | Needs a reliable stratifying variable; a wrong one corrupts the data |
| Systematic | Every k-th name on the list | A list sorted by anything but randomness biases it |
| Convenience | Whoever is convenient to find | Cheapest; whoever replies most ends up over-weighted |

Stratification costs the least, but stratifying on "do you use the internet" hides the answer in the frame. **A convenience sample is not useless; it is not a population.** It tells you what those respondents think, not what everyone thinks.

## The two traps that catch almost everyone

### Coverage bias: the frame leaves people out

**The frame decides who you can see at all.** No amount of extra sampling brings back the out-of-frame part. Eurostat's frame comes from the business registers kept by each national statistical office, and those registers have coverage problems of their own: micro-enterprise registration rates differ country by country, the informal economy barely registers, and the snapshot lags by months.

### Self-selection bias: respondents and non-respondents differ

**You sent 1,000 questionnaires and got 87 back. Those 87 are not a miniature of the 1,000.**

People who fill in questionnaires and people who refuse are systematically different groups. That is **self-selection bias**, and its direction depends on the question. Ask "are you satisfied?" and the complainers answer, so the result skews pessimistic. Ask "would you recommend us?" and the people still using the product answer, so the result skews optimistic.

One thing amplifies both at once: **reachability**. A convenience sample only reaches people already easy to find — those online, regulars of a particular shop. It trims the out-of-frame group and inflates the share willing to reply.

## A bigger sample is not a more representative one

**"We surveyed a thousand people" describes sample size, not representativeness.**

Sample size controls how small the random error can get. Representativeness is decided by the frame and by who replied. Sample 1,000 people or 100: the count differs by a factor of ten, but the share of owners rather than shop staff does not correct itself because you added nine hundred more. The frame decides that share, and however big Eurostat's survey grows, it never reaches the 94.12% of small enterprises.

Multiplying the sample by ten shrinks the interval to roughly a third. So "sample more" rescues precision, not bias. The remark "our sample was only 200, far too small" can be a real problem hiding a different one: the draw itself was skewed.

## Too few people willing to answer is an alarm

**The response rate is returned questionnaires divided by questionnaires sent.**

AAPOR's standard definition counts more strictly than most, closing off loose calculations that treat a partly completed form, or a refusal, as a completed response. Under any formula the conclusion is the same: **the lower the response rate, the further respondents and non-respondents drift.**

How low is too low? Leave that cell blank. AAPOR's pages sit behind a Cloudflare block, and the Eurostat quality reports for the national ICT surveys are PDF attachments — no structured response-rate table on the public web.

One check needs no outside data. **Look at who did not reply.** You sent 1,000 and got 87 back, so 913 did not reply. Compare the returns (industry, size, region) against the list you sent: the sent list is the frame, the returns are the response outcome, and wherever a layer is visibly off you now know it. This needs the list in hand — throw it away and response bias can never be checked again.

Dropping the response rate from 100% to 10% widens the interval half-width from 1.8 percentage points to 5.5. That is not the full cost. The chart assumes **respondents do not differ from non-respondents**. In reality the extra width does not fit the interval; the gap leaks out as bias instead.

## Ask who was drawn and who answered before reading the result

**Who was drawn?** What is the frame, and who does it miss? For this survey the missing part is 94.12%.

**How many were willing to answer?** How many went out, how many came back, what was the response rate? Do the returns and the sent list look alike?

**What if the answer is "I don't know"?** If the frame cannot be described, or the list is gone, treat that survey as if it does not exist. It may be right, but it cannot promise anything.

**If both questions have answers, go on and read the results.**

Two terms have to be kept apart. The **sampling frame** is who you planned to draw from; whoever it misses, it misses. **Sampling error** is the random chance that the people you did draw differ from the whole. The first is bias, the second is precision. When a survey is not to be trusted, the first is usually what broke.

Two more caveats. **Enterprises with fewer than 10 employees are not in the frame in every country** — the Eurostat survey metadata says that group is covered on a voluntary basis only, so the same 19.82% can cover different populations in different countries. And **the sample changes over time**: enterprises rotate in and out (rotation of enterprises), which makes the time comparability of some indicators "limited". A survey usually asks "how were you a year ago", so self-reported change removes the turnover effect.

## Fix the frame first, then talk about sample size

Once both questions are answered, a study's credible range is settled. A number that can only speak for "the people who filled in the questionnaire" is a communication tool, not a market fact. The difference is not the size of the number, but whether the frame and the response rate were pinned down first.
