# A survey and an official statistic each miss a different half of the market

Two numbers describe the same market. One says 62.35% of people bought something online in the past three months. The other says 94.8% of retail firms employ fewer than 10 people. Both are true, and neither says who it was talking about.

**A survey misses the people who are offline. An official statistic misses the shops that operate in the margins. The two biases point in opposite directions, so the two numbers can correct each other.** Below, one set of retail firms drawn two ways: a share of firms, and a share of turnover.

## When two numbers disagree, find out who each one dropped

**The question is not which is more accurate. The question is what each one failed to cover.**

## Who a survey leaves out

**Who a survey misses matters more than what it reports.** There are three ways to miss people, and the third is the hardest to spot.

### The frame is narrow to begin with

In 2020 the low-education group was at 32.16% and the high-education group at 73.84%, a gap of 41.7 points. By 2025 the lines sit at 39.44% and 79.39%, and the gap is still 40 points. Sampling error moves around year to year, so a gap holding steady for six years is not sampling error. It comes from the frame: people with more education are likelier to be online, and likelier to be drawn.

### The sample ages

A **panel** is a survey that keeps tracking the same people over time. Run long enough, and the sample stops resembling the people out there now.

In 2020, among EU residents aged 75 to 89, only 15.76% had bought anything online; among those aged 25 to 54 it was 72.36%. A panel built early follows a cohort now in their seventies, so its "online buying rate for these people" is not the rate for 30-year-olds today. The number can be right and still describe a frame from ten years ago, so find out when a panel was built and who it follows now.

### The sample leaks

People who keep answering and people who drop out after two rounds differ systematically on some traits. The stayers tend to be more conscientious, have more free time, and lean harder on the internet. Dropout is not random, so a panel drifts toward whoever remained. No figure is published; ask researchers for the response rate and for a comparison of dropouts against stayers.

## Who official statistics leave out

**A census or an administrative record looks complete. It still has a frame.** That frame is drawn by hand: industry codes, size thresholds, a minimum headcount, tax filing requirements, register membership.

### The definition is narrow

Official series state their own coverage boundaries. Eurostat's ICT survey for enterprises (`isoc_ec_evaln2`) has a hard boundary: only firms with 10 or more employees are counted. That is not in a footnote; it is in the series code.

Of the EU's 3.21 million retail businesses, only 5.2% fall inside that frame, and they carry 77.4% of retail turnover. So "online sales are 13.07% of turnover for EU retail enterprises" (2024, NACE G47) describes the firms holding three quarters of the money.

### The margins fall out

One layer deeper, administrative records lose whatever is not properly registered: sole traders, small shops with no tax history, businesses that run on cash.

Two things to confirm: whether it covers "registered enterprises" or "enterprises that exist", and whether the boundary years have a break. Eurostat's structural business statistics (`sbs_sc_sca_r2`) stop at 2020, so EU-wide data after 2021 is empty — not skipped, but the definition changed.

## The two numbers do not lean the same way

**This is where these two kinds of numbers earn their keep: their biases point in opposite directions.**

On the survey side, the bias runs toward overstating the share of people who buy, because people who are never online never enter the sample. On the official side, it runs toward understating the informal economy, because sole traders and cash businesses never reach the register. One leans up, the other leans down.

The two numbers above do not conflict: the household survey counts people, the enterprise survey counts money. Side by side, they get closer to the truth than either alone.

### The bias on the population side can be measured

The EU survey carries an indicator for the share who have never bought online: 18.96% in 2020, 15.25% in 2025. That last figure is 29.06% for the low-education group and 6.11% for the high-education group.

Split by age, the never-bought share in 2025 runs 11.77% for 16 to 24, 9.59% for 25 to 54, 21.96% for 55 to 64, 29.17% for 65 to 74, and 26.31% for 75 to 89. The headline 15.25% is these groups weighted by population.

But a group's share of the sample and its share of the population are two different things. If a group is small in the population, drawing it rarely does little damage. The reverse does not hold: an overweighted group is not corrected by weighting, because weighting needs the true population distribution of each group, and that is what the survey is measuring.

## Which kind of number you are holding

**Take a number and look at where it came from.** Three sources, three directions of bias.

- **Sampled survey**: narrow frame, ageing sample, non-random dropout. It leans toward people more engaged and with more time.
- **Registers and censuses**: narrow definition, thin margins. It leans toward subjects large and formal enough to be filed.
- **Platform logs**: only behaviour on one platform. It leans toward people already on it.

The third is the easiest to get wrong. Platform logs get treated as objective data, yet they know nothing about people outside the platform and nothing about users who churned off it.

The test: was this number asked, counted, or recorded? Asked, look at the frame and response rate. Counted, look at the definition boundary. Recorded, look at where the recording starts.

## When both numbers are wrong at once

**The worst case is not one number being off. It is two numbers leaning in opposite directions and nobody noticing.**

The first case is an ageing sample meeting a narrow definition. An old panel follows people who no longer represent the population, and the official frame is stuck at a size threshold. Each claims to cover "the market". Together they leave a wide strip uncovered: those too small for the official statistics and too absent from the panel.

The second case is one number counted as two. The same survey gets split into "users say they will buy" and "users actually bought", and each is compared against the official statistics. Both come from the same biased sample, so they look like they are talking to the official data while they have never checked each other. This case is more common and harder to spot: two numbers and two sources in one report reads like cross-validation.

## Ask who counted it and who was missed, then ask how accurate it is

**Four questions clear up any number.** Who counted it: asked, counted, or recorded? Who did it miss: a frame has edges, a register has thresholds, a log has its platform boundary. Which way does the bias point: for people, the ones offline; for business, the ones not formal enough. Is there a second source with an independent frame and the opposite direction of bias?

The first three decide whether the number is usable; the fourth decides whether it can be checked. **What you publish is not "the number" but "the number inside a frame", and whoever drew that frame decided which way it leans.**
