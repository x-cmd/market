# One percentage, four different numbers

"Online is 23% of our sales." An investor hears it and asks the only question that matters: 23% of what?

There is no clean answer, because that one sentence can point at four separate numbers — and each of them is defensible on its own.

## A share is three decisions, not one

**The denominator is the hard part. The numerator is easy.**

A share is made of three things: the denominator (whose scale you divide by), the numerator (what you count), and the population the number covers. Each of the three can double the same figure on its own. Most people end up arguing about two of them while thinking they are arguing about the third.

Read "online is 23%" four ways:

- In your own shop, online accounts for 23% of everything you sold.
- In your category, online channels account for 23% of the category's total size.
- Across retail as a whole, online transactions account for 23% of all retail.
- Counting offline as well, online accounts for 23% of the all-channel total.

All four are true. Putting them in one table and ranking them treats four different things as one.

## Change the numerator and the number doubles

**A clean example: the EU's e-commerce share in 2024.**

Eurostat runs an annual ICT survey of enterprises. One of its indicators asks what share of a company's turnover came from web sales. The denominator is total turnover, with a clear boundary: enterprises with 10 or more employees in NACE C-E (wholesale and retail, accommodation and food services, transportation).

Same denominator, same year, same set of enterprises. Change one thing — what goes into the numerator:

- All web sales counted: **19.82%** across the EU.
- Only the part sold to consumers (B2C): **8.49%**.

### Hungary is the row worth stopping on

Hungarian enterprises get 30.85% of their turnover from web sales, and only 0.92% of that is sold to consumers.

The other 30 points are almost entirely wholesalers selling to retailers.

So "Hungary's e-commerce share is 31%" and "Hungarians buy online" are two claims separated by an entire wholesale industry.

This trap is not specific to Eurostat. Any "e-commerce penetration" figure you come across comes with the same first question: is this sales to consumers, or sales to companies?

In Cyprus, 91% of web sales go to consumers. In Hungary, 3%. The first country is a set of retail shops each running their own webshop; the second is a set of wholesalers supplying convenience stores. One is retail e-commerce, the other is wholesale e-commerce, and both sit side by side in reports under the same label, "online share".

## The same metric can jump for different reasons

**There is a second layer to the denominator: you may not be in it at all.**

Look again at the boundary of that denominator:

- Fewer than 10 employees → your company is not in the survey.
- NACE J (information and communication), G (real estate), M (professional services) → not in it.
- Categories that transact mostly offline → statistically nonexistent.

So the "industry online share of 19.82%" someone quotes has nothing to do with you, unless you are inside that box. This is where meetings go wrong. You say "our online share is 40%, well above the industry" without knowing that the box behind "industry 19.82%" does not contain a company your size.

### The more common error: comparing two different kinds of number

"We are 40% online versus 25% for the industry, so we are ahead."

That sentence often does not hold. The denominator behind 40% may be **your own company's sales** — that 40% says "how much of your shop is online", which is a penetration rate. The denominator behind 25% may be **the size of the whole category** — that 25% says "how much of the category is online", which is a market share.

One is a penetration rate. The other is a market share. Comparing them inside one sentence merges two different things. The issue is not whether the denominators cover the same population. It is whether they measure the same kind of thing.

Germany: 12.63% in 2013, 13.66% in 2020, 27.69% in 2023, 15.96% in 2024, 42.01% in 2025.

Spain: 18.0% in 2011, climbing steadily to 31.81% in 2024, 29.8% in 2025.

The EU as a whole: 22.86% in 2011, 28.5% in 2025.

One line climbs steadily, one jumps up and down, one sits in the middle. Watch only your own line and it is easy to read a change in statistical definition as a change in the business. Real demand does not fall from 27.69% to 15.96% inside a year.

Swings that large usually carry more than business in them. Which enterprises answered the questionnaire, which version of the form was used, how many companies were covered — all of it is inside. To tell whether a given stretch is business or definition, check the metadata flags on the series rather than guessing.

### One more numerator difference, more everyday than B2B versus B2C: whether non-buyers count

Some platforms report "conversions driven by ads" with add-to-carts, form fills and page views all counted in. Others count only the completed purchase. The first numerator is much larger.

In the same dashboard, "conversions" and "conversion value" can also come from two different definitions. When reading a report, dig out the definition at that layer instead of staring at the number.

## Three questions that make a share defensible

**Ask three things before you put a number out.**

First, **whose denominator is it?** Whose turnover, whose market size, which year, which enterprises.

Second, **what is in the numerator?** Does it include B2B? Only completed purchases, or also the ones who merely browsed?

Third, **am I inside the same box?**

Run the EU figure through those three.

Whose denominator? Total turnover of enterprises in NACE C-E with 10 or more employees, 2024, EU-wide.

What is in the numerator? Turnover from web sales, counting both B2B and B2C.

Am I in the box? Wholesale and retail with more than 10 employees: yes. Software, even selling enterprise services: no. Eight employees: no.

Answer all three and the 19.82% can be compared. Miss one and it is a fact about someone else's market.

## What you report is "23% inside a box"

A share sits half in the numerator and half in the denominator.

A number can be correct and still not comparable. The same sentence, "online is 23% of our sales", can point at two entirely different things depending on who says it.

You never report "23%". You report "23% inside a particular box". And the box is yours to name.
