# Three numbers decide whether an industry is worth entering

Before you commit to an industry, three numbers matter more than the rest: how big it is, how fast it is growing, and who controls it.

They look easy. They are where most analysis goes wrong. Each has more than one valid calculation, and picking the wrong one raises no error — it flips the answer.

Take EU retail. Turnover was €3.47 trillion in 2024. From 2021 to 2025 it grew 19.5% in nominal terms but only 1.6% in real terms. And enterprises with 250+ employees take more than half the industry's turnover.

## The eight cells a usable industry entry fills

**Size, growth, concentration — and the order cannot be reversed.** Settle what "size" means, then ask whether the market grows, then who owns it. Reversing it causes specific mistakes: growth first, boundary second, and you compare a fast-growing niche against a stalled market; concentration first, size second, and you understate your place among the giants.

| Cell | What it answers | Where it goes wrong most often |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Definition and boundary | Which work counts | Wholesale and retail in one cell |
| Size | How much a year is worth | Turnover and value added mixed |
| Growth | Bigger or smaller than last year | Nominal read as real |
| Concentration | Do the giants decide | Enterprise count read as share |
| Price band distribution | Which price points money sits at | An average, no distribution |
| Seasonality | How big the peak-to-trough swing is | One month vs a different month |
| Sources and definitions | Who measured it, who it covers | Coverage never stated |

- **Retail turnover €3.47 trillion** (2024), NACE G47: goods sold by retailers
- **Wholesale turnover €6.43 trillion** (2024), NACE G46: goods sold to retailers
- **Wholesale + retail combined €11.48 trillion** (2024): the two added together
- **Retail value added €0.59 trillion** (2023): what is left after the goods cost is stripped out

Most of the €6.43 trillion in wholesale is the same merchandise passing through retail a second time: factory to wholesaler, wholesaler to retailer, retailer to consumer — three flows of the same goods, worth three times the money consumers spend in shops. So the combined figure is gross flow, 3.3 times the retail figure. The subtler version: treating value added as turnover when comparing margins. The 17.7% gets read as "gross margin 17.7%", while retail gross margins sit between 30% and 40%.

**The nominal turnover index** looks at money: how much was collected. **The real volume index** looks at goods: how many items were sold, price removed. The gap is the part that came from price.

2022 is the most extreme year: nominal +10.1%, real +1.4%, all 8.7 points of the difference were price. 2023 is worth pausing on: nominal +4.2%, real **−1.9%**. Fewer things were sold, and price covered the gap.

### The same gap is far wider country by country

Same period, same retail industry: Hungary nominal +41.7%, real +0.6%; Estonia nominal +20.2%, real **−7.9%**; Belgium nominal +7.8%, real **−8.2%**; Romania nominal +54.7%, real +14.5%.

Estonia and Belgium look like growing industries on paper. They are shrinking industries. Reporting "retail grew 4.2%" without saying whether that is nominal or real is the same error as reporting "23% share" without saying the denominator.

## How much of the industry the giants decide

Millions of small shops together take less than a quarter of the money; 3,508 companies take half the business. The average firm in the 250+ group turns over €502 million, the average in the 0-9 group €240,000 — a factor of 2,096.

Between 2021 and 2024 the number of firms with 250+ employees rose from 3,379 to 3,508, and their share of turnover went from 48.7% to 50.7%. The 0-9 group fell from 23.1% to 21.5%. The top got heavier in three years — not because one giant swallowed the market, but because large firms multiplied and small ones left.

### Why the CR4 and HHI cells stay empty

The two commonest concentration measures are **CR4** (the share taken by the four largest firms) and **HHI** (the Herfindahl-Hirschman Index, which squares each firm's share and adds them up to show how fragmented the market is: one dominant firm scores near 10,000, thousands of equal firms near zero). Both need company-level shares, and company-level turnover is not published — Eurostat's structural business statistics stop at "grouped by number of employees".

The available data can be pushed into a number, but that number measures "firms with 250+ employees hold 50.7%", which is neither CR4 nor HHI. Labelling it CR4 is fabrication. The cells stay empty, with a note on what would fill them: each company's turnover, from a company database, an industry association roster, or counting them one at a time.

**A cell you cannot fill is more useful when you say why than when you fill it with a wrong number.**

## What the three numbers say about the business

**Three combinations, three kinds of business.** EU retail is the test case for each.

**Big, barely growing, top-heavy** — the most common. The pie is large but no bigger than three years ago, and half of it belongs to 3,508 companies. Growth comes from taking share: every unit you gain comes off someone else.

**Big, growing, unconcentrated** — the comfortable one: a newcomer grows, the market grows, the top is not locked up.

Another common combination is also the dangerous one: **big, growing and concentrated at the same time.** Every headline number looks good, but concentration means the bar has been raised for a newcomer: the slice you can serve is exactly the slice the giants want.

### Direction beats level

A high concentration figure on its own says nothing about whether that is good or bad — the giants might be harvesting, or the industry might naturally be concentrated. But the *direction* is a signal in itself: rising concentration means entry is getting harder and today's opponent is stronger than the one from three years ago. Growth needs the same treatment: is it nominal or real, and is the whole industry growing or only its top?

Put together, EU retail is a €3.47 trillion market that barely grows while the top gets heavier. The pie is big enough, but not getting bigger, and the largest slice already has an owner. The room left sits inside the other 21.5%.

**Size, growth, concentration, definitions. The first three are findings; the fourth decides whether they can be used.**

Same year, same industry, same data — the two lines differ by 19 points. That gap is Christmas.

**Calendar adjustment (CA)** only corrects for months having different numbers of days, so a short February gets a slightly lower index. **Seasonal + calendar adjustment (SCA)** also removes the yearly repeating pattern, flattening the December spike. The two cannot be mixed: December's CA 138.8 against June's SCA 116.2 measures Christmas, not trend.

The more common error is comparing one month with the same month a year earlier, which works only when both numbers are calendar-adjusted and the months are the same length.

## The number you publish carries its own definitions

**The value of an industry entry is not in the number, but in the sentence behind it.**

> EU retail: €3.47 trillion turnover. From 2021 to 2025 it grew 19.5% in nominal terms and 1.6% in real terms. Enterprises with 250+ employees take 50.7% of turnover. CR4 and HHI cannot be computed without company-level share data.

That sentence is usable: anyone reading it knows who measured each number, what it covers and what is missing. Replace it with "the European retail market is large, growing well, and clearly oligopolistic" and everything in it is true and nothing in it can be acted on.
