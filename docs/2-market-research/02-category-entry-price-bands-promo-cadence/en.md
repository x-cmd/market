# The cheapest beef sells 45% of the volume and the priciest sells 26%

A category entry needs four numbers: where your price sits, how often the category discounts, how hard each discount lands, and how far peak season sits from the trough. All four come out of public statistics. This page works through one example: raw beef sold at retail in the US.

**Price and volume do not line up.** The priciest band (steak, $9.68/lb) sells 26% of the volume; the cheapest (ground beef, $5.31/lb) sells 45%.

## Settle the boundary before anything else

The boundary here: **raw beef sold fresh in retail, split into four cut bands** — ground beef, roast beef, steak, and other beef (excluding veal). Three questions get answered first:

- **Group by what is sold, or by how it is sold?** This page groups by what is sold. Group by how it is sold instead (supermarket, e-commerce, restaurant) and one band of beef splits into several price bands, because freight and channel markups differ.
- **Do services and installation count?** Raw beef has no installation fee, but it does have processing services such as grinding the meat for you. For appliances or cars, this single cell decides how many price bands the category has.
- **Do tax and tip count?** US retail prices, tax included, tip excluded.

**Get the boundary wrong and everything downstream is wrong.** The same band of "steak" can carry a price three times higher on a restaurant menu than on a supermarket shelf. That is not a markup problem. It is two different categories.

## Place yourself in the category before you analyze it

The first move with any category is not analysis. It is putting yourself inside it. A band's position comes from one calculation: divide that band's annual household spend by its average unit price, and you get how many pounds a household buys a year. In 2022, US households spent $317 on raw beef and bought 46.0 pounds of it.

| Band | Price per lb | Annual spend per household | Annual volume | Volume share | Spend share |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Ground beef | $5.31 | $110 | 20.70 lb | 45.0% | 34.7% |
| Steak | $9.68 | $117 | 12.09 lb | 26.3% | 36.9% |
| Roast beef | $6.82 | $55 | 8.06 lb | 17.5% | 17.4% |
| Other beef | $6.74 | $35 | 5.19 lb | 11.3% | 11.0% |

**Read across the row and the four bands are not one straight line.** Ground beef, the cheapest, sells 45% of the volume but takes only 34.7% of the money. Steak, the priciest, sells 26.3% of the volume and takes 36.9% of the money. **The priciest band earns 1.8 times more per pound.**

**Read down the column and your position is fixed by three numbers**: price per pound, volume share, spend share. If you sell steak, roughly half of your money comes out of the $9.68 band. Move that band, and you have moved your category.

## Cut the price bands by volume, not by instinct

The usual way to build price bands is to name three tiers — premium, mid, budget — and move on. The problem is that **a tier you invented can hold two bands whose volume differs tenfold.**

The workable cut is by volume, at **volume quartiles**: order the bands by volume from largest to smallest, and the cut points fall where the cumulative share crosses each quartile. The four beef bands land one per quartile: ground beef 45.0%, steak 26.3%, roast beef 17.5%, other beef 11.3%.

Cutting by volume carries one more point: **the order of the bands is not the order of the prices.** Other beef, fourth by volume at $6.74, is eight cents cheaper than roast beef, third by volume at $6.82. **Two bands eight cents apart sit in different tiers** — the 6.2 point gap in volume matters far more than the eight cents.

## How hard the discounts land, in numbers you can predict

A promotion you can forecast needs three numbers: how many a year, how long each one runs, and how deep it goes.

Public data has no cell that states this directly. It can be computed from the CPI price index. A month whose price index sits more than 1% below the trailing 12-month moving average marks the start of a promotion event; consecutive low months belong to the same event. The depth of an event is how far its lowest month falls below that average.

**One caution**: these numbers are read off the price index, not pulled from a promotional calendar. An index drop can mean a retailer discounted, and it can also mean the supply side moved. CPI cannot separate the two.

The four average 0.65 events per year. An entry claiming the category runs "eight big sales a year" is describing a retailer's marketing calendar, not the category's own behavior.

## Where the discounts concentrate tells you what each band is made of

**The deepest discounts land neither on the cheapest band nor on the biggest one. They land in the upper middle.** Steak goes deepest (−3.6%) but discounts least often, 0.5 times a year; roast beef is second deepest (−3.3%) and discounts most often, 0.9 a year; ground beef goes shallowest (−2.3%) despite 0.7 events a year.

**The cheapest band discounts shallowly because cost pins it down.** Ground beef sells for a little over half what steak does, so the room for a discount inside $5.31 is narrow to begin with.

**The priciest band discounts rarely but hard, because it protects its price.** Steak moves only 0.5 times a year, and when it moves it goes to −3.6%. **If steak were on sale every month, $9.68 would not be a price anyone could hold.** Discount frequency is positioning: the lower it is, the more credible the sticker price.

**The middle band is the one that actually lives on promotions.** Roast beef has no floor under it — not like ground beef with its cost cover, not like steak with its brand premium — so it discounts most and needs to clear inventory. Know which cell you land in before you set a price.

## The four cells, and the four you cannot fill

**Boundary** — fresh retail beef, four cut bands, tax included, services excluded.

**Price bands** — volume quartiles: 45.0% / 26.3% / 17.5% / 11.3%. Price order does not match volume order.

**Promo cadence** — 0.65 events a year, 3 to 5 months each, −2.9% average depth. **That is an order of magnitude below "eight big sales a year,"** and once it is in the entry, a lot of other sentences have to change.

**Seasonality** — July peak, February trough, 2.72 points; roast beef 6.8 against ground beef 3.4.

Each of the four can be recomputed by someone else — price bands from volume, promo cadence from the index, seasonality from the monthly series. If a cell fails to reproduce, the entry goes back to the drawing board.

Four cells genuinely cannot be filled.

**The promotion calendar for each band** — the start date, the number of days, the return to full price. Public statistics carry a price index, not a price tag, so the counts and depths on this page are **inferred backwards** from the index.

**Volume share in units per band** — the volume here is pounds, because household spend divided by unit price can only yield pounds. Converting to units needs packaging specs.

**The seasonally adjusted index for the other beef band** — BLS publishes only the not-seasonally-adjusted series (`CUUR0000SEFC04`); `CUSR0000SEFC04` cannot be found, so this band's seasonal factor sits on a different basis than the other three.

**The services and installation cell** — raw beef has none. In another category it cannot stay empty: for appliances, the "purchase price" and the "price with installation included" differ by 15% to 25%.
