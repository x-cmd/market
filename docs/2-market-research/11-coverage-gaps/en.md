# Before you quote a number, find out who it leaves out

The EU's 19.82% for "e-commerce as a share of enterprise turnover" in 2024 has been copied into countless reports and decks. What it omits matters more than what it says: **it covers only enterprises with 10+ employees, and only sectors C through E.**

Split the same retail firms into five size bands and the picture splits with them. The 94.8% with 0 to 9 employees take home 22.6% of turnover; the 0.1% with 250+ take 49.2%. Which slice a number covers decides who it describes.

## The firms this number never reaches

The size class in Eurostat's ICT survey of enterprises (`isoc_ec_evaln2`) has one category: `GE10` — firms with fewer than 10 employees are not in the sample. Cross-check against structural business statistics (`sbs_sc_sca_r2`): in 2020, EU retail had **3,212,640 enterprises**. Only **166,145** had 10+ employees — **5.17%** of firms, holding **77.18%** of turnover.

**A frame holding 5.17% of the firms and 77.18% of the revenue.** Any "e-commerce penetration" number produced inside it describes the larger end of retail. The 94.8% outside is a different business entirely. For "how much room is left in this industry", that 22.6% is not a rounding error. For "how penetrated are the leaders", it has to be excluded deliberately.

## It describes the counted, not everyone

The 19.82 numerator is turnover from web sales; the denominator is total turnover. The frame has four restrictions: 10+ employees, NACE C-E, the EU 27, 2024. Drop any one and a conclusion falls.

A parallel numerator runs the other way. In the same survey, turnover from web sales made to consumers — B2C only — was **8.49%** in the EU in 2024. **19.82 includes B2B and B2G; 8.49 keeps only B2C.** Saying "19.82% of people shop online" tells the 8.49% story at 19.82% volume.

### The frame moves on its own

In the same survey, the EU 27 went from 19.82% in 2024 to **28.5%** in 2025 — 8.7 points. Eurostat warns: **the 2025 survey has different coverage and is not comparable with 2024.**

Single countries make it plainer. The Netherlands: 28.12% in 2023, NA in 2024, 29.91% in 2025. Belgium: NA in 2023 and 2024, 26.46% in 2025. **A country going from "has data" to "no data" and back changes the sample, not the business.**

## The four gaps that get missed most

### Size: what gets missed is "small"

The 22.6% of turnover outside the frame is mostly web shops, small service providers, and local businesses with no premises. The revenue is small; the growth rate is not.

## Which way a gap bends the number

### Share figures: overstated

**When the excluded part has a large denominator and a small numerator, the share comes out too big.** A platform's GMV (gross merchandise value, the total value of transactions on it) counts only what happens there, excluding everything that could not be sold there: small merchants' low-value baskets, local businesses with no storefront. Its share of the whole market is overstated.

### Growth figures: understated

**When the excluded part is growing faster than the frame, the growth rate comes out too small.** A small firm goes from 100,000 to 300,000, a 200% gain. A large firm goes from 1 billion to 3 billion, also 200%. The first is nearly invisible in the total; the second rewrites it with a nudge. **The wider the frame, the easier growth is understated.**

Amounts work the same way: the excluded 22.6% of turnover is **605,562 million euros**. **One number can overstate a share and understate an amount at once.**

## How to mark what you left out

### Put the coverage in the title and the note

Bind the coverage to the number so it travels with it. Not "EU e-commerce is 19.82% of turnover" but "EU enterprises with 10+ employees, sectors C-E, 2024, web sales 19.82% of turnover". **The cost is a few words. The return is that everyone who picks it up knows what question it can answer.** Charts work the same way: put year, frame and scope in the title.

Three labels are enough: **in frame** is what the number measures directly, **at the edge** is adjacent but excluded, **out of frame** is different rules.

### Break the line when the gap is large

The most common move in a time series is to connect the missing years with a line. **That fills in the data visually and invents an observation that never happened.** Draw the EU e-commerce penetration series as one smooth rising line and the reader takes away "steady growth", when the truth is **the frame is changing**.

So break it. Leave the gap blank when it is large, and label the break with the reason ("2024 survey coverage adjusted", "no data this year"). Whitespace makes people ask one more question, and that question is often the one the number most needs. The test: **if a real judgement sits between two points, do not draw a line through it.**

## Whitespace is information, not a defect

**What a chart leaves out is often worth more looking at than what it draws.** On the sector chart, the 19.82% C-E total sits next to industry figures from 38.01% to 90.45%, and reads as "this is Europe". On the geography chart, seven NAs get squeezed out. A chart that says "there is nothing here" is really saying: there is 22.6% of turnover here, sectors differ by more than double, countries by eight times. **What cannot be drawn is not "no data". It is "you get it with a different frame".**

**What you publish is not "the number" but "the number inside a frame".** How much sits outside, who was dropped, which way it leans — write that on the chart, and other people can dare to use it.
