# Every percentage comes from a frame, and the frame is not everything

The EU's 19.82% for "e-commerce as a share of enterprise turnover" in 2024 has been copied into countless reports and decks. What it leaves out matters more than what it says: **it covers only enterprises with 10+ employees, and only sectors C through E.** The part the chart never draws is the part worth checking first.

**The same retail firms, split into five size bands: the 94.8% with 0 to 9 employees take home 22.6% of turnover. The 0.1% with 250+ take 49.2%.** Which slice a number covers decides who the number is about.

## The firms this number never reaches

Eurostat's ICT survey of enterprises (`isoc_ec_evaln2`) has a single size class: `GE10` — firms with fewer than 10 employees are not in the sample. Cross-check structural business statistics (`sbs_sc_sca_r2`): in 2020, EU retail had **3,212,640 enterprises**, of which only **166,145** had 10+ employees — **5.17%** of the firms, holding **77.18%** of the turnover.

**A frame holding 5.17% of the firms and 77.18% of the revenue.** Any "e-commerce penetration" figure produced inside it describes the larger end of retail. The 94.8% outside is a different kind of business — a two-person web shop, a hardware store with no website. For "how much room is left" that 22.6% is not a rounding error; for "how penetrated are the leaders" it has to be excluded.

## It describes the counted, not everyone

**The part of a number most often misread is the denominator, not the numerator.** The numerator behind 19.82 is turnover from web sales; the denominator is total turnover. The frame restricts four things at once: 10+ employees, NACE C-E, the EU 27, 2024. Change any one and a conclusion changes with it.

There is a parallel numerator, and it is more extreme. In the same survey, web sales made to consumers — B2C only — were **8.49%** of total turnover in the EU in 2024. **19.82 includes B2B and B2G; 8.49 keeps only B2C.** Saying "19.82% of people shop online" tells the 8.49% story at 19.82% volume.

### The frame moves on its own

The trouble is the frame shifts by itself. In the same survey, the EU 27 went from 19.82% in 2024 to **28.5%** in 2025, 8.7 points higher. Eurostat has warned about this: **the 2025 survey has different coverage and cannot be compared directly with 2024.**

Single countries show it more sharply. The Netherlands: 28.12% in 2023, NA in 2024, 29.91% in 2025. Belgium: NA in 2023 and 2024, then 26.46% in 2025. **A country moving from "has data" to "no data" and back has changed its sample, not its business.**

## The four gaps that get missed most

**Size, sector, geography and channel are four separate holes.** Any one alone moves the number by more than double; stack all four and the same "e-commerce penetration" runs from 9.66% to 90.45%.

### Size: what gets missed is "small"

The 22.6% of turnover outside the frame is mostly web shops, small service providers and local businesses with no premises. The revenue is small, the growth rate is not, and a frame stopping at GE10 understates exactly that growth.

Platforms hold 10.9% of retail web sales (9.08/83.16); the other nine tenths are own website and app. In retail, B2C is 72.59% and B2BG only 10.57%. ## Which way a gap bends the number

### Share figures: overstated

**When the excluded part has a large denominator and a small numerator, the share comes out too big.** A platform's GMV — gross merchandise value, the total value of transactions running through it — counts only what happens there. What is left out is what could not be sold there: small merchants' low-value baskets, local businesses with no storefront. Its market share is overstated.

### Growth figures: understated

**When the excluded part grows faster than the frame, the growth rate comes out too small.** Small firms start from a low base: 100,000 rising to 300,000 is a 200% gain; large firms go from 1 billion to 3 billion, also 200%. The first is nearly invisible in the total, the second rewrites it with a nudge. **The wider the frame, the easier growth is understated.** Amounts bend the same way: the excluded 22.6% of turnover is **605,562 million euros**. **One number can overstate a share and understate an amount at once.**

## Two ways to mark what you left out

### Put the coverage in the title

Bind the coverage to the number so it travels with it. Not "EU e-commerce is 19.82% of turnover" but "EU enterprises with 10+ employees, sectors C-E, 2024, web sales 19.82% of turnover". **A few words of cost, and everyone who picks it up knows what it answers.** Charts work the same way: put year, frame and scope in the title. Three labels are enough: **in frame** is what the number measures directly, **at the edge** is adjacent but excluded, **out of frame** is a different set of rules.

### Break the line when the gap is large

The commonest move in a time series is to connect the missing years with a line. **That fills the data in visually and invents an observation that never happened.** Draw the EU e-commerce penetration series as one smooth rising line and the reader walks away with "steady growth", when the truth is **the frame is changing**. So break it: leave the gap blank when it is large, and label the break with the reason ("2024 survey coverage adjusted", "no data this year"). Whitespace makes people ask one more question, and that question is often the one the number needs answered. The test: **if a real judgement sits between two points, do not connect them.**

## Whitespace is information, not a defect

**What a chart leaves out is often worth more attention than what it draws.** On the sector chart, the 19.82% C-E total sits next to industry figures from 38.01% to 90.45% and reads as "this is Europe"; on the geography chart, seven NAs get squeezed out. A chart turns "we did not draw this" into "there is nothing here", when the truth is: there is 22.6% of turnover here, sectors differ by more than double, countries by eight times. **What cannot be drawn is not "no data". It is "look again with a different frame".**

**What gets reported is not "the number" but "the number inside a frame".** How much sits outside, who was dropped, which way the number leans — put that on the chart, and other people can dare to use it.
